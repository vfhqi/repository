Section 2 – Ocean Cash-Generating Unit and Vessel Assets Impairment Risk

Section summary
  1. The remaining USD 1.1 billion impairment headroom is highly vulnerable to a severe container shipping downcycle.
  2. A massive vessel delivery wave in 2027-2028 will likely compress freight rates and trigger substantial asset write-downs.

This erosion reflects the immediate impact of weaker market conditions and rising capital commitments before any accounting adjustments. The shrinking buffer leaves the carrying value of vessel assets highly exposed to any further deterioration in global freight rates.

Section 3 – Logistics & Services Segment Goodwill and Reorganisation Impact

Section summary
  1. The reorganization of Logistics & Services into three divisions threatens to trigger massive goodwill write-downs as individual cash-generating units lose headroom.
  2. Persistent margin weakness in the Solutions division undermines the viability of the current USD 4.6 billion goodwill base.

Goodwill Accumulation and Acquisition History

1.
Aggressive expansion: Maersk deployed approximately USD 7.7 billion into logistics acquisitions between 2019 and 2023 to build its integrated capabilities, according to a Morgan Stanley broker note from September 9, 2025. This multi-year buying spree culminated in the acquisition of LF Logistics for USD 3.6 billion, which represents the largest single transaction in the segment's expansion history. This pro-cyclical investment strategy was intended to transform the company from a port-to-port carrier into a comprehensive global logistics integrator.
2.
Substantial carrying: Maersk reported a massive goodwill carrying value of USD 4,635 million at the end of 2025, according to its February 3, 2026 Annual Report. This asset base has grown steadily from USD 4,568 million reported at the end of 2024. The continuous accumulation of goodwill elevates the financial risk for shareholders because any future impairment charge would directly impact reported net income and erode the group's book value.
3.
Depressed capital: Morgan Stanley reported on September 9, 2025, that Maersk's Logistics & Services division has consistently generated returns below the group's 7.5% target. The division has failed to cover its cost of capital since its post-pandemic profitability peak in 2021. If operating returns continue to lag, the high goodwill balance will face increasing pressure during the next industry downturn.
4.
High asset: Bernstein Research observed in a report dated October 13, 2026, that goodwill represents approximately 75% of the invested capital for typical acquisitive logistics groups. This heavy intangible asset base is highly vulnerable to economic recessions when volume drops compress operating margins. Ebbing cash flows would force impairment tests to use lower near-term projections, putting the carrying value of these assets at immediate risk.

Structural Reorganisation and CGU Redefinition

5.
Structural realignment: Maersk announced in a press release on February 5, 2026, that it will re-group its Logistics & Services portfolio into Landside, Forwarding, and Solutions segments starting in April 2026. This administrative restructuring aims to align the segment with general industry standards and improve corporate transparency. However, reorganizing these activities also alters how the group monitors financial performance and cash flows across different product categories.
6.
Shifting cash-generating: Maersk's Annual Report published on February 3, 2026, stated that the entire Logistics & Services division, including first-mile activities, was historically tested for impairment as a single cash-generating unit. This integrated testing model allowed high-performing business units to mask weakness in underperforming acquisitions. Reallocating the goodwill across the newly formed Landside, Forwarding, and Solutions units will expose each individual segment to independent impairment testing.
7.
Immediate impairment: Morgan Stanley highlighted on August 13, 2026, that the newly separated Solutions division experienced a severe drop in EBIT margin to 1.7% in the second quarter of 2026, down from 3.8% in the prior year period. Because the Solutions segment holds a significant portion of the acquired contract logistics goodwill, this low margin makes the newly defined unit highly susceptible to a writedown. Any reallocation of goodwill to this lagging segment would likely trigger a technical impairment during the 2027-2028 downcycle.
8.
Rising discount: Maersk disclosed in its Annual Report on February 3, 2026, that it raised the pre-tax discount rate used for Logistics & Services impairment testing to 9.3% in 2025, up from 8.9% in 2024. This higher discount rate reduces the present value of projected future cash flows during impairment calculations. Unless the segment delivers significant margin expansion, this rising capital cost will continue to erode the segment's valuation headroom.

Profitability Pressures and Margin Targets

9.
Divergent segment: Bernstein Research reported on August 14, 2026, that Maersk's Logistics & Services division grew revenue by 15% to USD 4.22 billion in the second quarter of 2026, but profitability remained highly uneven. While Forwarding and Landside delivered solid EBIT margins of 6.4% and 6.3% respectively, Solutions underperformed due to ramp-up costs. This wide profitability gap indicates that the division's overall stability is heavily dependent on specific, high-margin segments.
10.
Challenging margin: Jefferies Research noted in an October 27, 2025 report that Maersk is targeting a long- term EBIT margin of 6% for the Logistics & Services segment. However, the division has struggled to meet this goal consistently, posting margins of 3.2% in 2023 and 3.6% in 2024. Ebbing margins in key segments suggest that the company's long-term targets remain highly optimistic and difficult to sustain.
11.
Internal integration: An expert call transcript with a former ground freight manager at Maersk on August 10, 2026, revealed that persistent technology and integration hurdles continue to limit logistics margin growth. The expert stated that combining multiple acquired IT systems and standardization across borders remains highly complex. These operational difficulties compress profitability and make the realization of expected acquisition synergies far more difficult.
12.
Contrary evidence: Easing supply chain disruptions and robust consumer demand in the United States supported a 15% revenue increase in Logistics & Services to USD 4.22 billion in the second quarter of 2026, as reported by Bernstein Research on August 14, 2026. This resilient revenue growth and sequential margin improvements could help the segment defend its carrying values if macroeconomic conditions remain favorable. If consumer spending defies the broader global slowdown through 2028, Maersk may generate sufficient cash flows to avoid any technical goodwill write-downs.

This highly optimized, asset-light business model allows DSV to consistently generate superior returns on invested capital compared to Maersk's capital-heavy approach. Section 4 – Accounting Estimates: Useful-Life Extension and Impairment Test

Section summary
  1. Maersk's decision to extend vessel useful lives from 20 to 25 years artificially reduces depreciation by USD 700 million in 2026, inflating near-term carrying values and delaying critical impairment triggers.
  2. Maersk's impairment testing relies on a pre-tax WACC of 9.8% and terminal growth rate of 2.1%, assumptions that appear aggressive when compared to direct peer parameters.

Assumptions

Useful-Life Extension and Carrying Value Impact

1.
Administrative depreciation relief: Effective January 1, 2026, Maersk increased the estimated useful lives of its vessels from 20 to 25 years following its annual useful-life review. This accounting change is applied prospectively as a change in accounting estimate and resulted in a reduction in depreciation expense of USD 175 million for Q2 2026 and USD 351 million for the first half of 2026. Consequently, this adjustment directly inflates operating income by lowering the Ocean segment's non-cash operating expenses.
2.
Artificially preserved headroom: The useful-life extension has a substantial full-year impact, reducing Maersk's annual depreciation charges by approximately USD 700 million for the year 2026. This reduction in non-cash opex artificially cushions the company's reported EBIT margin during periods of severe freight rate erosion. By keeping depreciation charges artificially low, the accounting policy shift prevents the rapid decline of Ocean operating cash flows.
3.
Elevated asset carrying values: At the end of 2025, Maersk reported a massive vessel carrying value of USD 20,451 million, up from USD 19,607 million at the end of 2024. This growing carrying value becomes increasingly difficult to support as industry-wide returns on invested capital face cyclical downward pressure. Lowering annual depreciation to USD 700 million below historic run-rates delays the natural reduction of these balance sheet values, setting up a larger necessary write-down when impairments are finally triggered.

Impairment Testing Assumptions and Peer Comparison

4.
Capital cost parameters: For its annual impairment testing, Maersk applied a pre-tax discount rate (WACC) of 9.8% for the Ocean segment in 2025, down slightly from 9.9% in 2024. This pre-tax rate is lower than the discount rates utilized by several key competitors, which may lead to overvalued future cash flows. For instance, COSCO Shipping Holdings applied a higher pre-tax discount rate of 10.20% for its container shipping operations in both 2024 and 2025.
5.
Peer risk premiums: ZIM Integrated Shipping Services applied an even higher post-tax discount rate of 12.5% for its container shipping services impairment testing in 2024 and 2025. Comparing Maersk's 9.8% pre-tax rate to ZIM's 12.5% post-tax rate reveals that Maersk is discounting its shipping cash flows at a significantly more generous rate than its peers. If Maersk adjusted its Ocean WACC upward to match peer risk profiles, the present value of its cash flows would contract, eliminating its remaining impairment headroom.
6.
Optimistic terminal growth: Maersk utilized a terminal growth rate of 2.1% per annum for its Ocean segment in 2025, unchanged from 2024. This terminal growth rate sits above the 2.0% terminal growth rate used by COSCO Shipping Holdings for its container shipping business. A higher terminal growth rate assumption directly elevates the calculated value-in-use, which serves as a major defense against technical write-downs.
7.
Sensitivity to WACC and growth: Maersk's Ocean value-in-use calculations are highly sensitive to both the pre-tax discount rate and terminal growth rate assumptions. A 0.2 percentage point increase in Maersk's Ocean pretax WACC, combined with a 0.2 percentage point decrease in the terminal growth rate, would reduce the Ocean segment's value-in-use and erode its remaining USD 1.1 billion buffer. A broader 0.5 percentage point increase in the discount rate alone would impact the segment's valuation by approximately USD 2.7 billion. Contrary Evidence and Peer Accounting Policy

8.
Contrary evidence: The vessel useful-life extension is technically justified because modern container ships are structurally designed to operate far beyond 20 years if properly maintained. An expert insight from a Head of Feeder Procurement at Maersk indicated that while the minimum life of a vessel is typically 25 years, many operators run their fleets for 35 to 40 years with proper maintenance. Therefore, Maersk's accounting change to a 25-year useful life aligns its financial statements with real-world physical asset operations rather than being a purely manipulative accounting maneuver. Standardized peer policies: Other major carriers have already standardized on a 25-year useful life for container shipping assets, making Maersk's shift a catch-up to industry norms. Maersk's accounting policies historically allowed for a vessel useful-life range of 5 to 25 years depending on the vessel class. By formalizing the 25- year average useful life starting in 2026, Maersk aligns its reporting with competitors like Hapag-Lloyd and COSCO, who utilize similar depreciation horizons.

Alternative Readings of Estimates

9.
Other side of the story: While the useful-life extension lowers depreciation, independent auditors can challenge Maersk's value-in-use assumptions if freight rates remain depressed. If long-term freight rates fall below the levels assumed in Maersk's budgets, auditors may force the company to lower its projected operating cash flows during annual impairment tests. Consequently, the administrative opex savings from the depreciation change will not prevent a massive asset impairment if macroeconomic demand collapses and vessel oversupply remains unaddressed.
