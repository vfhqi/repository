Section 2 – Refinancing dynamics and debt capital markets

Section summary
  1. Compacting credit spreads and selective bond reopenings are providing viable refinancing routes, though persistent leverage limits absolute earnings performance.
  2. Bilateral bank lending and documentation adjustments act as vital credit rating defences, insulating prime developers from capital market shocks.

Public bond markets and spread compression

1.
Compressing debt costs: Castellum successfully priced a three-year three hundred million euro unsecured bond in August 2026 at a fixed annual coupon of 3.75 percent, signaling a significant return to capital markets for Nordic issuers.
2.
Strategic liability optimization: Castellum executed the early redemption of eight hundred sixty-four million euros of outstanding bonds to align its unsecured bond documentation with its "Back to basics" operational strategy.
3.
Substantial spread reduction: Regional Swedish landlord Dios successfully issued six hundred million kronor in green bonds at a margin of 1.33 percent, representing the lowest pricing margin achieved yet under its active medium-term note program.
4.
Capital structure relief: Central European landlord Aroundtown issued seven hundred fifty million euros in perpetual notes in January 2026, which effectively refinanced expensive near-term obligations.
5.
Non-consensus credit caution: Morgan Stanley analysts maintain a highly selective approach to continental issuers, noting that elevated average debt leverage remains a structural headwind for Aroundtown despite recent liability management success.
6.
Margin compression benefits: Regional specialist NP3 refinanced its debt by issuing a five hundred million kronor green bond at a two hundred basis point margin, which replaced legacy high-coupon debt previously priced at five hundred twenty-five basis points over Stibor.

Alternative pathways and credit defences

7.
Structured bank support: Nordic bilateral relationship banks and Danish mortgage networks provided stable alternative credit to Wihlborgs, allowing the firm to maintain a simple capital structure without resorting to complex hybrid instruments.
8.
Rating defence consolidation: Castellum terminated its credit rating from S&P Global, choosing to rely solely on Moody's Baa2 rating to simplify its debt administration and reduce ratings overhead.
9.
Non-consensus liquidity buffer: Fastighets Balder maintained twenty-three billion kronor in robust available liquidity which DNB Carnegie analysts calculate fully covers its debt maturities through the middle of 2026 despite its Moody's fallen-angel Ba1 downgrade.
10.
Strategic asset recycling: CPI Property Group utilized asset disposals to offset rising interest expenses, though S&P Global maintains a negative outlook on its credit rating due to tight interest coverage limits.

12.
Other side of the story: Persistent high interest rates and wider swap spreads could severely pressure future earnings as expiring interest rate hedges roll off across the broader European real estate sector over the next twenty-four months.

Section 3 – Asset yield valuation and rental indexation mechanics

Section summary
  1. Compounding rent indexation and stabilizing property yields are solidifying book values, with recent transaction premiums proving that valuation declines have troughed.
  2. Contractual consumer price index clauses insulate commercial landlords from inflation, while transaction proof points like Castellum's disposals confirm valuation book support.

Contractual indexation and inflation pass-through

1.
Robust contractual protection: Diös reports that ninety-seven percent of its commercial leases contain indexation clauses, with ninety-four percent linked directly to CPI, which secures highly predictable income growth.
2.
Geographic indexation differences: Wihlborgs management explains that Swedish indexation occurs annually using October CPI, whereas Danish leases are adjusted throughout the year based on individual lease signing anniversaries.
3.
Delayed rent adjustment: Bernstein analysts highlight that Swedish commercial leases typically run for three to five years without mid-term reviews, meaning indexation provides intermediate cash flow support before rents renegotiate to market levels.
4.
Robust inflation pass-through: Wihlborgs annualized contractual rental income reached over four billion kronor on January 1, 2026, supported by customary inflation indexation that insulates the top-line from macro shocks.
5.
Uncapped indexation protection: German logistics specialist MLP Group structures its lease agreements with uncapped Harmonized Index of Consumer Prices indexation, ensuring full inflation pass-through and flooring indexation at zero if deflation occurs.
6.
Valuation cash flow support: Atrium Ljungberg reports positive property value changes of 0.2 percent in its third-quarter 2026 internal valuation, which was primarily driven by positive cash flows from successful rent indexation pass-through.

Yield trajectories and book value stabilization

7.
Improved transaction proof: DNB Carnegie analysts note that transaction markets are bottoming out and rolling twelve-month asset writedowns have bottomed, indicating stabilizing asset values that narrow wide net asset value discounts.

Section 7 – Data gaps

Section summary
  1. Direct direct-lending opacity and delayed appraisal marks represent the most critical data gaps, requiring alternative monitoring tools to map European property trajectories.

Capital market and reporting blindspots

1.
Unseen private debt: Direct direct-lending agreements in Europe remain deeply opaque, meaning that analysts must monitor specialized platforms like 9fin to extract anonymized transaction pricing.
2.
Lagging appraisal marks: European property appraisers are historically backward-looking, forcing analysts to conduct detailed field-visit checks on actual transaction yields.
3.
Stale bank collateral: The European Central Bank recently identified major shortcomings in CRE collateral valuations by regional banks, which relied on stale transaction evidence from preceding years.
4.
Stale collateral tracking: Addressing stale regional bank CRE valuations requires reviewing upcoming regulatory updates, since CRR III implementation in 2025 enforces stricter historical valuation averages.
5.
Undisclosed corporate impairments: Savings banks and small regional lenders often omit localized CRE impairment ratio details from financial statements, which requires direct expert consultation to fully resolve.

Private transactional and tenant metrics

6.
Unmapped green metrics: Harmonized European energy performance certificate data is not yet publicly available, making it necessary to track individual green lease filings to assess climate risks.
7.
Hidden contractor distress: Rising bankruptcies in Swedish and Norwegian construction sectors are increasing credit risk, necessitating that analysts monitor local corporate registration filings for early signs of distress.
8.
Opaque direct pricing: Transaction volumes in key European commercial real estate markets remain highly subdued, meaning analysts must wait for publicized portfolio disposals to establish true clearing yields.
9.
Opaque deal friction: Fee transparency in European real estate transactions is significantly lower than in the United Kingdom, meaning analysts must conduct local broker calls to estimate actual frictional costs.
10.
Other side of the story: If these data gaps are gaps in name only, the analyst's focus on structured data may be a way of flinching from visible structural declines in office occupancy that require no further validation.
