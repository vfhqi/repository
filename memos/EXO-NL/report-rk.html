Credibility of the Bear Case

The bear case is not backed by any public short campaign. Searches of available databases and broker research surfaced no published short-seller reports or activist campaigns targeting EXOR itself, and the holding company remains free of direct public short campaigns. Quantitative short-interest data for EXOR is unavailable, and the absence of large disclosed short positions suggests institutional short sellers have not crossed the Dutch regulatory thresholds.

The bear case also has no standalone sell-side validation. Searches did not surface direct coverage, target prices, or rating distributions for EXOR as a standalone entity, and analysts instead focused on underlying assets such as Stellantis and Ferrari. The bearish arguments are therefore largely inferred from portfoliocompany data. The strongest, filing-based bear evidence concerns governance and control.

Filings show Giovanni Agnelli B.V. holds 54.94% of the economic rights but 83.97% of the voting power. Related-party transactions include a €570 million participation in the €1 billion tender offer, which the controlling shareholder used to reduce its own net debt. The counterpoint is that material related-party transactions must be approved solely by nonexecutive directors with no Agnelli family associations. Valuation and NAV data lend the thesis substance.

The discount to NAV widened to a historical high of 57.6% at the end of June 2026, well above the five-year average of 35%. NAV fell 3.9% (about €1.2 billion) in the first half of 2026, and shareholder return lagged asset performance because of the wider discount. The report's overall verdict is medium. The likelihood of the bear case materializing over 18 months is medium because heavy luxury-asset concentration in Ferrari balances severe cyclical industrial downturns. Buybacks at deep discounts are highly accretive but cannot fully offset persistent discount widening.
