[Materiality of] Investment Thesis (BB 1) Rating = D

Thesis. BT is a cash-flow self-help case, not an earnings-growth case: the fibre build has passed its peak, capital expenditure falls by more than £1bn in FY27 and a cost programme that has taken out £1.5bn takes out £2.2bn more, so the company's normalised free cash flow is targeted to double from £1.5bn in FY26 to about £3.0bn by FY30 while consensus adjusted earnings per share stay at about 18p to 19p. At 196.55p the shares already pay 10.25x 24-month-forward earnings, the 80th percentile of their own five-year range, for that cash plan, and the modal return is about 7% a year against the framework's 20% bar. It is a case to park and to watch, with a price, about 165p, at which it becomes compelling on its own arithmetic.

  • The change is internal, broad and real, and the external forces subtract from it rather than add to it.
    • A chief executive appointed in February 2024 has replaced nearly the whole executive committee, cut the perimeter to the United Kingdom through five disposals and a $4bn joint venture with Verizon, raised the cost target from £3.0bn to £3.7bn and passed the capital-expenditure peak on schedule
    • that passes the screen's rule of three strong forces with room to spare, which is why 'Change Forces' are C and not lower.
    • The external side is four headwinds out of seven forces: competition tightening at wholesale (nexfibre's £2bn purchase of Netomnia; Virgin Media O2's new wholesale unit; Sky selling over CityFibre at £35 and £45 a month) and in mobile (VodafoneThree complete), a regulator that on 28 July 2026 blocked an Openreach pricing offer for the first time, a retail broadband market flat at 28.6m lines for four years, and a UK gilt yield near 5% acting on £19,966m of net debt and a £4.2bn pension deficit.
    • Nothing on the external side meets the 'Demand Inflection' test
      • the demand that exists is a migration from copper to fibre inside a flat market, which Openreach partly wins (574k FTTP net additions in Q1 FY27) and partly loses (825k lines lost in FY26, about 800k guided for FY27).
  • The change has travelled four of the six layers of the 'Six Layer Inputs-outputs' chain in cash and stops before the earnings line.
    • It shows in the company's people, its choices and its actions
    • in its key operating metrics (23.4m premises passed, 40% take-up, total labour resource down to 108,000, quarterly build down from 1.5m premises to 514k)
    • and in its financial statements in margin (operating EBITDA margin 41.7% in FY26 from 33.6% in FY20), capital expenditure (£5,127m at the FY26 peak, £4.2bn to £4.3bn guided for FY27) and leverage.
    • It has not reached the sell-side's earnings estimates: consensus adjusted earnings per share are 18.3p for FY26, 18.2p for FY27, 19.2p for FY28, 19.2p for FY29 and 19.4p for FY30 (the last on two brokers), a compound rate of about 1.5% a year.
    • The reason is structural rather than a delay: depreciation of about £4.8bn a year follows capital expenditure down only slowly because fibre assets are long-lived, net interest of about £1.0bn is flat as cheaper debt is refinanced at higher coupons, and EBITDA is flat (-0.8% in Q1 FY27) because the savings are spent on wage inflation, on the £150m to £200m a year of legacy voice loss and on a Business division whose EBITDA fell 6.5% in the quarter.
  • 'Financial Outputs' are improving below the revenue line and above the earnings line, which is exactly where the framework's metric does not look.
    • Margin and cash conversion are B
    • revenue growth is D
    • earnings-per-share growth is F at every horizon.
    • Normalised free cash flow was £1,508m in FY26 (18% of EBITDA), is guided to about £2.0bn in FY27 (24%) and about £3.0bn by FY30 (36%)
      • consensus has 2,009, 2,308, 2,531 and 2,645 GBPm for FY27 to FY30, so consensus sits £355m below the company's FY30 target and on top of its FY27 one.
    • The sell-side has been raising its price targets (+2%, +4%, +3% over one, three and six months) and trimming its earnings estimates (0, -1, -3 on the dashboard's scale) at the same time.
      • That is the exact shape of a stock being valued on cash while the framework values it on earnings.
  • What we think it earns.
    • Our modal adjusted earnings per share run 18.3p (FY26) to 18.0p (FY27), 19.0p (FY28), 19.8p (FY29) and 20.6p (FY30), about 3% a year, most of it from depreciation declining and almost none from revenue or margin
    • on free cash flow per share, our cross-check and not the metric, the company's targets imply 15.3p in FY26 rising to about 30p in FY30, about 19% a year and inside the framework's 12% to 20% band, and consensus free cash flow gives about 27p, about 15% a year.
    • The gap between those two readings is the whole case.
  • What it costs. 196.55p is 10.25x the 24-month-forward consensus earnings of about 19.2p: the 80th percentile of the stock's own five-year range (median 8.09x; 25th to 75th percentile 6.86x to 10.01x), the 88th percentile of its ten-year history on the dashboard's reading, above the ten-year 'defendable' band of 9.90x and just under the 'mania' band of 10.90x.
    • The multiple rose from a five-year low of 5.66x while earnings per share fell (23.2p in FY21 to 17.5p in FY26 on the Viewforth definition), so the re-rating has already happened, on cash expectations that are now in every broker's model
      • the last two years have averaged 10.22x with no earnings growth.
    • Against the comparison set the stock is not expensive: 10.7x current P/E against a sector median of about 11.9x and about 12.9x for the fourteen integrated incumbents.
      • That, and a normalised free-cash-flow yield above 10%, is the one argument for the multiple holding rather than reverting.
  • The return does not clear the bar on the framework's own convention, and the dividend carries almost all of it.
    • We apply 10.0x, the modal branch (the two-year median of 10.22x, the five-year 75th percentile of 10.01x and the ten-year 'defendable' band of 9.90x), to earnings two years beyond each horizon.
    • Eighteen months (March 2028): 10.0x on our modal FY30 earnings of 20.6p is 206.0p, plus 11.2p of dividends, 217.2p, a total return of +10.5%, about 6.9% a year.
      • Three years (September 2029): 10.0x on about 21.5p (our FY31 and FY32 averaged) is 215.0p, plus 26.7p of dividends, 241.7p, +23.0%, about 7.1% a year.
      • Both are F against the framework's anchors (D is about 10% to 12%).
    • On the one-year-forward footing beside it (the dashboard's 10.7x on about 18.1p), holding 10.7x on our FY29 earnings of 19.8p gives about 212p plus 11.2p at March 2028, about +13.5%, about 8.8% a year
      • a little more, because the nearer footing catches one more year of the depreciation tailwind, and still below 10% a year.
    • The drop-through sensitivity: net interest £100m higher than our path and a 25% tax rate rather than 22%, both of which the last two years have shown, take FY30 earnings per share from 20.6p to about 19.3p, and the return to about 2.6% a year over eighteen months and about 5.2% a year over three.
      • The rating is F either way
      • what the sensitivity shows is that the return excluding the dividend is close to zero when the below-EBITDA lines move against the case by amounts they have recently moved by.
      • The case has no earnings cushion.
    • The whole difference between park and progress is the multiple, not the earnings: at the conceded anchor of 12.0x, which is what a market pricing the company on a constant normalised free-cash-flow yield would carry, the eighteen-month return is about 20.0% a year (B, at the bar) and the three-year return about 13.2% a year (C).
      • We do not use it as the modal case because normalised free cash flow is not shareholder cash, because the stock has traded above 12x on this measure only briefly in five years, and because the market has had two years of the same cash story at 10x.
  • The pattern it fits is 'Major Corporate Change', in half.
    • The input half fits crisply
    • the output half, major earnings-per-share trajectory upgrades, does not, because this change produces a free-cash-flow trajectory upgrade with earnings per share left where they were.
    • The fit is input plus early output, a C.
  • Where the chain holds and where it breaks.
    • It holds from resources through choices and actions into the operating metrics and the cash line, and the one thing the bears cannot argue away is that capital expenditure is a hard number the build has physically already reduced.
    • It breaks in two places: between EBITDA and earnings per share, where £4.8bn of depreciation, £1.0bn of interest, a tax rate drifting toward 25% and a flat share count absorb the cash before it reaches the metric
    • and at the multiple, which has already priced the plan and has nothing left in it to discover.

  • What the case therefore is.

    • A park at 196.55p, with the price at which the modal path clears 20% a year stated: about 165p over eighteen months at the modal multiple (153p at the arithmetic blend of 9.24x) and about 140p over three years
    • the 52-week low was 172.93p.
    • What we are betting on if we hold it, in one line: that a market which already pays 10x for flat earnings pays it again in eighteen months for the same flat earnings plus a 4% dividend because the cash arrived.
    • Nothing more, and that is not enough.

  • Rating = D, because the change forces are material (internal forces B) but do not travel into the metric: the 'Financial Outputs' on earnings per share are not improving (D, with 'Goldilocks growth' at F), the 'Modal return' is about 7% a year (F), and the parts combine so that the strong input is bound by the weak output rather than carried by it.

    • Not C: two of the three Elements sit at D or F and the return is F.
    • Not F: the internal forces would pass the screen on their own and the cash outputs are measured, dated and on plan.

Most plausible competing hypothesis, and our response.

  • The case against, at its strongest, is that the cash inflection is optical: the risks report's 'false friend'.
    • As capital expenditure falls, the £600m-plus of own-work capitalised labour returns to operating expense (Redburn sizes a £1bn capital-expenditure cut at about £600m of decapitalisation), compressing EBITDA while the cash line looks better
    • 30% of FY26 normalised free cash flow came from working-capital programmes that reverse, and factoring and supply-chain finance rose from £68m to £476m in a year
    • cash tax steps up from FY28 as full-expensing losses are used
    • and the £780m a year of pension deficit payments never enter the measure the company reports.
    • Meanwhile a scaled second wholesaler forms (nexfibre-Netomnia, plus Virgin Media O2's upgrade of its own footprint), TalkTalk's roughly £1bn a year of Openreach payments are in play in a sale process, Sky is already multi-sourcing, and since 28 July 2026 the regulator has removed Openreach's nationwide discount.
    • The result on the bears' arithmetic is Openreach share drifting toward 50% over a decade, EBITDA margins compressing 150 basis points, reported free cash flow falling 15%, leverage rising to 3.5x, and an equity value of 100p at 5x FY27 earnings (Deutsche Bank's 150p target and Morgan Stanley's 100p bear case; options in mid-2026 gave a 4.4% probability of the shares below 100p).
    • The evidence for it is real: specific items of £867m in FY26
      • net debt up £150m in a year with the dividend paid
      • the sector precedents of Telia and Orange
      • and BT's own 2016 to 2024 history, in which the dividend was suspended and then rebased.
  • What we concede.
    • The mechanism, in full: normalised free cash flow is not shareholder cash, and we use the shareholder's cash (after pension deficit payments and restructuring) wherever cover is tested in this record
    • the own-work-capitalised reversal is real and is the one thing that could turn the margin line down while the cash line rises
    • the cash-tax step-up is real from FY28
    • the wholesale base is eroding at about 4% a year and the regulator has just made it harder to defend.
    • We also concede the accounting point about the margin: a 41.7% EBITDA margin that has risen every year while revenue fell is partly a capitalisation effect.
  • Why we still hold our view, which is 'park' and not 'bearish'.
    • First, capital expenditure is a hard line: £5,127m in FY26 to a guided £4.2bn to £4.3bn in FY27, with consensus at 4,477 GBPm, and the build has physically slowed to a third of its peak quarterly pace
    • the decapitalisation the bears fear cannot exceed the labour that was capitalised, which the risks report itself puts at about £600m against a capital-expenditure reduction of about £1.6bn by FY30, so the net cash improvement is positive even in the bears' own arithmetic.
    • Second, the wholesale-erosion case was written before the Q1 FY27 update, and the quarter came in better on lines (192k lost against 198k expected), take-up (40.0%) and ARPU (+7%), with alt-net construction down at least 40% year on year
    • the bears' own trigger for abandoning their case, 'a rapid exit of capital from the alt-net sector', is half met.
    • Third, the bear equity value of 100p rests on 5x earnings, a multiple the stock has not seen in five years (the low was 5.66x) and which a free-cash-flow yield above 10% and a 4% dividend argue against.
    • What we do not hold is the bull's return either: the bears are right that the cash is not what it looks like, and the bulls need a multiple the stock has not sustained.
  • The bull's 'Openreach alone covers the enterprise value' claim, tested like for like.
    • Openreach's consensus one-year-forward EBITDA is 4,304 GBPm
    • the enterprise value is 39,310 GBPm (market capitalisation 19,026 plus debt 19,275 less cash 2,123 plus pensions 3,136 less associates 4).
    • At the 6.4x median enterprise-value-to-EBITDA multiple the risks report attributes to peer operators, Openreach is worth about £27.5bn, 70% of the enterprise value: not covered.
    • At the 10.4x enterprise-value-to-EBITDAaL multiple Redburn gives BT itself on FY28 (an after-leases basis, so not exactly like for like), it is worth about £44.8bn, 114%: covered.
    • At the 28x the chief executive achieved for tower stakes at Telia the claim is trivially true and irrelevant, because Openreach is a regulated access network and not a tower company.
    • The cover exists only at a multiple above the telecom peers' and is therefore an argument about what Openreach ought to be worth, not a bound on the downside
    • we do not rely on it.

Internal forces: are there sufficiently strong internal change forces under way? (RA 2, E 1, BB 1) Rating = B
  • Rating = B, because at least five of the twelve internal forces are strong (chief executive change; other leaders; strategy change; perimeter reduction; standards improvement) and the screen's decision rule of at least three strong forces is met, with the two largest forces of all, the capital-expenditure step-down and the cost transformation, sitting outside the screen's twelve.

    • no crown jewel has been sold

  • Rating = B, because the two largest internal forces are not in the screen's twelve and belong here: the capital-expenditure step-down and the cost transformation, both of which act on cash first and on earnings per share only through the slow decline of depreciation.

[Robustness of] Foundations upon which the Investment Thesis is built upon (BB 2) Rating = C

The 'Foundations' are robust enough to carry a cash thesis and not robust enough to carry an earnings thesis: a national passive-infrastructure asset with the lowest build cost in Europe, run by an operator with the record to deliver exactly this programme, sits inside a capital structure and a wholesale market that absorb the cash before it reaches earnings per share, and the market is not yet confirming anything.

  • The value chain supports the thesis at its core and undermines it at the edges.
    • Openreach's duct and pole network is an asset alt-nets cannot replicate at scale (about £300 per premise passed against £500 to £1,800), the regulator has confirmed a five-year 'fair bet' framework to 2031 with no cost-based controls in 86% of premises, and the challengers are running out of capital (construction down at least 40% year on year; CityFibre cutting 18% of staff and raising £900m; G.Network and Trooli in administration).
    • The edges are live and dated: a scaled second wholesaler is forming (nexfibre-Netomnia, CMA decision by December 2026; Virgin Media O2's wholesale unit), the large retail ISPs are diversifying away from Openreach (Sky over CityFibre at £35 and £45; TalkTalk's roughly 2.7m lines in a sale process), and on 28 July 2026 Ofcom for the first time blocked an Openreach pricing offer, so the challengers may discount and the incumbent's nationwide discount is blocked.
      • That asymmetry is new.
    • Industry structure is concentrated where BT is a retailer (31% of broadband; 26% of mobile service revenue; the UK mobile market just went from four to three) and fragmenting by design where BT is a wholesaler, and the concentration BT enjoys at wholesale is the one the regulator exists to reduce.
  • The business has one large advantage and weak financial outputs, and the two facts are the same fact seen twice.
    • The advantage is the network
    • the outputs are what a regulated network earns: adjusted return on capital employed 8.9%, adjusted EBIT margin 17.0%, gross margin 19.3% on the standardised line, earnings per share falling for five years, and cash conversion on reported measures near zero once £780m of pension deficit payments and £311m of restructuring cash come off the company's normalised £1,508m.
    • On the framework's own financial-output tests (return on capital 15%-plus; EBIT margin 20%-plus; 80% cash conversion; 12% to 20% growth) the company fails every one, and earnings quality is poor: adjusted earnings per share of 18.3p against reported 11.0p, £867m of specific items, own-work capitalised up more than £600m since the build accelerated, 30% of normalised free cash flow from working-capital programmes.
    • The scope for improvement is large, named and congruent with the strategy (£2.2bn of the £3.7bn savings still to come; capital expenditure down £1.5bn to £1.6bn from peak; restructuring cash from £400m to £100m; the voice drag ending), and the peer precedent that shows what is possible (KPN's 15% return on invested capital and 47.8% EBITDA margin) also shows how far BT is from it.
  • The operator is the strongest foundation, and it is a B for the commitments it chooses to make.
    • Three consecutive years of meeting cash and EBITDA ranges through the peak of a £5bn-a-year build, five disposals, a full executive refresh, a raised cost target with the cost to achieve stated honestly, and the fastest fibre build in European telecoms (4.8m premises passed in a year; 2.2m FTTP net additions in FY26 against 1.2m to 1.5m for Deutsche Telekom, Orange and Telefonica).
    • The record on the commitments it has not chosen is one of misses: the revenue outlook cut in November 2024, 0% payout on three transformation metrics in FY26, Business EBITDA -5% in FY26.
      • The insider signal is thin (the departing finance director's discretionary sale of 400,105 shares in June 2026; directors' net buying of £12k against a £150k European median), and the executive committee has turned over almost entirely within eighteen months during the PSTN migration.
  • The market is not confirming the thesis, and for reasons the record can explain.
    • Share-price momentum is a pinned C (price below all three moving averages; relative strength percentile 42; +1.7% over twelve months), sell-side earnings momentum is a pinned D (earnings per share trimmed 0, -1 and -3 while targets rose +2, +4 and +3), and the sector's cash-return theme is strong in the market (Telia +35.5%, Vodafone +46.9%, NOS +53.4%, Telecom Italia +64.1% over twelve months) and absent in this stock.
    • The explanation is the one 'Investment Thesis' gave: the sell-side values the cash and the framework, like the earnings-momentum pin, reads the earnings, and the earnings are flat.
      • The market is watching rather than leaving: the 200-day average has risen in ten of the last twelve months and targets are rising.

Summary: Written Summary of the Investment Case (not rated) (E 3, BB 4)
The case in 8 to 10 sentences (the thesis as tested), kept in full at both ends of the record (Part 1, E 3, BB 4)
  • This is a self-help cash case in a regulated, ex-growth, leveraged incumbent.
  • BT has passed the peak of a £5.1bn-a-year fibre build, and as capital expenditure falls by more than £1bn to £4.2bn to £4.3bn in FY27 and toward £3.5bn by FY30, and as a cost programme that has already taken out £1.5bn takes out £2.2bn more, the company's normalised free cash flow is targeted to rise from £1.5bn in FY26 to about £2.0bn in FY27 and about £3.0bn by FY30, which would first cover the dividend and the £780m a year of pension deficit payments with room to spare, then take leverage to BBB+ metrics, then fund buybacks.
  • The change is real and has travelled far: a chief executive appointed in February 2024 has replaced nearly the whole executive committee, cut the perimeter to the United Kingdom through five disposals and a $4bn joint venture, raised the cost target and passed the build peak on schedule
    • the key operating metrics show it (23.4m premises passed, 40% take-up, 108,000 total labour resource, 514k premises built in the last quarter against 1.5m in the one before), and the financial statements show it in margin, capital expenditure and leverage.
