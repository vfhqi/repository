The external forces are earnings-neutral to slightly positive over 18 months and skew negative beyond it. Demand is structurally stable and upgrading; regulation and Greek macro are tailwinds; inflation is a rare earnings positive because OTE's price rise is contractual. Against these, the one strong new force, the Vodafone-PPC wholesale venture, attacks the layer that carries OTE's excess return, with damage that is slow (three to five years) and bounded (tens of millions of euros of EBITDA, not hundreds). The RRF roll-off and the energy hedge roll-off make 2027 the year where external forces are least helpful. C-01

To what extent is end-market demand changing, structurally or cyclically, and with how much visibility?

C58

Question asked To what extent is end-market demand changing, structurally or cyclically, and with how much visibility? (A cyclical swing counts when it has two-plus years of visibility.)

Judgement: consumer connectivity demand is structurally flat in units and steadily upgrading in value, with high visibility; the one sharp swing is cyclical and policy-made, the public-sector ICT surge funded by the RRF, which peaked in 2026 and has visibility only to the end of 2026. Nothing in the last six months changed the structural picture; what changed is that the price-driven bump of 2025 lapped and the ICT surge reached its peak.

  • Units flat, mix upgrading (Q2 2026 against a year earlier): retail broadband 2,386,158 (-0.1%); fixed lines -1.1%; FTTH subscribers 687k (+62k in the quarter, 29% of the broadband base against 20%); postpaid 3,147,844 (+8.0%, 62k net additions, a 17-year high); prepaid 3,952,955 (-6.1%), still 56% of lines; data usage +20% to 21%; pay-TV subscribers +7.9% to 793k. The migration is worth about 10% more revenue per broadband line (FTTH over copper) and 3 to 5 EUR a month per prepaid-to-postpaid move.
  • What changed in the last six months against the prior 12 to 18: mobile service revenue growth went from +5.2% (Q4 2025) and +5.5% (Q1 2026) to +2.3% (Q2 2026) as the 2025 rise in the minimum prepaid top-up lapped; retail fixed service revenue from +2.6% (Q4 2025) and +1.1% to +0.3%. The volume engines did not slow; the price component faded.
  • Macro backdrop is supportive but squeezing the consumer: Greek real GDP 2.0% in 2026 and 1.9% in 2027 against a euro area below 1%; but HICP inflation 3.8% for January to September 2026 and 5.1% in September (energy +25.4%), real disposable income falling (-3.3% in Q1 2025), utility bills up from about 5% to nearly 15% of household income. Connectivity is non-discretionary; the exposure is downgrading at the low end (prepaid, pay-TV), not volume loss.
  • ICT is the cyclical leg: System Solutions grew about 64% in H1 2026 on public projects rushing to spend RRF money before the programme ends in December 2026. Management calls ICT "front-loaded in 2026". Offsets that soften the cliff: Morgan Stanley estimates only 56% of RRF money will be absorbed by end-2026, leaving a spill-over pipeline; a 23 bn EUR National Development Programme and a 49 bn EUR 2028-2034 EU budget allocation. OTE publishes no backlog, against Quest's 671 EURm.
  • Modelling implication: build consumer service revenue from volumes (FTTH, postpaid, TV) plus price; model System Solutions separately with an explicit 2027 decline scenario rather than extrapolating 2026.
C-02

To what extent, and in which direction, is competitive intensity changing?

❌ D40

Judgement: intensity is stable at retail and rising at wholesale, which is the layer that carries OTE's excess return; the direction is adverse but the speed is slow. Twelve to eighteen months ago the fear was PPC's retail price attack; that has been disproved. In the last six months the threat moved to wholesale and was formalised.

  • Retail, stable to benign: the incumbents (Nova, Vodafone Greece) raised prices with inflation for a second year in early 2026; Vodafone raised fixed-wireless, data-sharing and entry pay-TV prices. PPC's broadband-only offers at 17.90 EUR (500 Mbps) and 19.90 EUR (1 Gbps), 52% to 59% below the incumbents, reached under 11k subscribers at end-2025 and about 20k by March 2026, under 1% of OTE's 2.4m lines. Nova offers 3 Gbps at 27 EUR; Vodafone is upgrading 290k copper customers to FTTH at flat bills, a retention move, not a price war.
  • Wholesale, tightening: Vodafone Greece and PPC signed heads of terms (June 2026) and a non-binding term sheet (August 2026) for a 50:50 open-access fibre joint venture, with about 130 EURm of cash to PPC, over 1.6m premises, completion targeted for H1 2027 and a 3.8m-premise target by 2028. It gives PPC's network the anchor tenant it lacked (Vodafone has 0.91m broadband customers). Nova is building its own FTTH to 1.6m premises. Today 48% of the rivals' FTTH subscribers ride on OTE's network (46% a year earlier), and national wholesale revenue was about 239 EURm in FY2025, already down about 15 EURm that year.
  • Magnitude and timing: the earnings research quotes up to 50 EURm of wholesale revenue at risk if Vodafone moved its whole base to the venture, over a three-to-five-year horizon set by volume-commitment contracts (up to 28% discounts, effective August 2024) and in-building cabling. At wholesale margins that is roughly 2% to 3.5% of group EBITDA spread over 2027 to 2030. Nova (1.07m broadband customers) is the swing factor.
  • Mobile: the triopoly is intact; PPC's chief executive has repeatedly ruled out mobile and spectrum bidding.
  • Peers: Digi's entry into Portugal was less disruptive than feared (NOS gained share), while in Spain it cut postpaid ARPU 9.8%; Greece has no mobile entrant, which is why its retail pricing held.
C-03

To what extent is a disruption force strengthening or being disproved, and in which direction does it act on the company?

❌ D42

Judgement: the retail disruption thesis (a utility undercutting OTE's broadband) has been disproved; the infrastructure disruption thesis (a cheaper second wholesale network) has been strengthened by the joint venture and acts against OTE from 2027. The other disruption forces are contained or work for OTE.

  • Aerial utility fibre: PPC's FiberGrid passed about 1.88m homes (1.1m to 1.3m ready for service) at about 160 EUR per home on its electricity poles, against OTE's duct build at about 500 EUR per line; OTE itself estimates PPC's last-mile cost up to 20% lower. Retail conversion was 0.6% to under 2%, against 15% to 25% for Spanish and Portuguese altnets within two years, because PPC has no mobile, no pay-TV and no telecom retail channel, and about 70% of OTE's broadband customers bundle. The joint venture fixes PPC's missing piece (a retail anchor), so the disruption migrates from retail price to wholesale volume. In the language of the Wisdom Library's "Unbundle, Capture, Rebundle": PPC unbundled the access layer; the venture is trying to capture the wholesale interface; the customer relationship stays with the retailers, so OTE's retail franchise is not the target, its wholesale host role is.
  • Satellite: Greece had the second-highest Starlink usage in the EU in Q1 2026, but at about 40 EUR a month plus 349 EUR of equipment and 100 Mbps; OTE's 5G fixed-wireless base grew 166% to 118,894 lines and is winning back the 150k to 160k lines the market lost. The sector view is that the satellite worry is clearing for European network operators.
  • OTT and piracy in pay-TV: being reversed by anti-piracy fines (750 EUR, 1,500 EUR for repeat offences), the end of the 10% pay-TV tax and the sports-sharing deal with Nova; pay-TV revenue +9.2% in Q2 2026.
  • AI: today a cost tool for OTE (indirect costs down to 28% of service revenue); the sector's longer-run warning is that premium-priced operators have more revenue to lose to AI switching agents than cost to save.
C-04

To what extent is political or regulatory change under way, and how does it bear on the company?

C64

Judgement: substantial change is under way and on balance it moves demand and cash toward OTE; the adverse items are a known FTTH wholesale tariff cut and the latent risk that a cost-of-living climate turns politics against inflation-linked price rises.

  • Favourable: FTTC stop-sell where FTTH exists (from June 2025), forcing migration; proposed copper wholesale charges +4% to 5%; FTTH margin-squeeze obligation lifted, giving retail pricing flexibility; European Commission approval of OTE's wholesale volume-discount scheme (up to 28%); 5% broadband tax on lines above 100 Mbps removed (a 6.2% consumer price cut with no ARPU hit) and the 10% pay-TV tax removed from 1 January 2026; anti-piracy fines; 900 MHz and 1800 MHz renewals (September and November 2027) estimated by Deutsche Bank at about 78 EURm against 119 EURm paid 15 years ago; the 26-year rural UFBB concession now wholly OTE's; the Romanian-exit tax benefit in FY2026 cash.
  • Adverse: regulated FTTH wholesale rates cut 10% to 13% (effective date pending European Commission Article 32 review; Deutsche Bank says it is inside the FY2026 free cash flow guidance); Significant Market Power designation retained; 180 EURm of fibre vouchers exhausted in February 2026; the RRF ends December 2026; EU Gigabit Infrastructure Act gives altnets symmetric access to utility poles.
  • Contract law: the EU Court of Justice ruling of 10 September 2026 on Vodafone Germany removed operators' right to raise prices at discretion mid-contract but appears to leave clauses written in at signing intact, which is the form OTE's December 2025 adjustment took.
  • Fragility: with inflation at 5.1% and the government spending 1.6 bn EUR on tax cuts and 500 EURm on living-cost support, pressure on telecom indexation is a plausible political response; EETT could revisit the volume-discount scheme if the joint venture alleges foreclosure; competition review of the joint venture could go either way (a second wholesale network is pro-competitive in a regulator's eyes).
C-05

How are input costs, input availability and supplier concentration changing, and is the net effect a headwind or a tailwind?

C50

Judgement: a mild tailwind in 2026 turning into a mild headwind in 2027, as energy hedge cover falls just as Greek energy and wage inflation rise; supplier concentration is high but buffered by Deutsche Telekom.

  • Energy: about 80% of FY2026 and about 66% of FY2027 consumption hedged, through virtual power purchase agreements running to 2034 and 2036; Morgan Stanley puts a 50% energy-cost spike at only 0.6% of FY2026 EBITDAaL. With Brent at about 111 USD and Greek energy inflation +25.4% in September 2026, the unhedged share roughly doubling from about 20% to about 34% makes FY2027 roughly twice as sensitive as FY2026. The 5 EURm FY2025 saving from lower spot prices will not repeat.
  • Labour: personnel costs were 10.6% of revenue in FY2025 and fell 4%; voluntary exits save 15 to 20 EURm a year. Against that, a large minimum-wage rise in April 2026 and services inflation of 6.4% in Greece. The net personnel line can keep falling only while exits continue, and the exits are themselves a recurring cash cost.
  • Equipment: radio access is a Nokia, Ericsson and Samsung triopoly; memory prices are up about 7.5x since early 2025 and Nokia's optical lead times exceed 52 weeks. OTE buys through Deutsche Telekom's framework and an intra-group equipment-sharing platform, so the risk is rollout timing (2.4m homes passed by end-2026), not unit cost.
  • Content and spectrum: sports sharing with Nova cut content cost; UEFA rights run to 2027 (renewal is a 2027 cost event); spectrum renewal is cheaper than last time.
  • Financing: refinancing the 500 EURm 0.875% bond (September 2026) at about 3% adds roughly 10 EURm a year of interest, about 1% of pre-tax profit.

  • Quarterly reported revenue growth: +5.0% (Q3 2025), +8.7% (Q4 2025), +4.9% (Q1 2026), +0.1% (Q2 2026). Q2 carried a 60 EURm transit fall; excluding transit, revenue grew about 8%, mostly System Solutions.

C-07

To what extent is a macro force acting on the company, on its sector and on its industry, and at which level does it bite hardest?

C55

Judgement: the macro force bites hardest at the valuation level, through the discount rate and the Greek country risk premium, not through earnings; at the earnings level Greek inflation is, unusually, a net positive for OTE in 2027 because it sets the size of a contractual price rise on near-100%-margin revenue.

  • Company: 98.3% of revenue from Greece and 99.89% in euros; Greek growth around 2% against below 1% for the euro area; public debt in the low 140s% of GDP; Greek 10-year yield in line with France; OTE rated A- by S&P since June 2026, the only A-category corporate in Greece. Net debt is 0.27x EBITDA and all debt is fixed-rate euro, so rates do not reach earnings.
  • Sector and industry: European telecoms trade as yield stocks; the US 10-year near 5.10%, the ECB at 2.25% with a hike expected, and the sector median at 11.8x earnings 24 months ahead (34th percentile of its history). The weekly environment digest reads risk-off, French sovereign stress spilling into Italy, and Country GR cut from Overweight to Neutral on 9 October 2026.
  • Where it bites: a rise in the euro risk-free rate or a widening of peripheral spreads de-rates the whole group; OTE's 2012 to 2015 history (6x earnings in 2012, a 75% Grexit probability in a 2015 broker note) is the extreme version. Inflation's earnings effect is two-sided: it lifts the December 2026 indexation, and it squeezes prepaid and pay-TV customers and raises wages and unhedged energy.
  • Peer contrast: Polish and German contractual pricing is under legal attack (UOKiK investigation of Orange Polska; the Vodafone Germany ruling); Greek pricing is not yet, so OTE is among the few European incumbents where higher inflation is still a clean earnings positive.
C-08

To what extent is a key driver at the industry or sector level accelerating the company's demand?

❌ D48

Judgement: none of the industry drivers is accelerating demand; they are steady value migrations producing 1% to 3% service revenue growth, and the one that did accelerate revenue (RRF-funded digitalisation) is ending. What is accelerating is utilisation of a fixed-cost network, which lifts margin rather than demand.

  • Fibre migration: stop-sell plus footprint growth (2.22m homes passed, +18.5%; 2.4m by end-2026; 3.5m, about 73% of premises, by 2030) took FTTH to 54% of eligible customers in the footprint and utilisation to about 42% from 31% a year earlier. Value per line +10%; units flat.

  • Pay-TV legalisation: a real but small accelerator (+15k net additions a quarter; Greek pay-TV penetration below 35% against 50% in Europe).
  • Digitalisation and AI: the RRF accelerator ends; GPU-as-a-Service, cloud and security are small and undisclosed.
C-09

What other material external dynamic, positive or negative, is at work?

❌ D48

Judgement: the material dynamic beyond the value chain is ownership change at the two Greek challengers, which decides whether competitive conduct stays rational; the reclassification of Athens to Developed Market status is a share-price force that has largely played out.

  • Challenger ownership: United Group (BC Partners) is exploring a sale of the group or of Nova; Vodafone Group is under pressure from Vega (Xavier Niel, founder of the price disruptor Iliad, 17% to 19.9%) for cost cuts and a return-on-capital focus, and Vodafone has already sold Spain and Italy. A return-focused Vodafone has a strong motive to move its Greek fixed base off OTE's wholesale network into the venture it co-owns. A new Nova owner could join the venture, build, or behave like a maverick. Base case: rational; tail: a disruptive owner.
  • Reclassification: Athens moved to Developed Market status by September 2026; Morgan Stanley flagged the risk that OTE is not included in MSCI Developed Market standard indices during the transition, with emerging-market funds selling before developed-market funds buy. The shares are 7% below their 52-week high and fell 6% over three months.
  • Other: S&P's A- lowers funding cost; Deutsche Telekom's own priorities (cash extraction, tower monetisation in Europe) shape OTE's options; wildfires and floods are a physical risk to an aerial-free but extensive network.

To what extent have the chief executive, or other key leaders or key actors changed, and what does the change signal?

Question asked To what extent have the chief executive, or other key leaders (e.g. finance director, operations director, board, division heads, etc.) or other key actors (e.g. activists, major shareholders) changed, and what does the change signal?

Judgement: the decisive change (a new chief executive from Deutsche Telekom's own bench on 1 July 2024) is 28 months old and fully expressed; the last six months brought no further change, so the signal now is continuity and maturity, not discovery. The next leadership signals are dated: all ten board terms expire together in 2027, and the finance succession is untested.

  • What changed and when: Kostas Nebis (Chairman and CEO) succeeded Michael Tsamaz after about 14 years; he replaced the CTO and CIO in July 2024, appointed a new CCO Business from 1 March 2025, and ran a 2025 organisational redesign. Loop by loop: mechanism, evidence, and what it does to the numbers.

  1. Retail fibre lock-in (reinforcing, positive; slow).   2. Capital return (reinforcing, positive; mechanical). Free cash flow funds a 70% to 100% payout; the buyback retires about 2.7% to 3.1% of shares a year; per-share free cash flow and dividends rise faster than the totals; the policy is re-anchored each year to actual cash. Financial effect: about +3 points a year of EPS and DPS growth with near certainty; it is the most predictable loop in the case. Its limit: it is formulaic, not price-sensitive (the 9.79m shares cancelled in June 2026 were bought at 16.52 EUR on average), so it does not accelerate when the shares are cheap.

  3. Wholesale (reinforcing against OTE from 2027; currently held back by a balancing defence). Today the loop still runs for OTE: rivals rent OTE fibre (48% of the rivals' FTTH subscribers, up from 46%), volume-commitment discounts of up to 28% (effective August 2024) tie them to it, and every migrated rival line pays about 10% more than copper.   6. Challenger fragility (reinforcing for price discipline; latent reversal through ownership change). Rivals that cannot fund a price war raise prices instead; but their weakness invites a sale (United Group exploring a sale of Nova; Vega pressing Vodafone), and a new owner with a long horizon (Saudi Telecom and e& were reported doing due diligence, Deutsche Bank 20-Sep-24) could flip the loop.
