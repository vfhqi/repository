TORM (TRMD.A-DK): what the Q3 2026 results do to the existing case

These results leave the existing case where it was, because the case was written with them in hand; but the same evidence admits two readings the case does not hold, one bearing on when the fade starts and one on whether our signposts measure what TORM actually earns.

1. What changed

  • The quarter. TORM reported Q3 2026 on 4 November 2026: time charter equivalent earnings of USD 353 million (Q3 2025: USD 236 million), EBITDA of USD 274 million (USD 152 million), net profit of USD 199 million (USD 78 million), adjusted net profit of USD 185 million after a USD 14.4 million profit on two vessel sales, basic EPS of USD 1.94, free cash flow of USD 256 million. The fleet earned USD 44,803 per day in the quarter.
  • The dividend. USD 1.35 per share, USD 139 million in total, 75 percent of net profit excluding non-recurring items, payable 2 December 2026.
  • Guidance. Only the lower end moved. Full-year 2026 TCE is now USD 1,750 million to USD 1,900 million (from USD 1,700 million to USD 1,900 million on 12 October 2026); EBITDA USD 1,350 million to USD 1,500 million (from USD 1,300 million to USD 1,500 million). The top was left unchanged.
  • Bookings, as of 28 October 2026. 53 percent of Q4 earning days at USD 78,419 per day: LR2 68 percent at USD 112,150, LR1 38 percent at USD 106,271, MR 49 percent at USD 59,722. For the full year, 88 percent of days at USD 50,487 per day.
  • Assets. NAV of USD 4,062 million, USD 39.60 per share, at 30 September 2026; fleet on water valued at USD 4,333 million, a 53 percent premium to carrying value.
  • Commitments. Eight LR2 newbuildings agreed after the quarter through a 50/50 joint venture with a shipyard; contracted MR newbuildings now eight.
  • The response. The shares fell 19.44 percent in the week ending 6 November 2026, alongside Frontline (down 20.18 percent), Hafnia (down 13.80 percent) and Scorpio Tankers (down 12.79 percent). The review found no post-results broker notes on TORM and no pre-results consensus for the quarter, so there is no measured beat or miss and no sell-side response to carry yet.

Against the case: all of this is what the case assumed. It took the quarter, the booked Q4, the guidance range and the NAV as its starting point.

2. The challenge

Reading one: the record rates are being paid by flows coming back, not by flows being shut, so the first stage of normalisation is probably good for product tankers. The case treats the four disruption forces as creating "distance and dislocation, not cargo", and maps de-escalation to a fall in rates within weeks. The review's evidence points to a different sequence. The closure of the Strait of Hormuz constrained 14 percent of global clean product flows: it removed cargo as well as lengthening routes. Q3, the quarter in which the ceasefire collapsed and the Strait closed again, earned USD 44,803 per day; the Q4 book, taken as crude exports recovered to pre-conflict averages by late September, stands at USD 78,419 per day. Middle East refined product exports were still at one-third of normal, and Deutsche Bank Research described Q4 as a catch-up trade as crude moves through regional refineries. Inventories were drawn at 2.4 mb/d from March to September 2026 and management puts restocking at 1 to 2 percent of seaborne trade. On this reading the crude market turning is not the leading edge of a product fall; it is the supply of the product cargo that is now paying. The case's early-normalisation branch, to which it gives 33 percent, would then have the sign wrong for its first two or three quarters.

What would have to be true: product export volumes recover faster than route efficiency does (war-risk inspections, shuttle operations and Red Sea avoidance persist while cargo returns); the LR2 fleet stays largely in dirty trades (355 dirty against 152 clean in late October); and demand destruction, with global oil consumption projected to fall 2.6 mb/d in 2026, does not absorb the restocking. This reading moves the timing of the fade later. It does nothing for the settled rate, which is where the case says the value sits.

Reading two: the benchmark rates we watch are not the rates TORM earns on its larger ships. Deutsche Bank Research put average LR2 and LR1 spot rates at USD 294,110 and USD 212,340 per day in late October 2026. TORM's Q4 book at 28 October was USD 112,150 and USD 106,271 per day: roughly two-fifths and one-half of the benchmark. On MRs the gap almost disappears (USD 59,722 booked against a global average of USD 63,735 per day). The case explains such gaps by booking lag. The stronger alternative is that the large-ship benchmark is a thin, route-specific print that a compliant fleet with close to 20 LR2s in the market tied up in shuttle work cannot earn at scale. Management left the top of guidance at USD 1,900 million through the October spike, which is what one would expect if it does not see those rates in its own book. If this is right, two things follow. The windfall TORM is earning is mostly an MR windfall near USD 60,000 per day. And the case's LR2 signpost is too lenient: it holds the base case intact while the LR2 product benchmark stays above about USD 100,000 per day, yet at the capture rate seen in October a benchmark at that level would correspond to realised LR2 earnings at or below what the case assumes for 2027.

What would have to be true: the remaining 32 percent of Q4 LR2 days book close to the existing USD 112,150, not close to the benchmark, and peers show the same shortfall.

The reading we do not put up. That the share-price fall was group noise led by crude names is plausible, but the case already holds it as its counter-case and has a test for it. It is not a challenge.

3. What it does to the case

  • Thesis's Change Forces' Strength (the case's 15) and the tailwinds topic (20): marginally less pressure on timing under reading one; unchanged on the settled rate and the dated supply wave. The review's 20.4 percent orderbook, 63 percent of it delivering from 2028, matches the case.
  • Case Riskiness (20) and the riskiness topic (19): more pressure. The case lifts this rating for weekly trackability. Reading one makes strong rates into January ambiguous between persistence and first-stage normalisation; reading two says one of the weekly thresholds may not transmit to earnings. Not enough to move a grade.
  • Scope for upgrades (21): two-sided. If lag explains the LR2 gap, Q4 is likely to exceed the top of guidance; if the print is thin, it is not.
  • Foundations' Robustness (33): the same. The review restates the MR premium of USD 32,600 against USD 29,800 per day; it offers no evidence of a premium on the larger ships or at mid-cycle rates.
  • Setups' Fit/Crispness (9): the same.
  • The share price against the case's thresholds: DKK 247.4 on 13 November 2026 is below the 50-day average of DKK 250.0, which the case requires to be reclaimed before reading the November fall as noise, and above the 200-day average of DKK 201.6, a close below which the case treats as confirming the turn. Neither threshold has been crossed.

4. What would settle it

  • The second review, four working days on. It is likely to carry the broker notes, estimate changes and targets the first could not find, and so the first measured sell-side response. It will not carry anything that tests either reading: no realised Q4 rates, no Q1 2027 bookings, no evidence on the settled rate.
  • Reading two is testable soonest through peers: Hafnia's results, which the case dates to 18 November 2026, and Scorpio and Ardmore in mid-February 2027, by setting their booked LR2 rates against the same benchmark. TORM's own answer is the realised Q4 rate by class, which the case expects on 3 March 2027.
  • Reading one is testable in public weekly data: Middle East refined product export volumes against product rates through January, and the count of LR2s in clean trades. Rates holding while volumes recover and the count stays flat supports it; rates falling as volumes recover refutes it.
  • What nothing is committed to answer. TORM does not disclose its realised rate against benchmark by route, the premium at normalised rates, the hurdle rates on its newbuildings, or the conditions under which the payout would be cut. The review asks for all four; the company has committed to none.
