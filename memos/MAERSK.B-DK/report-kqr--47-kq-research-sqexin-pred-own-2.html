Section 2 – Suez Return and Cape of Good Hope Cost Dynamics

Section summary
  1. Rerouting container fleets around Africa has raised global voyage costs but returning to Suez will trigger a severe rate correction.
  2. Maersk faces rising network tolls as it transitions services back to the canal, while peers like Hapag-Lloyd and ZIM show varying route exposures.

Cape Routing Cost Penalties

1.
Elevated expenses: Rerouting vessels south of Africa has significantly inflated the operating expenses of global liner networks. According to a Water Tower Research report from June 18, 2026, circumnavigating the Cape of Good Hope can add USD 1 mm or more in incremental opex per individual voyage. These additional opex items are driven by extended transit times of 10 to 14 days and heavy bunker fuel requirements. These persistent voyage cost surcharges have successfully been passed through to shippers via elevated base rates.
2.
Increased fuel: Maersk has faced highly volatile bunker opex during the prolonged routing disruptions. In its Q3 2026 interim report, Maersk reported that its total bunker opex rose by 37% year-on-year to USD 2.10 bn. This surge was driven by a 49% increase in the average bunker price to USD 798 per ton, although overall consumption was mitigated by a partial return to the Suez Canal. This volatile energy opex represents a major bottomline risk for Maersk if energy price spikes persist.
3.
Contractual recovery: Maersk has actively deployed commercial surcharges to insulate its Ocean segment from sudden fuel cost spikes. In its Q3 2026 earnings presentation, Maersk stated that its higher operational opex was successfully recovered through contractual bunker surcharges and spot rates. These surcharges are automatically adjusted based on global fuel indices, protecting margins from localized supply shocks. This surcharge pass-through mechanism is critical for sustaining Ocean profitability when navigating longer routings.
4.
Rising tolls: Returning services to the Suez Canal replaces long-distance opex with expensive transit fees. Maersk reported in November 2026 that its network costs excluding bunker rose by 12% because of additional canal transit charges and Strait of Hormuz contingencies. These rising transit opex items have contributed to a 4.80% increase in the company's unit cost at fixed energy. These network costs are likely to remain elevated until the alliance networks fully stabilize.

Section 3 – Unwinding 2026 Port Congestion and Handling Costs

Section summary
  1. Unwinding 2026 port congestion will release substantial container capacity but significantly compress Maersk's Ocean segment opex as network inefficiencies dissolve.
  2. Maersk's structurally attractive Terminals segment has successfully captured elevated storage fees to offset rising labor costs and localized network disruptions.
  3. Asset-light peers like ZIM remain highly exposed to operational delay opex, while integrated liners leverage port infrastructure to mitigate severe cargo backlogs.

Drivers of Congestion Costs

1.
Severe disruptions: Widespread port congestion and adverse weather in East Asia significantly impacted Maersk's operational flow during the third quarter of 2026. According to Maersk's Q3 2026 interim report, these external challenges constrained loaded volumes by 0.80% year-on-year. This disruption severely reduced asset turnaround times and restricted the Ocean segment's capacity to capitalize on strong demand.
2.
Rising opex: Operational opex suffered from elevated network inefficiencies and handling expenses as vessels faced extensive delays. Morgan Stanley reported on November 5, 2026, that Maersk's container handling costs jumped by 9.30% due to terminal congestions and Strait of Hormuz contingencies. These compounding bottlenecks pushed Ocean unit costs at fixed energy up by 5% year-on-year, representing the highest level in five quarters.
3.
Underlying bottlenecks: Landside infrastructure deficiencies have worsened the operational friction caused by seasonal weather anomalies. Maersk stated in its Q3 2026 earnings results that underlying congestion is driven by landside under-investment worsened by typhoons in East Asia. These persistent domestic constraints limit the speed at which container ports can evacuate cargo, leading to extended vessel dwell times.
4.
Contrary evidence: Some shipping executives argue that structural landside underinvestment will prevent terminal congestion from fully normalising even as weather conditions improve. Maersk management argued on its Q3 2026 call that the industry lacks sufficient terminal capacity to handle incoming vessel supply, which means congestion will become more frequent and help support freight rates. If landside bottlenecks persist, they will prevent the expected rate collapse even as routing normalizes.
5.
Severe delays: Port facilities in North Asia experienced the most intense operational backlogs due to severe seasonal weather. Bernstein Research reported on August 24, 2026, that Maersk cited vessel delays of up to twelve days in Shanghai following Typhoon Dolphin. This extreme backlog illustrates how localized weather shocks can quickly remove effective liner capacity and support elevated spot rates.
