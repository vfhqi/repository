Section 3 – Guidance Integrity, Cycle Peaks, and Rate Index Lag Behavior

Section summary
  1. Stated annual earnings guidance from Maersk shows massive historical deviations from actual results due to extreme freight rate volatility.
  2. Realized container rates lag spot market indices by several weeks due to contract structures and voyage-recognition accounting rules.

Historical Guidance Discrepancies

1.
Significant guidance variance: Maersk demonstrated a wide gap between initial EBITDA targets and actual outcomes across major cycle years. For instance, the company initially guided for 2024 EBITDA of USD 1.0bn to 6.0bn, but ultimately delivered USD 12.1bn as Red Sea disruptions altered the market. This variance illustrates how initial targets carry limited predictive value during sudden geopolitical disruptions.
2.
Extensive dataset gathered: Financial analysts identified 12 unique historical documents, 4 peer shipping companies, and 2 cyclical past cases to analyze these trends. The research tracks how guidance deviations develop over multiple shipping cycles. This structured historical perspective improves the modeling of future downcycles and rate recovery times.
3.
Moderate cycle results: In more stable years like 2019, the EBITDA discrepancy remained far more muted. Maersk initially expected 2019 EBITDA of around USD 5.0bn, which was subsequently raised to a range of USD 5.4bn to 5.8bn, eventually landing at USD 5.7bn. This shows that guidance reliability increases significantly when underlying freight rates remain balanced.
4.
Vast consensus gaps: Broker consensus estimates often show substantial deviations from both Maersk's initial targets and the final results. For example, the 2023 EBITDA consensus was positioned much higher than the actual USD 9.8bn result before being revised downward. This mismatch proves that brokers struggle to model operating leverage during cyclical transition phases.

Case Study of FY2026 Guidance

5.
Rapid upward revision: Maersk updated its FY2026 underlying EBITDA guidance in a series of dramatic hikes as market conditions tightened. The company raised its EBITDA range from an initial USD 4.5bn to 7.0bn in February 2026 to USD 14.0bn to 14.5bn by November 2026. This case study highlights that management guidance remains highly reactive rather than proactive during periods of congestion.

Consequently, the pricing-led surprise forced massive earnings estimate upgrades from researchers as capacity remained tight.

Post-Peak Declines and Lag Dynamics

7.
Delayed rate transmission: Realized container rates for Maersk do not immediately reflect spot market movements due to contract structures. Maersk experiences a transition lag of up to one month because revenues are recognized only after a voyage ends. Thus, sudden drops in the SCFI or Drewry WCI take several weeks to manifest in reported average loaded rates.
8.
Post peak declines: Historical precedents from the 2015 and 2022 peaks show that realized loaded rates fall far slower than spot indices. During these downcycles, the high share of long-term contracts insulated Maersk's earnings initially. However, as those contracts renegotiated at lower levels, realized rates experienced a painful drag toward spot floors.
9.
Contrary evidence: Some spot indices show rapid, unlagged transmission on specific trade lanes when overcapacity is severe. During the 2015 downcycle, spot rates ex-fuel dropped near zero within months, quickly absorbing Maersk's cost-saving benefits. This indicates that extreme supply waves can overpower contract lags and accelerate rate collapses.
10.
Unpredictable regional dispersion: The correlation between global spot indices and realized rates breaks down when regional routes diverge. In 2016, the CCFI and SCFI rose toward the end of the year, but Maersk reported softer realized rates. This divergence occurred because weaker North-South trades offset East- West gains, demonstrating that global indices cannot be fully extrapolated.
11.
Other side of the story: While lags delay the negative impact of falling spot rates, they also prevent Maersk from immediately capturing spot market spikes. During the early phases of rate rallies, contract business locks in lower pricing, leaving Maersk temporarily disadvantaged compared to fully spot-exposed peers like ZIM. Therefore, lag behavior smooths both peaks and troughs rather than acting as a defensive shield.

Section 4 – Below-the-Line Mechanics: Tonnage Taxation, Cash Interest, and Capital

  1. Rigid tonnage tax structures and fixed lease interest costs create severe below-the-line headwinds for Maersk during Ocean shipping downcycles.
  2. Cash depletion rapidly erodes interest income, while jointventure contributions from APM Terminals fail to prevent group earnings per share from collapsing into negative territory.

Structure in Troughs

Fixed Lease Obligations

8.
Fixed lease interest: Rigid lease obligations under IFRS 16 form a permanent drag on Maersk's downcycle profitability. Maersk carries massive right-of-use commitments, with lease liabilities standing at USD 10.45 bn at the end of 2023. This structural leverage incurred substantial lease interest expenses of USD 611 m in 2024 compared to USD 563 m in 2023. Because these lease interest costs are contractually fixed, they cannot be easily scaled down when Ocean earnings collapse.
9.
Peer leverage variance: Maersk's lease liabilities create a more rigid interest profile compared to highly spot- exposed peers like ZIM. While ZIM relies heavily on short-term chartered capacity that can be quickly returned, Maersk's lease liability remained substantial at USD 12.2 bn in late 2026. This fixed obligation means Maersk's capital structure is far less flexible during downturns than peers with shorter lease tenures. Thus, high lease interest keeps net income depressed even if spot rates temporarily stabilize.
10.
Contrary evidence: Some lease structures permit carriers to reduce liabilities through charter terminations and vessel redeliveries during market troughs. Maersk has the option to return between 10% and 15% of its total fleet capacity within a single year if overcapacity worsens. This flexibility allows management to reduce future lease liabilities and associated interest expenses, providing a partial cushion against fixed financial costs.
11.
Varying interest profiles: Maersk's total borrowing costs are split between traditional bank debt and lease obligations with differing rate sensitivities. Financial reports reveal that Maersk incurred traditional interest expenses on liabilities of USD 1 bn in 2024 compared to lease interest of USD 611 m. Unlike bank debt which can be refinanced, lease interest under IFRS 16 remains locked into vessel charter contracts spanning multiple years. This structural division prevents Maersk from quickly lowering interest costs when cash balances drop.

Joint Ventures and Minorities

12.
Stable equity income: Share of profits from joint ventures and associates provides a vital earnings buffer during container shipping downcycles. Maersk's joint ventures, primarily within APM Terminals, contributed USD 108 m of equity income in Q2 2026. DNB Carnegie projects this share of profit from joint ventures to remain steady at USD 486 m in 2027 and USD 492 m in 2028. This predictable cash flow helps mitigate severe Ocean losses when shipping freight rates plummet.
13.
Contrary evidence: Partnership disagreements and regional regulatory changes can quickly disrupt joint-venture profit distributions. Maersk cautions that shared ownership in key terminals across emerging markets can result in delayed capital decisions or operational disputes with local partners. These internal frictions mean jointventure income is not a guaranteed backstop during periods of heightened geopolitical and trade tensions.
14.
Slight minority drag: Non-controlling interests represent a small but persistent drain on Maersk's consolidated equity earnings. DNB Carnegie reports that Maersk's minorities remained steady at USD 1.06 bn in 2023 and are projected to hold flat at USD 1.16 bn through 2028. This stable equity slice means that minority partners continue to absorb a portion of net earnings even as group profits turn negative. Consequently, minority interest lines further compress the remaining EPS left for parent shareholders during cyclical troughs.
15.
Other side of the story: While minor non-Ocean segments and joint-venture earnings provide a optical cushion, they fail to protect group EPS from turning negative in a deep trough. DNB Carnegie estimates that despite USD 492 m in JV income, Maersk's adjusted net income will collapse to negative USD 2.22 bn in 2028, resulting in a negative EPS of USD 154.19. Ultimately, the immense operating leverage of the Ocean segment completely overwhelms below-the-line items, meaning freight rates alone dictate the sign of trough-year earnings.

Section 6 – Supply Wave Dynamics: Deliveries, Scrapping Requirements, and Asset

Section summary
  1. The looming containership supply wave threatens to overwhelm global shipping networks as heavy vessel deliveries extend through the end of the decade.
  2. Required vessel scrapping remains severely suppressed because cash-rich ocean carriers are playing a game of chicken to maintain their market shares. Net read: Asset impairment triggers pose a severe threat to carrier balance sheets during oversupply troughs as evidenced by historical shipping write-downs.

Impairments

Orderbook Scale and Delivery Trajectories

1.
Surging capacity additions: Core container carriers face an unprecedented wave of new vessel deliveries that threatens to undermine global rate stability. Jefferies Research highlights that the global containership orderbook stands at 14.90 mm TEU or approximately 41% of the existing fleet, with 3.50 mm TEU scheduled for 2027 and 5.50 mm TEU in 2028. This concentration of deliveries is highly likely to trigger severe industry-wide overcapacity, compressing shipping margins.
2.
Prolonged supply pressure: Extreme ordering activity from major ocean carriers continues to push the delivery peak further into the decade. NH Investment and Securities observes that Maersk ordered 26 ultra-large container ships of the 18.6k class and COSCO ordered twelve 22k-class vessels, with deliveries scheduled through 2029 and 2030. This continuous order spree will prolong the downward pressure on freight rates, dragging down medium-term earnings.
3.
Tight yard constraints: Tight global shipyard capacity has locked in several years of high utilization, preventing any immediate cooling of the newbuilding market. Maybank Investment Banking Group notes that Yangzijiang Shipbuilding secured 6.98 bn USD of orders in ten months of 2026, with deliveries extending into 2031. This persistent yard tightness keeps vessel construction costs high, raising the capital intensity of the sector.
4.
Historical delivery acceleration: Global container fleet expansion has accelerated dramatically compared to the pre-pandemic decade. Bernstein Research observes that between 2009 and 2019, an average of 1.30 mm TEU of capacity was delivered annually, representing less than 6% of the fleet. However, recent deliveries have reached historical highs, and this is expected to sustain high single-digit fleet growth through 2028.
5.
Concentrated operator orders: The concentration of the orderbook among top liner operators means that capacity decisions remain in the hands of a few dominant players. Deutsche Bank Research reports that the top ten players control 85% of the market and have 26% of the current global fleet capacity on order. Consequently, these major carriers will directly dictate the industry's supply balance as they absorb these vessels.

Scrapping Requirements and Yard Friction

6.
Ambitious demolition goals: Balancing the oversupplied shipping market will require a massive, historically unprecedented acceleration in vessel recycling. Bernstein Research projects that approximately 2.50 mm TEU of scrapping is required across 2026 to 2028 to keep the supply-demand imbalance from worsening. This required volume vastly outgrows historical demolition rates, making a balanced market highly difficult to achieve.
7.
Contrary evidence: Ship recycling has run at record lows as high freight rates encourage carriers to keep old vessels in active service. Deutsche Bank Research reports that only 5k TEU of capacity underwent recycling in 2025, and only 43k TEU is estimated for 2026. This near-absence of demolition compounds the nearterm capacity glut, showing that carriers are delaying critical fleet rationalization decisions.

Regulatory scrapping triggers: Stricter international carbon standards could eventually force carriers to accelerate the retirement of older vessels. DNB Carnegie forecasts scrapping rates to rise from 0.30% in 2026 to 2% in 2027 and 3.90% in 2028 as environmental regulations tighten. Severe scrapping bottlenecks: Physical limitations in global recycling yards and fleet age profiles represent major bottlenecks to rapid capacity reduction. DNB Carnegie reports that vessels aged 20 and above represent only 13% of the global fleet, while recycling yard capacity limits a quick fix. Therefore, scrapping cannot act as an immediate escape route for carriers suffering from severe overcapacity.

Asset Impairments and Balance Sheet Impacts

11.
Severe asset write downs: Severe overcapacity troughs can trigger massive non-cash asset impairment charges that devastate carrier balance sheets. ZIM Integrated Shipping Services reported a massive 2.06 bn USD non-cash impairment charge in the third quarter of 2023 on its container shipping services cash-generating unit. This enormous charge depleted the company's retained earnings, highlighting the risk of high operating leverage.
12.
Rigid lease obligations: Fixed lease liabilities under IFRS 16 increase the risk of material vessel impairments when market rates collapse. ZIM Integrated Shipping Services allocated its 2.06 bn USD impairment charge to right-ofuse assets of vessels and containers based on a discounted cash flow method. This allocation shows how fixed charter commitments inflate balance sheets, worsening write-down risks in troughs.

14.
Strategic book resilience: Financially disciplined carriers with low-cost fleets and solid contract coverage can avoid devastating impairment charges. Hapag-Lloyd AG reported that its impairment tests as of December 2016 and December 2023 identified no need for write-downs on vessels or goodwill. This resilience demonstrates that conservative asset valuations protect book value during market troughs.
15.
Other side of the story: While a massive supply wave threatens to trigger severe overcapacity and asset writedowns, persistent route closures and port congestion could continue to absorb excess vessel supply. Jefferies Research observes that Middle East geopolitical disruptions and outdated port facilities are expected to largely offset excess supply in 2027. If these disruptions remain unresolved, they will sustain elevated average freight rates, protecting carriers from net losses.
