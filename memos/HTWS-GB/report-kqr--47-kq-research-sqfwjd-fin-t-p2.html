Section 1 – The Key Question and Why It Matters

Section summary
  1. Pricing power across diverse operating conditions directly dictates the mid-term earnings trajectories and capital returns of Helios Towers and its global peers.
  2. Contractual protections such as currency pegging and power pass-throughs face increasing pressure from customer alternatives and regulatory shifts.
1.
Central query: "how much pricing power does this company have, and how does it hold up across different operating conditions, specifically: (1) high and volatile diesel prices with pass-through lags; (2) sharp local currency devaluation and hyperinflation (Ghana 2022 to 2023, Malawi 2025 to 2026, Nigeria for peers); (3) master lease agreements reaching the end of their initial terms, in particular the Airtel Africa and Orange agreements in the DRC; (4) a slowdown in operator capex after the front-loaded 2026 cycle; (5) customers gaining alternative tower options (Indus Towers with Airtel, Esengo Towers, MTN's acquisition of IHS); (6) sovereign stress and currency controls; (7) very low or negative US inflation acting on dollarlinked escalators?"

Consequently, pricing power represents the fundamental driver of whether Helios Towers can sustain its projected double-digit earnings compounding over the next 18 months to 3 years.

Section 2 – Diesel Volatility and Pass-Through Pricing Dynamics

Section summary
  1. Fuel pass-through clauses effectively insulate absolute cash flows from volatile diesel prices but compress margins due to revenue inflation.
  2. Structural variations in pass-through contracts explain the differing pricing power and tenancy retention rates between Helios Towers and its peers.

Pass-Through Structures and Margin Compression

1.
Analytical scope: This analysis evaluates 15 relevant sources, 6 named companies, and 2 comparable past cases, all of which are used in this section with none omitted. These materials provide comprehensive coverage of fuel dynamics across the African and Indian tower sectors. This broad data pool allows for a rigorous crossexamination of contractual pass-through efficacy and margin trends.
2.
Defensive structures: Helios Towers manages fuel volatility through contract mechanisms that pass diesel price variations directly to customers. These agreements insulate absolute cash flow from energy inflation but create a mechanical drag on profitability metrics. Jefferies Research reported on 5 November 2026 that power pass-throughs protect absolute profit but compress Adjusted EBITDA margins by approximately 200 basis points. Consequently, this arrangement shifts the operating risk to mobile network operators while preserving Helios Towers' cash generation.
3.
Protected income: Helios Towers reported that its Adjusted EBITDA margin of 55% for the nine months ending 30 September 2026 was weighed down by fuel price inflation. This performance represents a slight year-on-year expansion of 1% from 54% in the same period of 2025. The company's underlying contract terms match power-linked revenues and power operating expenses comparably. Ultimately, this pass-through alignment ensures that absolute cash flow remains protected despite the volatile price environment.
4.
Fuel mitigation: Helios Towers mitigates diesel availability and price transition risks by stockpiling fuel at key off- grid locations. The company is proactively expanding its investments in battery and renewable energy backup power sources. This strategy aims to reduce dependence on expensive diesel fuel and improve the customer proposition. Therefore, reducing operational diesel usage serves as both an environmental target and a structural cost-reduction initiative.

Peer Precedents and Contractual Vulnerability

5.
Unfavorable pricing: IHS Holding historically absorbed Nigerian diesel costs directly, exposing its operating margins to extreme fuel price volatility. This structure contrasted sharply with the direct fuel pass-through models utilized by other international tower companies. Deutsche Bank reported on 3 May 2024 that IHS suffered severe customer churn in Nigeria because MTN Nigeria found its technology-priced tower leasing costs excessive. This precedent illustrates that failing to establish clear, space-and-power-focused contracts can critically damage tenant relationships during inflationary periods.
6.
Tenant migration: American Tower benefited from the contractual disputes of its peers by securing long-term tenant migrations in Nigeria. MTN Nigeria scheduled the relocation of 2,500 tenancies from IHS Holding to American Tower starting in 2025. These new agreements were structured on space and power contracts which are less sensitive to technology upgrade surcharges. Consequently, American Tower's competitive pricing model allowed it to capture market share from a financially strained competitor.
7.
Unusual case: Vodafone Idea demonstrated the operational complexity of network optimization by physically decommissioning 21,000 tower sites in India. The company noted during its earnings call on 7 February 2019 that the immediate rental savings did not translate instantaneously into energy cost reductions due to contractual lag effects. This precedent indicates that network consolidation and tower handbacks do not provide immediate cash flow relief to operators. Therefore, tower companies retain short-term pricing defense even when tenants aggressively downsize their active footprints.

Customer Perspectives and Cost Sharing

8.
Carrier pressure: Airtel Africa actively manages its network energy exposure by negotiating cost-sharing agreements with major tower providers. The mobile operator reported during its 29 July 2022 call that it had renegotiated fuel pass-through terms in exchange for guaranteeing additional operational sites to partners. Airtel Africa has also pushed tower operators to replace diesel generators with lithium-ion batteries and solar installations. These initiatives aim to split the capital costs of green energy transitions while reducing absolute leasing rates.
9.
EBITDA erosion: Airtel Africa confirmed that elevated fuel costs remain the principal driver of margin pressure in its largest market. The operator's CFO stated on 25 October 2024 that diesel prices in Nigeria had escalated to NGN 1,226 per liter. Although Airtel's leases utilize pass-through structures linked to market prices, the sheer scale of the increase diluted its consolidated EBITDA margins below historical levels. This pressure has intensified the operator's push for alternative, off-grid power investments.
10.
Sharing solutions: Vodacom Group is addressing severe energy grid challenges in South Africa by pursuing advanced joint-venture sharing agreements. The group noted on 13 May 2024 that energy costs grew by 19% and contribute 15% of its South African cost profile. To mitigate these headwinds, Vodacom partnered with Eskom and approached the Competition Commission alongside MTN to advance network sharing under energy user block exemptions. Ultimately, this collaborative approach highlights that operators are bypassing traditional tower energy models to secure their own power stability.
11.
Contrary evidence: Deutsche Bank reported on 9 August 2024 that Helios Towers' CEO denied experiencing any of the intense price discount pressure that MTN Nigeria applied to its major tower partners. This contrast suggests that Helios Towers' geographically diversified footprint and robust currency-hedged contract structures protect it from the localized customer pushbacks seen in Nigeria. Therefore, the threat of severe pricing concessions due to fuel volatility may be less pronounced for Helios Towers than for its direct peers.
12.
Other side of the story: Despite robust contractual protection, volatile diesel prices create persistent working capital friction and timing lags that temporarily reduce tower cash flows. While absolute profit is protected under pass-through terms, the initial capital outlay for fuel purchase must be carried by the tower company before being billed back to the operator. If devaluations occur during this billing lag, the recovered currency may fail to cover the cost of replacing fuel inventory. Consequently, the pass-through defense is imperfect, exposing tower operators to cash flow volatility during rapid inflation.

Section 3 – Local Currency Devaluation, Hyperinflation, and Escalator Structures

Section summary
  1. Robust contractual protections and hard-currency indexing shield Helios Towers from severe local currency devaluations and hyperinflation.
  2. Structural currency renegotiations by peers highlight the tradeoffs between absolute currency protection and customer lease sustainability during devaluations.

Currency and Escalator Mechanics

1.
Analytical scope: This section evaluates 12 relevant sources, 3 named companies, and 2 comparable past cases to assess contractual protection mechanisms. Helios Towers reported that 69% of its revenue and 70% of its Adjusted EBITDA are denominated in hard currency. This structure ensures that core operating cash flows remain insulated from emerging market currency volatility. Consequently, the company's high exposure to hard currency minimizes transaction and translation risks.
2.
Contractual indexing: Helios Towers embeds automatic inflation and power price escalators in virtually all of its customer contracts. Inflation escalators typically take effect during the first quarter and are directly linked to the currency of invoice. This means that dollar-denominated billings escalate with US CPI, whereas local currency revenues utilize local consumer price indices. Therefore, these structured revenue escalators maintain purchasing power over the long-term lifecycle of the assets.
3.
Diversified protection: Deutsche Bank Research reported in March 2026 that four of Helios Towers' nine operating markets are innately hard-currency or pegged. Additionally, the broker noted that portions of revenues in Tanzania, Ghana, Madagascar, and Malawi are pegged to hard-currency price structures. These structural defenses protect the operator's hedged revenue streams from severe devaluations in individual markets. Ultimately, this geographic and currency mix limits the impact of localized macroeconomic shocks on consolidated earnings.

Historical Devaluations and Hyperinflation

4.
Ghanaian resilience: Helios Towers navigated the severe Ghanaian Cedi devaluation of 2022 to 2023 with minimal impact on its consolidated earnings. Jefferies Research noted in October 2022 that Ghana accounted for only 7% of the company's forecast EBITDA. While Cedi depreciation pressured local tenants, the small consolidated EBITDA exposure of Ghana insulated the wider group. Consequently, the company's diversified footprint successfully mitigated localized currency distress.
5.
Malawian hyperinflation: Helios Towers applied IAS 29 hyperinflation accounting to its Malawian operations starting in 2025 after cumulative three-year inflation exceeded 100%. Airtel Africa confirmed during its January 2025 call that Malawi met the criteria for hyperinflation accounting. Because Malawi represented only 5% of Helios' EBITDA, the hyperinflationary adjustments did not materially alter group results. Thus, the application of hyperinflation accounting highlights the extreme conditions under which contract indexation must perform.
6.
Contrary evidence: Deutsche Bank Research reported in March 2026 that Helios Towers' Group EBITDA mix remains exposed to minor fluctuations in Madagascar and Malawi. This exposure is challenging because local inflation escalations can be delayed by contractual lags during sudden currency drops. However, the company's total EBITDA exposure across Madagascar and Malawi combined is only 7%, which is too small to disrupt group earnings. Therefore, these minor currency fluctuations remain immaterial to the broader consolidated earnings trajectory.

Peer Precedents and Currency Renegotiations

7.
Nigerian restructuring: IHS Holding renegotiated its master lease agreements with MTN Nigeria in August 2024 to address extreme currency devaluation. Morgan Stanley reported that this agreement split the economic benefits evenly and reduced the USD-linked lease component. In exchange, the restructured leases shifted toward Naira-denominated terms indexed to the Nigerian CPI. This structural adjustment helped minimize the customer's foreign exchange exposure at the expense of IHS's hard-currency revenues.
8.
Peer adjustments: Zedcrest Wealth reported in June 2026 that telecom operators carry significant foreign currency lease liabilities primarily tied to long-term tower leases. Under the renegotiated August 2024 terms, contracts are structured with Naira-based CPI and diesel indexation. Consequently, this multi-component pricing model aims to systematically de-risk the customer's foreign exchange exposure. Therefore, this peer adjustment shows how operators shift currency risks back to tower infrastructure providers.
9.
Historical precedents: American Tower has historically faced significant currency translation headwinds from its extensive international operations. Deutsche Bank Research reported in June 2020 that AMT reduced its property revenue guidance by 5% due to devaluations in India, Brazil, and Mexico. To mitigate these risks, AMT historically reinvested local cash flows into new builds and selectively issued local currency debt. This shows that currency depreciation directly hurts translated earnings even when underlying operational growth remains stable.
10.
Other side of the story: While hard-currency pegging provides robust balance sheet protection, it can strain customer relationships and lead to terminal contract renegotiations if local currencies collapse. As noted by Morgan Stanley on 8 August 2024, keeping lease costs pegged to the US dollar during a hyperinflationary spiral can threaten the financial viability of key tenants. If customers face unsustainable currency mismatches, they may demand structural price concessions or see multiple rating downgrades. Consequently, absolute pricing power through dollar pegging may be compromised by customer distress during severe devaluations.

Section 5 – Operator Capex Slowdowns and Alternative Infrastructure Options

Section summary
  1. Declining operator capital expenditures and emerging alternative tower options present critical medium- term challenges to Helios Towers' long-term lease rates.
  2. Structural variations in competitor cost models and joint-venture tower builds in the DRC could dilute the company's regional tenancy growth.

Carrier Capex Cycles and Passive Economics

1.
Slowing investment: Mobile network operators are actively scaling back their core network capital budgets following the heavy infrastructure deployments of the recent cycle. Morgan Stanley reported on 25 February 2026 that falling carrier spending is leading to significantly less tower work across emerging markets. This capital constraint directly threatens the volume of new colocation bookings that Helios Towers can secure over the next three years. Consequently, the company must rely more heavily on its existing contractual lease agreements to protect its underlying earnings per share trajectory.
2.
Alternative allocation: Airtel Africa is shifting its capital allocation strategy away from traditional tower coverage expansion toward home broadband and digital services. The mobile operator's management stated on 28 July 2026 that core mobile capex requirements are beginning to come off as peak investments pass. Instead, capital is being aggressively redeployed into data centers, cloud services, and fiber networks. 147 strategic shift indicates that future tower-specific carrier budgets will face downward pressure, potentially limiting organic tenancy additions for independent tower operators.
3.
Sustained demand: Quantitative market metrics show that data volume growth remains the primary structural driver supporting long-term passive infrastructure requirements. Jefferies Research reported on 29 July 2026 that data traffic for Airtel Africa increased by 56% year-on-year across its footprint. This rapid growth in data consumption forces operators to continue investing in network densification despite broader capital constraints. Therefore, ongoing capacity upgrades should partially offset the impact of declining coverage capex on Helios Towers' organic lease-up rates.

Build-To-Suit Pricing and Tenancy Disincentives

4.
Calibrated returns: Helios Towers utilizes a highly disciplined investment framework that links new build-to-suit tower construction directly to long-term anchor tenant commitments. The tower company reported on 26 March 2026 that its new builds target an average day-one return on invested capital of 12%. These projects are initiated only after securing a firm contract with an initial lifespan exceeding ten years. Consequently, this strict threshold protects the company's initial capital expenditure from being deployed on low-yield or speculative locations.
5.
Incremental leverage: Commercial lease-up on newly constructed sites remains highly profitable because of the minimal incremental operating costs required for additional tenants. Morgan Stanley reported on 3 November 2026 that attracting a second tenant to a Helios site yields an incremental return on capital of over 35%. Furthermore, a third colocation tenant can drive this incremental return to more than 50% per tenant. These highly lucrative economics mean that increasing the consolidated tenancy ratio to the company's 2030 target of 2.5x remains the primary driver of margin expansion.
6.
Premium positioning: Helios Towers maintains a rigid pricing structure by refusing to offer discount incentives to secondary colocation tenants. Peer analysis shows that Cellnex typically offers a substantial 50% discount to secondary tenants relative to the rate paid by the primary anchor. In contrast, Helios Towers charges secondary tenants rates similar to those of primary anchors on a point-of-service basis. While this policy protects average lease rates, it may disincentivize non-anchor operators from colocating on Helios towers when cheaper alternative structures are available.

Emerging Alternative Tower Options in Africa

7.
Competitive entry: Indus Towers is leveraging its low-cost Indian operating model to establish a direct competitive presence in key African markets. The infrastructure provider confirmed on 19 August 2026 that it has secured operating licenses and established step-down subsidiaries in Nigeria, Uganda, and Zambia. Management claims that its established vendor relationships and engineering expertise allow it to achieve meaningful capital expenditure savings compared to regional peers. This cost advantage could allow Indus Towers to undercut established lease rates as it rolls out passive infrastructure.
8.
Alternative anchors: Airtel Africa has committed to acting as the primary anchor customer for Indus Towers' new African operations. Airtel Africa's CEO stated on 28 July 2026 that the operator will utilize Indus Towers to meet its coverage and capacity requirements in Zambia and Uganda. This partnership directly threatens Helios Towers' market share, as Airtel represents its largest customer and a key driver of lease-up.
