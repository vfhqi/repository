Section 3 – Drawdown of Grants and Recovery Plan Loans

  3. Strict European Union regulatory milestones and bureaucratic bottlenecks represent the primary execution risks for rapid capital drawdowns.

These state-supported credit facilities offer fixed interest rates that are significantly below market averages. This cheap financing is critical because it will substantially reduce interest expense burdens and support earnings per share expansion.
2.
Growing recovery liabilities: PGE has actively drawn from its recovery credit limits to finance long-term renewable and distribution grid transformations. The utility's outstanding liabilities under the RRP rose to PLN 1,952 million by June 30, 2026, up from PLN 1,663 million at the end of 2025. This strategic deployment allows PGE to finance large-scale capital programs while maintaining acceptable leverage ratios.
3.
Extensive credit capacity: PGE maintains a massive credit limit under the National Recovery and Resilience Plan to fund upcoming green investments. The available credit capacity under this national program reached PLN 12,832 million as of June 30, 2026. Securing these funds is crucial to insulate PGE from capital constraints as its total strategic outlays accelerate.

This financing mechanism shows how Polish utilities can optimize their long-term debt profiles through state development bank partnerships.

This stable funding schedule will underpin distribution network modernization projects without diluting current shareholder equity.

Section 4 – Net Debt Definitions and Rating Agency Covenants

Section summary
  1. PGE's standard net debt definition excludes massive carbon obligations, causing a large gap between standard leverage and actual economic debt.
  2. Bank covenants provide generous exclusions for project finance and factoring, whereas rating agencies apply broader metrics that increase downgrade risks.
  3. Peer comparisons with Tauron reveal similar covenant limits of 3.5x to 4.0x, though peers currently maintain superior covenant headroom.

PGE Net Debt Architecture

1.
Conservative standard measure: PGE defines its standard net debt as short- and long-term financial liabilities less cash, cash equivalents, and short-term deposits. The company explicitly excludes restricted cash from this standard calculation as of December 31, 2025. This traditional calculation provides a basic picture of liquidity but understates the total economic obligations the group faces during its transition.
2.
Alternative economic definition: PGE tracks a broader metric called economic net debt to capture its extensive environmental and operational commitments. This economic definition adds future payments and settlements for carbon dioxide allowances, lease liabilities under IFRS 16, and the reversal of accounting valuation effects of recovery loans. By incorporating these obligations, the group can accurately assess its true leverage and credit risks.
3.
Substantial liability expansion: PGE saw its calculated economic net debt reach PLN 15,280 million as of March 31, 2026, compared to standard net debt of PLN 7,136 million. This massive difference arises primarily from the heavy cost of outstanding carbon dioxide obligations that are excluded from standard debt measures. This wide gap demonstrates how standard metrics significantly underrepresent PGE's funding needs for carbon compliance.
4.
Favorable leverage trends: PGE reported a standard net debt of PLN 1.8 billion at the end of June 2026, which translates to a low leverage ratio of 0.13x. However, the group's economic net debt reached PLN 14.2 billion in the same period, corresponding to an economic leverage ratio of 1.06x. This multi-fold leverage increase illustrates why credit rating agencies focus heavily on economic debt rather than standard measures.

Maintaining this ratio is a core managerial objective because breaching it would trigger costly credit defaults and accelerate repayments.
