Section 3 – Industry Structure + Concentration + Value Chain Economics 21 findings

Section summary
  1. The global mining industry exhibits a highly bifurcated profit pool where seaborne iron ore and copper capture structurally superior, high-barrier margins, while rising energy costs steepen the cost curves

Commodity profit pools and margin distribution

1.
Premium commodity margins: Long-run EBITDA margins since 1985 highlight that copper leads at 46% and iron ore at 42% because of oligopolistic structures and high entry barriers
2.
Low margin commodities: Mid-stream and smelter-heavy aluminium yields the lowest long-run EBITDA margin at 18% since 1985 due to price-taking dynamics
3.
Steeper cost curves: Commodity cost curves show that first-quartile iron ore operators achieve 61% EBITDA margins compared to only 16% for fourth-quartile producers
4.
First quartile copper: High-tier copper miners in the first quartile secure 63% EBITDA margins versus 28% for those in the fourth quartile
5.
Inelastic copper supply: Growing demand faces extreme supply inelasticity, meaning if copper prices rise, the supply response remains years away

Seaborne iron ore oligopoly and structure

6.
Concentrated export base: Global seaborne iron ore supply is highly consolidated, with the Big Four producers controlling 64% of the 1,760 Mt shipped in 2025
7.
Extreme demand concentration: A single buyer dominates the import market, with China importing 1,260 Mt or 71.6% of global seaborne iron ore in 2025
8.
Decelerating steel demand: Global steel consumption growth is slowing to a projected 1.7% CAGR out to 2030, driven by structural plateaus in Chinese real estate
9.
Fragmented competitor base: Outside the major oligopolists, minor and non-major producers account for 35.6% of seaborne supply, introducing marginal cost volatility during cycles
10.
Domestic supply support: China's high-cost domestic mines supply 18.9% of its internal consumption and set the global cost floor at US$80-100/dmt

Seaborne copper concentrate dynamics

11.
Growing structural deficits: Global refined copper demand of approximately 28 million tonnes is outstripping mine supply, pointing to persistent long-term deficits
12.
Rising supply constraints: Chilean production underperformance has tightened seaborne concentrate markets, creating a severe feedstock squeeze for global smelters
13.
Inelastic mine capacity: Developing new greenfield copper assets requires more than a decade of lead time, limiting near-term volume responses to high prices
14.
Concentrated mine supply: The top three copper producing nations (Chile, DRC, and Peru) are projected to control 51% of global output by 2030
15.
Concentrated smelting power: Downstream value-chain processing is highly localized, with China controlling 47% of global copper refining capacity, creating significant supply-chain bottlenecks

Energy cost curves and competitor conduct

16.
Asymmetric cost inflation: Fuel shocks act asymmetrically, as each US$10/bbl oil price increase lifts iron ore costs by US$0.40 to US$0.80 per tonne for major producers
17.
Copper diesel exposure: Open-pit mining is highly sensitive to energy prices, with C1 costs rising 1.3c/lb for every US$10/bbl oil price spike
18.
Pilbara diesel sensitivity: Rio Tinto consumes ~1.6 billion litres of diesel annually, meaning a US$10/bbl oil price increase lifts Pilbara unit costs by US$0.15/t
19.
Peer cost escalations: Peer operators face comparable pressures, as Vale's C1 cash cost escalated to US$24.1/t in Q2 2026 due to exchange rates and fuel prices
20.
Margin over volume: Major miners have maintained strict volume discipline, representing a clear shift toward value-over-volume strategies compared to the capital-destroying cycles of the past
21.
Other side of the story: Sceptical view: A systemic breakdown in Chinese steel mill discipline, combined with a faster-than-expected supply ramp-up from Simandou, could flood seaborne iron ore markets

Plausible problems to monitor

Rio Tinto exhibits several structural vulnerabilities where its operational and financial metrics fall materially short of peer medians or industry base rates. Aluminium smelting operations deliver the weakest long-run EBITDA margins at 18% compared to iron ore's 42% and copper's 46%, exposing the company to persistent price-taking dynamics on both input and output pricing. This downstream integration represents a structural drag on group-level returns, particularly as energy costs typically account for approximately 40% of total aluminium production costs, creating acute sensitivity to power price volatility that diversified mining peers avoid by maintaining upstream-only exposure [Cross-ref: Section 4].

Customer concentration risk reaches extreme levels, with Greater China accounting for 57.9% of consolidated sales revenue in H1 2026 and a single buyer–China–importing 71.6% of global seaborne iron ore. This monopsonistic exposure is further amplified by the consolidation of Chinese purchasing power through China Mineral Resources Group, which centralizes procurement to curb oligopolistic pricing leverage and is estimated to negotiate approximately 70% of Chinese steel mill imports.

The structural vulnerability is magnified by emerging pressure to transition away from traditional Platts pricing toward Fastmarkets indices and yuandenominated spot pricing, eroding Rio's historical pricing power advantage relative to less China-dependent peers

Iron ore cost curve positioning has deteriorated, with Pilbara unit costs rising to US$23.5-25.0 per tonne in 2026 compared to diversified peer BHP's superior cash cost position at approximately US$19 per tonne. This cost inflation stems from acute diesel price sensitivity, where each US$10 per barrel oil price increase lifts Pilbara unit costs by US$0.15 per tonne due to Rio consuming approximately 1.6 billion litres of diesel annually. The widening cost gap versus the peer median undermines Rio's historical first-quartile positioning and compresses margins during commodity price downturns [Cross-ref: Section 3, Section 9].
