Business model: Instalco’s economic architecture combines more than 150 locally managed companies with a small central organisation, allowing subsidiaries to retain customer, staffing and project decisions while receiving support in purchasing, finance, acquisitions and shared projects. This structure can create a reinforcing loop because local trust supports repeat work, repeat work improves utilisation and cash generation, and stronger cash generation funds new platforms or acquisitions. It can also create a balancing loop because weak governance or poor project selection at a minority of subsidiaries can produce under-absorption, write-downs and working-capital leakage that group-level diversification only partly offsets.

Section 2 – Capital, Cash Conversion, Leverage and Acquisitions

The capital loop and its current starting point Instalco’s reinforcing capital loop is economically straightforward: operating profit becomes cash, cash reduces net debt, lower leverage restores acquisition capacity, acquisitions add EBITA and EPS, and stronger earnings and cash flow can support a higher valuation multiple and cheaper equity financing. The loop is not yet fully closed, however, because the company’s Q2 2026 leverage remained above target and its cash conversion weakened while earnings and backlog improved.

Instalco’s audited 2025 baseline shows the importance of cash conversion to the model, with EBITA of SEK800 million, operating cash flow of SEK1,010 million, cash conversion of 108%, net debt/EBITDA of 2.8x and diluted EPS of SEK1.28. The improvement in cash generation reduced leverage from 3.3x at Q3 2025 to 2.8x at yearend, but did not yet restore the company below its long-term 2.5x target.

- Earnings Calls Q1 2026 briefly suggested that the loop was beginning to operate, because rolling cash conversion reached 100% and net debt/EBITDA fell to 2.6x while diluted EPS rose to SEK0.27 from SEK0.16 in the prior-year quarter. The Q2 report then showed the more difficult second step, as operating cash flow fell to SEK104 million from SEK202 million despite 10.3% sales growth and a 22% increase in EBITA to SEK274 million.

The Q2 2026 figures therefore support the premise that leverage was 2.6x, but they do not support a completed deleveraging cycle. The same report states that rolling cash conversion was 90%, compared with 107% in Q2 2025, while Instalco’s presentation compares the 90% figure with 108% at the end of 2025. The difference between those two cash-conversion comparisons reflects the measurement date rather than a contradiction, but in both cases the direction is negative for near-term acquisition capacity.

The comparison with H1 2026 is therefore between a historical multi-year average and a current period in which management has deliberately prioritised deleveraging and selective investments. 47 DNB Carnegie’s January 2026 analysis explicitly said Instalco was holding back on M&A to deleverage and improve profitability.

That evidence weakens the assumption that a return to SEK800 million of annual acquisitions follows automatically once leverage moves from 2.6x to below 2.5x, because management may require sustained margin and cash-flow quality rather than a single quarter below the threshold. The EPS mechanism is asymmetric between debt-funded and equity-funded acquisitions. Debt funding can produce stronger initial EPS accretion if acquired EBITA exceeds incremental interest and amortisation, but it raises leverage and can delay subsequent acquisitions if cash conversion disappoints. Equity funding reduces immediate balance-sheet pressure but requires acquired earnings to exceed the earnings yield implied by the shares issued, otherwise dilution can outweigh the operating contribution.

The Fabri transaction demonstrates both mechanisms at once, because the first 24% investment used a SEK138 million targeted share issue while the second 27% step, agreed in July 2026, was financed within Instalco’s existing credit facility. This structure preserves liquidity in the first step but increases debt-funded exposure in the second, so the EPS outcome depends on Fabri’s consolidated earnings, minority interests, interest costs and the pace of further German acquisitions.

Addtech supplies a similar contrast on self-funded M&A discipline. Addtech states that its acquisition strategy is funded from its own cash flow and supported by a strong balance sheet, while its 2026 interim presentation listed four acquisitions during the financial year adding SEK500 million of turnover. Addtech’s reported financial debt/EBITDA of 1.4x and EPS of SEK7.95 demonstrate the balance-sheet flexibility that allows acquisition activity to continue without a new equity issue.

InstallatørGruppen demonstrates that acquisition pace can remain high at moderate leverage, but its cash conversion also shows the risk of seasonal overstatement. The company completed eight acquisitions in H1 2026, increased revenue 29.5% and adjusted EBITA 34.6%, while net debt/EBITDA was 2.0x. However, adjusted cash conversion was only 71.3% because of seasonal working-capital investment, so acquisition capacity depends on the full-year cash outcome rather than the half-year leverage number alone.

DNB Carnegie’s framework makes the acquisition arithmetic explicit. It defined unleveraged acquisition growth as the product of acquisition return on incremental capital and cash conversion, with leveraged growth adding the effect of refinancing acquired EBITA through debt. Under that framework, Instalco’s 90% cash conversion is not a minor deviation from its 100% target, because a 10 percentage-point shortfall directly reduces the internally generated capital available for the next acquisition round.

Negative precedents and restart timing Fasadgruppen is the strongest negative precedent for the proposed Instalco loop. Fasadgruppen’s targets include 15% annual growth, an EBITA margin above 10% over a cycle and leverage below 2.5x, but its acquisition pace slowed while leverage remained elevated. In February 2026, the company announced a fully secured SEK504 million rights issue at a subscription price of SEK15, illustrating that a consolidator can require equity repair before its acquisition engine is trusted again.

Fasadgruppen’s Q2 2026 report showed net interest-bearing debt of SEK1,616.9 million and a pro forma interestbearing debt/EBITDA ratio of 3.3x at 30 June, while working capital consumed SEK79.4 million during H1. The case indicates that a backlog or project recovery does not automatically restore acquisition capacity, because delayed projects can consume cash before they generate earnings and leverage is measured against a stilldepressed EBITDA base.

Green Landscaping supplies a second negative precedent involving a decentralised roll-up. DNB Carnegie estimated that Green Landscaping’s FCF-to-EBITA conversion was only 28% over 2020–2025, compared with 60%–65% for proven compounders, and expected management to restrict add-on acquisitions until leverage moved below 2.5x, potentially delaying a full restart until at least Q1 2027. The evidence is particularly relevant to Instalco because it shows how an apparently attractive acquisition pipeline can remain unusable when cash conversion and leverage have not recovered sufficiently.

Green Landscaping’s operating experience also shows how acquired growth can reverse into balance-sheet repair. DNB Carnegie attributed a SEK79 million Q3 2026 one-off cost to two Swedish subsidiaries with project overruns and a German operation with accounting-related writedowns, while noting that acquisitions from 2020– 2023 had not performed as intended. The implication is that decentralisation magnifies both sides of the capital loop, because local autonomy can accelerate sourcing and growth but weak underwriting can convert acquired EBITA into provisions, lower cash flow and higher leverage.

Nimlas shows that private-equity-backed groups may continue acquiring at leverage levels that would constrain a listed company’s equity narrative. Nimlas completed eight acquisitions in H1 2026, adding approximately SEK367 million of annual revenue, while H1 operating cash flow was SEK438 million and Q2 adjusted EBITA margin reached 10.3%. Yet net debt was SEK4,750 million against pro forma LTM EBITDA of SEK1,258 million, implying approximately 3.8x leverage, which demonstrates that ownership structure and financing agreements can support a more aggressive acquisition posture than Instalco’s stated 2.5x target.

A system of interacting loops Instalco’s model contains one principal reinforcing loop and four material balancing loops. Addtech’s Q2 2026 presentation reported 8% sales growth, a 16.7% EBITDA margin, four acquisitions adding SEK500 million of turnover and net debt to EBITDA of 1.5x. The contrast implies that Instalco’s 150-plus subsidiaries provide a potential sourcing network, but its central controls must become sufficiently scalable before historical M&A pace can safely return. 375 376, governance and decentralised execution The decentralised model is a reinforcing asset only while local autonomy and group accountability remain balanced. Local leaders preserve customer relationships and project knowledge, while group systems provide procurement, monitoring and access to larger assignments.

Excessive autonomy permits weak project underwriting to persist, but excessive centralisation can reduce entrepreneurial behaviour and damage the relationships that make the model valuable.

Green Landscaping is the clearest precedent for this balancing loop. Its 2026 review identified SEK79 million of one-off costs, including SEK40 million of Swedish project writedowns and SEK25 million associated with a German acquisition, while the company strengthened country management and launched Green Performance. The case suggests that improved governance can ultimately protect EPS, but the initial discovery and closure of problem subsidiaries can reduce earnings before the benefits arrive.

InstallatørGruppen offers a more positive people precedent. No former majority owner-manager had left, and the group had formal succession planning covering approximately 70 leadership positions, with eight companies identified for active multi-year succession work. This is important for Instalco because the critical risk is not aggregate employee turnover but the loss of subsidiary leaders, project managers or relationship holders who cannot be replaced quickly.

Labour scarcity can reinforce pricing but also constrain revenue conversion. The World Bank reported in October 2026 that labour shortages were becoming structural constraints in construction, manufacturing and transport even while hiring demand weakened. Technical-installation groups with qualified labour can capture more work and defend price, but groups that win backlog without sufficient crews may suffer delays, overtime, subcontractor costs and weaker cash conversion.

Capital, valuation and EPS hierarchy The capital loop is strongest at SPIE, Addtech and the proven Nordic compounders because their low leverage and established cash conversion allow acquisitions to continue through ordinary market volatility. SPIE’s H1 2026 leverage rose only to 2.1x while it sustained self-financed bolt-on activity and improved operating cash flow by €138.1 million year on year. Addtech’s 1.5x leverage and 16.7% EBITDA margin provide a similar buffer, although its industrial exposure differs from Instalco’s installation mix.

Fasadgruppen shows the opposite interaction between earnings, leverage and valuation. Its H1 2026 cash conversion fell to 26.3% from 56.3%, working capital consumed SEK79.4 million and no acquisitions had been completed during the preceding twelve months before selective activity resumed. The rights issue supplied SEK504 million, but EPS still benefited from lower interest expense and debt after the recapitalisation, demonstrating that balance-sheet repair can precede renewed per-share growth.

Valuation is reflexive because a credible acquisition engine can raise the multiple, while a stalled engine can lower it and make equity funding less attractive. Redeye states that serial acquirers can obtain multiple arbitrage by buying businesses at lower private-market multiples than their own listed shares command, but warns that leverage, cyclical subsidiaries and organisational indigestion can cause multiple contraction. 394 ’s current valuation case therefore depends less on announcing acquisitions than on demonstrating that each acquisition produces cash and does not reopen the leverage constraint.

DNB Carnegie’s September 2026 Instalco framework places the shares at 13x 2027 estimated EV/EBITA and identifies three rerating conditions, approaching the 8% margin target, reducing leverage and returning to more frequent M&A. The sequencing matters for EPS because organic margin recovery can lift earnings first, lower leverage second and restore acquisition-led growth only third. Fabri may accelerate the sequence if its higher reported margin translates into cash, but 49% minority ownership means consolidated EBITDA will exceed the earnings directly attributable to Instalco shareholders.

Data-centre investment could partially offset this risk by absorbing skilled subcontractors and reducing excess capacity in conventional installation markets. Bravida’s September 2026 analysis argues that data-centre work requires substantially more white-collar capacity than a typical project and that Bravida and Assemblin Caverion are the only local groups with sufficient reach and track record for the most complex work. People, decentralisation and execution

A third hypothesis is that tighter Instalco 2.0 controls produce enough subsidiary-leadership departures to weaken the local relationships that support repeat work. DNB Carnegie identifies the risk that managing directors could lose commitment or entrepreneurial spirit after years of decentralised operation, particularly as the company imposes stricter accountability and execution requirements. If departures are concentrated among leaders responsible for major customers or difficult projects, the effect could move from employee turnover to lower tender conversion, project overruns, weaker collections and lower EPS.

The evidence does not yet show a confirmed wave of subsidiary-CEO departures, so this remains a scenario rather than an observed trend. Aggregate employee turnover was 15.8% in 2025, but the company’s employee data include voluntary resignations, terminations, retirements and deaths and do not isolate managing directors or senior project managers. The decisive evidence would be country-level voluntary turnover, named succession changes, customer retention and project performance in subsidiaries undergoing the operational reset.

The contrary evidence is that Instalco’s employee eNPS remained 31 in 2025 despite higher demands, while management reported improved project control and stronger motivation during the reset. Instalco also secured a 2026–2032 framework agreement with Skanska through two subsidiaries, extending an existing relationship from electrical work into heating and plumbing. These facts support the possibility that tighter controls improve relationship durability rather than destroy it, but they do not test whether smaller or weaker subsidiaries experience a different outcome.

Green Landscaping demonstrates how decentralised execution problems can produce a delayed financial reversal. The company incurred SEK79 million of one-off costs in 2026, including project write-downs in Sweden and costs associated with a German acquisition, after acquired businesses failed to perform as expected. Instalco’s own annual report recognises the same general risk, stating that acquired companies may fail to meet earnings expectations and that underperformance can trigger goodwill impairment, integration costs and reputational damage. The relevant downside is therefore not only lost revenue but a possible simultaneous reduction in EBITA, cash conversion and valuation confidence.
