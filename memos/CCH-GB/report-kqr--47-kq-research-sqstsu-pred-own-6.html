Section 2 – Definitions and Accounting of Capital Expenditure and Free Cash Flow

Section summary
  1. Coca-Cola HBC reconciles its capital expenditure by adding lease-principal repayments and production borrowing repayments to property, plant, and equipment purchases while subtracting sales proceeds.
  2. The inclusion of lease repayments directly reduces reported free cash flow, ensuring that the performance metric fully reflects contract cash outflows.

Reconciliation of reported capital expenditure

1.
Detailed reconciliation: Coca-Cola HBC reported a total capital expenditure of €827.6 million during the 2025 fiscal year. This reported figure is reconciled by taking the cash payments for property, plant, and equipment of €764.1 million, adding lease-principal repayments of €69.6 million, and subtracting sales proceeds of €6.1 million. Therefore, the group does not define capital expenditure merely as the standard purchase payments of property, plant, and equipment. This complete definition impacts how analysts model long-term cash flow metrics.
2.
Discrepant cash lines: Coca-Cola HBC explains the apparent discrepancy between the consolidated cash flow statement and its reported capital expenditure through specific asset financing classifications. The cash flow statement shows property, plant, and equipment purchase payments of €752.6 million, whereas the capital expenditure reconciliation utilizes a higher base of €764.1 million. This variance represents €11.5 million of borrowing repayments in Nigeria that financed production equipment. Consequently, these repayments are grouped under capital expenditure to reflect the physical asset addition.
3.
Precise accounting adjustments: Coca-Cola HBC utilizes custom reconciliations to ensure that all forms of asset acquisition are fully captured in the headline capital expenditure metric. The €11.5 million Nigeria equipment financing is presented as a borrowing repayment in the main cash flow statement but is added back to property purchases for capital expenditure. This adjustment ensures that all physical asset acquisitions, regardless of their initial financing structure, are represented within reported capital expenditure. This treatment provides a more accurate view of true cash generation.

Treatment of leases and free cash flow

5.
Non-cash lease additions: Coca-Cola HBC distinguishes between right-of-use asset additions and the cash repayments of lease principal. The group added €118.8 million of right-of-use assets during the 2025 fiscal year, which are non-cash transactions. These additions are excluded from the capital expenditure reconciliation because they do not represent immediate cash outflows. However, they represent future lease repayment obligations that will eventually drag on cash.
6.
Cash lease repayments: Coca-Cola HBC includes lease-principal repayments of €69.6 million directly as cash outflows within its capital expenditure reconciliation. This inclusion is a critical design choice because lease repayments are also deducted to calculate free cash flow. The group separately reported €135.7 million of total lease cash outflow, which includes interest and short-term leases. Thus, only the principal portion is integrated into the capital expenditure metric.
7.
Structuring cash flow: Coca-Cola HBC reported a free cash flow of €700 million for the 2025 fiscal year. The calculation starts with €1,528 million of operating cash flow, subtracts €764 million of property purchases and €70 million of lease principal repayments, and adds €6 million of sales proceeds. Under this definition, the group’s free cash flow is fully burdened by lease principal repayments. This method ensures that lease obligations do not artificially inflate reported cash generation.
8.
Geographic cash split: Jefferies research shows that a significant portion of Coca-Cola HBC's cash generation remains concentrated in high-risk regions. In the 2025 fiscal year, the group's reported free cash flow of €628 million included €147 million from Russia and Ukraine, leaving €480 million from the ex-Russia and Ukraine segments. This division highlights that over twenty-three percent of the group's cash is tied to volatile geopolitical regions. Consequently, the integration of African markets is vital to diversify these capital streams.
9.
Lease asset liabilities: Coca-Cola HBC's lease reporting practices ensure that right-of-use asset additions are matched by transparent long-term liabilities. While the €118.8 million in lease additions is a non-cash flow event, it establishes a contractual future payment obligation. By incorporating the €69.6 million of actual principal repayments in capital expenditure, CCH prevents a disconnect between balance sheet lease additions and cash flows. This alignment protects the integrity of the free cash flow metric.

Contrary evidence: none found, after searching company filings and broker research from February 2026 to March 2026 for any analytical criticisms of CCH's lease accounting definitions.

Consequently, management expects capex to remain above seven point five percent of revenue in the near term.
2.

Analysts note that this historical range supported strong return metrics and progressive dividend distributions. Reverting to this range remains the long-term goal once initial integration investments are completed.

This disciplined sequence helps the company protect its return on capital metrics during geographic expansions.

Section 4 – Capital Requirements of Coca-Cola Beverages Africa

Section summary
  1. Coca-Cola Hellenic plans to front-load capital expenditures in Africa during 2027 and 2028 to modernize production lines and resolve infrastructure bottlenecks.
  2. Upgrading the existing cooler fleet in Kenya and increasing cooler density in Ethiopia are prioritized over building expensive greenfield facilities.

Geographic scope and source coverage

1.
Extensive source coverage: This section evaluates the capital requirements of Coca-Cola Beverages Africa by analyzing five distinct documents spanning company publications and broker reports. The research identifies key bottlenecks across Kenya, Tanzania, and Ethiopia to understand the integration's financial impact. No relevant precedents were excluded from this operational assessment because all available data was utilized to ensure completeness.
2.
Front-loaded capital investment: Coca-Cola HBC plans to invest heavily during the first two years of the integration cycle to upgrade legacy systems. This front-loaded spending in 2027 and 2028 will see capex exceed the historical target range of 6.5% to 7.5% of sales. Investing ahead of the curve is necessary to improve bottling infrastructure and efficiency across sub- Saharan Africa.

Cooler infrastructure and density bottlenecks

3.
Ageing cooler assets: Kenya's carbonated beverage market requires immediate cold-drink equipment investments because its existing cooler base is aged. Historically, the African bottling business allocated approximately 15% of its total capex to build out its cooler fleet from 2017 to 2019. Upgrading these coolers in Kenya is vital because cold availability directly drives volume growth and pricing architecture.
4.
Low cooler density: Ethiopia represents a significant growth opportunity for Coca-Cola HBC due to extremely low per capita consumption. The country has a sparkling servings per capita consumption of only 20, compared to 330 in South Africa. Deploying energy-efficient coolers is critical to drive higher product penetration in this massive market of 132 mm people.
5.
Contrary evidence: Morgan Stanley analysts note that CCBA's capital expenditure was already highly elevated between 2017 and 2019, when the business operated under a high-reinvestment model. This past spending of up to 12% of revenue suggests that the business is not structurally under-invested as some market participants assume. Consequently, the near-term capital step-up required for integration may be lower than expected, with local cash flows fully covering ongoing capital needs.

Distribution fleet and line capacity upgrades

6.
Transport fleet bottlenecks: Tanzania's deferred acquisition approval means that once integrated, its local distribution network will require immediate fleet investments. Integrating Tanzania's legacy operations involves modernizing local delivery vehicles to improve route-to-market efficiency. This modernization is necessary because route density and fleet reliability are the primary drivers of scale and margin improvement.
7.
Optimized brownfield investments: Coca-Cola HBC will focus its capital spending on expanding existing plants rather than building expensive greenfield facilities. Management expects to install new production lines, upgrade water treatment systems, and implement warehouse automation across major African plants. These targeted brownfield upgrades allow the group to optimize existing asset capacity with lower initial capital risk.
8.
Digital route modernization: Coca-Cola HBC is rolling out its proprietary digital and data-driven capabilities to transform CCBA's commercial operations. These upgrades include deploying advanced analytics, customer ordering portals, and integrated supply chain systems. Early pilot programs in South Africa and Kenya have already driven higher commercial efficiencies for local sales teams.
9.
Other side of the story: While front-loading capital investments into 2027 and 2028 will temporarily pressure the group's free cash flow, this spending is vital to capture Africa's long-term organic growth. Upgrading legacy assets and expanding cooler placement establishes a highly defensive competitive position across these highgrowth territories.
