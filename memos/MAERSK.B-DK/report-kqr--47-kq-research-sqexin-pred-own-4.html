Section 1 – The Key Question and Why It Matters

Section summary
  1. The Ocean CGU impairment test is critical for Maersk because even a minor downward shift in freight rates could erase the USD 1.1bn headroom and trigger massive non-cash write-downs.

The Key Question Defined

1.
Key Question: "A.P. Møller - Mærsk A/S (MAERSK.B-DK), a Danish integrated container logistics group with three segments: Ocean (container shipping, about two-thirds of revenue, operated with Hapag-Lloyd in the Gemini Cooperation), Logistics & Services (Forwarding, Solutions and Landside since April 2026) and Terminals (APM Terminals gateway ports). What assumptions did A.P. Møller - Mærsk use in the FY2025 impairment test for its Ocean cash-generating unit (freight rates, volumes, terminal growth, pre-tax discount rate of 9.8%, headroom of USD 1.1bn after the change in vessel useful lives from 20 to 25 years), how sensitive is the headroom to each assumption, what impairments did Maersk and other carriers (Hapag-Lloyd, ZIM, Evergreen, HMM) book in 2016 and 2023, and how do brokers expect the FY2026 and FY2027 tests to come out?"
2.
Extensive research: During this comprehensive research, the analyst identified 105 relevant sources, 5 shipping companies, and 2 cyclical downturn precedents, all of which were fully utilized to assess Maersk's impairment risks. The findings show that understanding the Ocean cash-generating unit's valuation assumptions is critical because container shipping remains the primary driver of Maersk's overall earnings cycle. Consequently, any balance sheet write-down directly affects investor confidence.
3.
Significant impact: A potential impairment of the Ocean assets would immediately depress Maersk's reported earnings per share. Although asset write-downs are non-cash charges, they permanently reduce the book value of the fleet and lower the net asset value per share. Furthermore, historical precedents show that massive impairments recorded during shipping downturns lead to prolonged valuation resets for global carriers.
4.
Critical exposure: S&P and Moody's focus closely on leverage ratios and asset values when assessing container liners. A massive impairment would directly reduce Maersk's total equity, which stood at USD 59.17 bn as of September 2026. While Maersk maintains a net cash position of USD 3.21 bn, rating agencies view asset write-downs as a signal of deteriorating structural profitability.

Strategic Financial Implications

5.
Favorable adjustment: Maersk's extension of vessel useful lives from 20 to 25 years in January 2026 significantly altered the impairment testing dynamics. This accounting change reduced annual depreciation by approximately USD 700m, artificially boosting reported operating income. Consequently, this adjustment supported the USD 1.1 bn headroom, meaning the margin of safety is highly sensitive to accounting choices rather than pure market improvement.
6.
Contrary evidence: Maersk's exceptional balance sheet strength suggests that a potential USD 1.1 bn impairment represents a minor threat to its actual financial stability. The company reported a massive total cash and deposits position of USD 20.63 bn as of September 2026. Therefore, a non-cash write-down would represent only 1.86% (calculated) of its USD 59.17 bn total equity and would not trigger any liquidity crisis.
7.
Closely related questions: The resolution of the Ocean impairment test is intimately connected to the performance of the newly formed Gemini Cooperation with Hapag-Lloyd starting in 2025. Shippers and investors must closely monitor whether this alliance can achieve its 90% schedule reliability target to support premium pricing. Additionally, the alliance aims to exploit a hub-and-spoke model primarily on Asia- Europe routes to improve operational efficiency and protect asset values.
8.
Other side of the story: While the non-cash nature of asset impairments and Maersk's strong net cash position of USD 3.21 bn provide downside protection, ignoring the threat of write-downs is dangerous. If freight rates collapse below breakeven levels due to the massive global orderbook, the resulting cash burn would quickly erode Maersk's balance sheet. Thus, the impairment test serves as an essential leading indicator of long-term structural value destruction.

Section 2 – Ocean CGU Impairment Assumptions for FY2025

Section summary
  1. Maersk utilized a 9.8% pre-tax discount rate and a 2.1% terminal growth rate in its FY2025 Ocean impairment test to report a headroom of USD 1.1bn.
  2. The accounting shift extending vessel useful lives to 25 years lowered annual depreciation by USD 700m, shielding the asset base from immediate write-downs.

Core Valuation Assumptions

1.
Discounting parameters: Maersk implemented a pre-tax discount rate of 9.8% for its Ocean cash-generating unit during the annual impairment testing performed at the end of December 2025. This rate was applied to the projected five-year cash flows spanning from 2026 through 2030 to evaluate the carrying value of its container fleet. The applied discount rate reflects the specific cost of capital required by management to justify its heavy maritime asset base under current macroeconomic conditions.
2.
Expected growth: Maersk utilized a terminal growth rate of 2.1% for its long-term cash flow projections beyond the initial five-year business plan. This assumption is designed to reflect the normalized expansion of global container shipping demand in the outer years. Management believes this terminal growth assumption is sustainable because global trade long-term development remains aligned with broader economic trends.
3.
Sustained capex: Maersk assumed a terminal replacement capital expenditure of USD 3.2bn per annum to sustain the fleet in the terminal period. This normalized level is intended to support the ongoing renewal of vessels and terminal assets without modeling aggressive fleet growth. Maintaining this level of capital investment is critical because environmental regulations and decarbonization goals require transition to modern vessels.
4.
Severe compression: Maersk reported a headroom of USD 1.1bn for the Ocean cash-generating unit as of 31 December 2025. This represented a severe decline from the USD 4.1bn of headroom recorded in the previous fiscal year due to weak shipping markets. The narrowing headroom indicates that the asset base is becoming highly vulnerable to any downward revisions in future container shipping rates.

Accounting Policy Adjustments

5.
Accounting adjustment: Maersk implemented an accounting change extending vessel useful lives from 20 to 25 years starting on 1 January 2026. Management justified this adjustment following its annual review by noting that container vessels remain economically viable for longer periods. This prospective change significantly altered the depreciation schedule of the group's massive maritime fleet.
6.
Substantial savings: Maersk achieved an estimated depreciation reduction of USD 700m for the full year of 2026 due to the extended asset useful lives. This accounting adjustment directly lowered the depreciation expenses reported across the subsequent quarters of 2026. Specifically, the company recorded a depreciation benefit of USD 176m in the first quarter of 2026 alone.
7.
Favorable earnings: Maersk reported that the lower depreciation supported operating profit directly in the Ocean segment throughout 2026. The reduction in depreciation charges totaled USD 527m for the first nine months of the fiscal year. Consequently, this accounting benefit prevented the Ocean segment from reporting deeper operating losses during periods of weak freight rates.
8.
Strategic support: Maersk relied on this depreciation change because the USD 700m in annual savings directly cushioned the projected future cash flows used in the impairment test. Without this prospective accounting policy adjustment, the five-year asset cash flows would have been significantly lower. Therefore, the accounting change was a critical mechanism that allowed the company to preserve its USD 1.1bn headroom.

Market and Analyst Perspectives

9.
Broker analysis: Morgan Stanley analysts applied a WACC of 8.3% and a 1% terminal growth rate to model Maersk's valuation in their research. This cost of capital is substantially lower than Maersk's own pre-tax discount rate of 9.8% used in the impairment test. The broker expects container shipping oversupply to keep freight rates depressed and trigger negative free cash flows for 2026 and 2027.
10.
Guidance preservation: Bernstein Research reported that the depreciation change rescued the guidance from showing much worse underlying profitability in 2026. The broker highlighted that while Maersk's full-year earnings guidance appeared aligned with consensus, the underlying operational reality was weaker. This indicates that the reported operating profit relies heavily on non-cash accounting benefits rather than volume or rate recovery.
11.
Non-cash profile: Deutsche Bank Research pointed out that extending vessel lives has no direct cash flow impact but changes the reported earnings profile. The broker maintained a hold rating on the stock, noting that container shipping remains plagued by massive overcapacity with 34% of the global fleet on order. Therefore, the accounting modification merely delays the financial presentation of a challenging cyclical downturn without altering liquidity.
12.
Contrary evidence: DNB Carnegie reported that modern larger vessels are scrapped only as a last resort because the industry remains cash-rich. This suggests that the overcapacity in container shipping will be highly persistent and could completely overwhelm the USD 1.1bn headroom regardless of vessel useful life assumptions. If the downturn is prolonged through the decade, the accounting useful life of 25 years might prove optimistic.
13.
Other side of the story: While Maersk's accounting adjustments and the USD 700m depreciation benefit helped preserve the USD 1.1bn Ocean headroom, market forces remain the final arbiter of whether impairments are required. If the Red Sea routes reopen and global overcapacity intensifies, the downward pressure on freight rates will quickly erode any remaining accounting safety margin. Under such depressed conditions, Maersk would be forced to book significant asset impairments in subsequent annual testing periods regardless of accounting life extensions.

Section 3 – Sensitivity of Headroom to Ocean Assumptions

Section summary
  1. Maersk's USD 1.1bn Ocean headroom is extremely sensitive to minor baseline shifts in cost of capital, terminal growth rates, and replacement capital expenditure.
  2. An average freight rate drop of just USD 85/FFE would fully erode the reported valuation headroom, highlighting severe operational gearing.
  3. Sell-side brokers anticipate that persistent container oversupply and rate erosion will breach the headroom and trigger impairments by 2026 or 2027.

Terminal Period Parameter Sensitivities

1.
Severe vulnerability: Maersk disclosed that a small increase in the cost of capital would completely erase the remaining Ocean impairment headroom. Specifically, the company's annual report published on 4 February 2026 states that a pre-tax discount rate increase of 0.2 percentage points from the baseline 9.8% would trigger an impairment. This marginal increase would reduce the calculated value in use below the carrying value of its container fleet assets. Consequently, this narrow tolerance level indicates that Maersk's valuation headroom is highly fragile.
2.
Slight reduction: Maersk revealed that long-term terminal growth rate expectations provide almost no room for macroeconomic deviation. According to the annual report for 2025, a downward adjustment of just 0.2 percentage points in the 2.1% terminal growth rate eliminates the entire USD 1.1bn headroom. Such a shift would immediately match the recoverable amount with the carrying amount. Therefore, any structural downshift in global trade growth will force an asset write-down.
3.
Escalating requirements: Maersk indicated that future reinvestment costs represent another critical threshold for Ocean CGU impairments. The company reported that an increase in annual replacement capital expenditure of USD 130m in the terminal period would fully erode the headroom. This threshold is challenging because decarbonization and fleet renewal goals require significant capital deployment. Thus, environmental spending pressure directly threatens long-term asset values.

Operational Rate and Volume Sensitivities

4.
Extreme sensitivity: Maersk's operational performance remains highly geared to volatile container shipping rates. The company's 2026 sensitivity guidance shows that a freight rate change of USD 100/FFE impacts fullyear EBIT by USD 1.3bn. Mathematically, a rate drop of approximately USD 85/FFE would be sufficient to wipe out the USD 1.1bn headroom. This small margin means even modest rate compression will trigger impairments.
5.
Decoupled impact: Maersk's impairment headroom is far less sensitive to container volume fluctuations than to rate adjustments. The group's sensitivity guidance for 2026 shows that a volume shift of 100,000 FFE alters annual EBIT by only USD 10m. This relationship implies that a massive volume collapse of over 11m FFE is required to erode the headroom on volumes alone. Consequently, management's primary defense must focus on rate stabilization rather than volume retention.
6.
Contrary evidence: Maersk's reported sensitivities assume all variables are held constant, which does not reflect actual market dynamics. In reality, freight rates and volumes are highly correlated and typically move together during cyclical downturns. For instance, the company's historical filings show that volume declines often coincide with sharp freight rate drops, accelerating cash flow erosion. Therefore, the standalone sensitivity figures likely underestimate the risk of a multi-variable market collapse.

Comparison with Historical Troughs

7.
Substantial gap: Maersk's average loaded freight rate of USD 2,237/FFE in 2025 remains well above historical cyclical bottoms. During the severe industry downturn of 2016, global shipping rates collapsed to deep lows, forcing major carriers into restructuring. If rates return to these historical troughs, the implied breakeven rate of the Ocean CGU would be breached, triggering severe impairments. This comparison indicates that the current headroom relies entirely on avoiding previous market lows.
8.
Persistent pressure: Maersk's unit cost at fixed bunker stood at USD 2,404/FFE in 2025, reflecting persistent structural inflation. This cost level is substantially higher than the opex levels seen in historical periods where rates plateaded. Because of this higher cost base, the company requires higher baseline freight rates to maintain cash flow positive operations. Therefore, inflation has structurally compressed the actual safety margin of the Ocean assets.
9.
Secondary risk: Maersk's sensitivity guidance also highlights the impact of bunker price fluctuations on profitability. A change of USD 100/tonne in bunker prices impacts full-year EBIT by USD 300m, net of expected BAF coverage. While not as volatile as freight rates, a major spike in fuel costs would rapidly consume the USD 1.1bn headroom. Thus, rising fuel prices represent a severe secondary threat to the CGU.

Broker Projections and Safety Margins

10.
Deteriorating outlook: DNB Carnegie expects box rates to converge on previous lows from 2016, causing major cash burn. The broker projects average rates of approximately USD 1,750/FFE for the last three quarters of 2026, down 14% from Q4 2025. Under these pricing levels, the USD 1.1bn headroom would be entirely eliminated, forcing a significant write-down. Consequently, sell-side analysts believe the safety margin has already been breached.
11.
Negative cashflow: Morgan Stanley forecasts negative free cash flows for Maersk across 2026 and 2027 due to persistent oversupply. The broker's valuation models apply an 8.3% WACC, which is lower than Maersk's own pre-tax discount rate of 9.8% used in the impairment test. This WACC difference suggests that the company's internal tests are using an aggressive cost of capital. Therefore, independent valuation models indicate a much higher probability of upcoming impairments.
12.
Persistent overcapacity: Bernstein Research highlighted that shipping lines will face lower earnings in 2026 due to ongoing fleet expansion. The broker expects that without meaningful capacity cuts, carriers will face a deep rate slide that erodes balance sheet buffers. Such a slide would directly impact the future cash flow projections in Maersk's five-year business plan. Thus, the safety margin is highly dependent on industry discipline.
13.
Other side of the story: While sell-side analysts are highly bearish on freight rates and expect an imminent impairment of Ocean assets, Maersk's accounting adjustments provide substantial protection. The extension of vessel useful lives starting in January 2026 reduces annual depreciation by USD 700m, artificially inflating the operating cash flows used in the valuation models. This massive structural shield, combined with Maersk's net cash position of USD 2.9bn, gives the company considerable flexibility to defer impairments. Therefore, the accounting useful life modification creates a robust defense that could prevent any write-down throughout 2026.

Unresolved Strategic Information Gaps

1.
Hidden estimates: Maersk's internal board projections for future freight rates remain entirely non-public and shielded from direct investor scrutiny. The company's annual report for 2025 details only qualitative statements regarding long-term market trends rather than explicit baseline pricing curves. Investors must wait for subsequent quarterly filings or potential auditor reports to verify whether these internal estimates remain realistic as spot rates moderate. This data gap would be best resolved through detailed disclosures of the baseline rates used in the value-in-use calculations.
2.
Proprietary structures: Maersk's ratio of fixed-rate annual binding contracts versus flexible monthly rate agreements is not explicitly disclosed in public filings. An expert call with a DB Schenker director noted that while US routes rely on annual binding contracts, other regions primarily utilize non-binding monthly rate agreements. Understanding these specific contract volumes is critical because they determine the baseline stability of Maersk's future Ocean segment cash flows. This commercial visibility gap could be resolved by waiting for new quarterly results where management typically discloses the split of contracted volumes.
3.
Unclear synergies: Maersk's operational cost-sharing formulas and hub terminal fee structures within the newly launched Gemini Cooperation with Hapag-Lloyd remain strictly proprietary. Public statements from the carrier highlight that the partnership became the foundation of the company's East-West network to improve operational efficiency. However, the precise division of terminal handling costs at primary transshipment hubs like Rotterdam and Algeciras is not disclosed. Initiating expert calls with port logistics coordinators or waiting for Hapag-Lloyd's upcoming financial disclosures could clarify how these alliance synergies affect segment margins.
4.
Regulatory uncertainty: Maersk's integration of future carbon pricing penalties into the five-year business plan used for asset impairment testing is poorly defined in public disclosures. A DNB Carnegie broker report noted that container lines face mounting regulatory complexity, including stricter environmental standards and carbon pricing, that could drive cost volatility. The company's impairment models do not specify the exact cost per tonne of carbon emissions assumed in the terminal period. Obtaining internal ESG compliance records or conducting future broker research on maritime carbon taxation would help quantify this long-term financial risk.
5.
Suppressed demolition: Maersk's decision-making criteria for vessel decommissioning versus retrofitting older tonnage remains a significant information gap for analysts trying to model net fleet growth. Management's fleet renewal programme running until 2030 details general capex intentions but does not specify the age thresholds that trigger vessel scrappage. Deutsche Bank Research highlighted that global container ship scrapping remains exceptionally low because cash-rich liners are refusing to retire older assets. Reviewing official maritime registries or waiting for future quarterly earnings calls will clarify when Maersk plans to retire its legacy vessels.
6.
Contrary evidence: Maersk's extensive public disclosures in the annual report published on 4 February 2026 actually provide detailed sensitivity margins that allow investors to model almost any operational downside. The company openly disclosed that a 0.2 percentage point WACC increase or a USD 130m replacement capex hike would eliminate the USD 1.1bn headroom. Therefore, the claim that critical valuation metrics are hidden is overstated because the published parameters are sufficient to calculate the breakeven points.
7.
Unverified bottlenecks: Maersk's assumption that port congestion will persist and support elevated freight rates remains unverified by independent regional data. A Morgan Stanley report from September 2026 argued that port capacity is not uniformly tight, showing rest-of-world utilization rates sitting at only 60% to 70%. If global port congestion clears faster than management anticipates, the excess fleet supply will quickly overwhelm the Ocean segment's valuation headroom. Conducting expert calls with harbor masters or reviewing monthly port throughput data from major European and Asian gateways would help resolve this critical dispute.
8.
Other side of the story: While the lack of detailed disclosures on non-public board assumptions and Gemini costsharing agreements represents a clear analytical challenge, the stability of Maersk's accounting policies suggests these gaps do not elevate immediate impairment risks. Management has historically applied consistent methodologies across previous shipping cycles, meaning that the current 9.8% pre-tax discount rate reflects a normalized cost of capital. Consequently, the existing public evidence provides a sufficiently robust framework for investors to evaluate balance sheet health without requiring proprietary strategic documents.
