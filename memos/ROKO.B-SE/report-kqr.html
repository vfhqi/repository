Section 3 – Minority Interest Accounting and Option Liabilities

Section summary
  1. Röko AB consolidates its subsidiaries to 100 percent under IFRS 9 because of contractual put and call agreements, bypassing minority deductions in the income statement but creating substantial long-term option liabilities.
  2. The conversion of non-interest-bearing option liabilities into interest-bearing bank debt upon exercise will pressure group interest expenses while gradually improving cash conversion as minority dividend outflows shrink.

Full consolidation and earnings distortions

1.
Complete ownership: Röko AB fully consolidates all subsidiary companies to 100 percent in its financial statements provided the contractual put and call option agreements regarding outstanding ownership with minority shareholders. This accounting treatment means that the entire result for the year is presented as attributable to Parent Company shareholders. By avoiding minority interest deductions on the face of the income statement, Röko reports higher consolidated net income than its economic ownership stake would otherwise imply.
2.
Equity revaluations: Röko AB accounts for put and call option liabilities by recognizing them at fair value through equity rather than the income statement in accordance with IFRS 9. During the first nine months of 2026, the company recorded an option liability revaluation increase of MSEK 135 directly in equity. This treatment prevents volatile revaluations of these massive liabilities from distorting reported earnings per share on a quarterly basis.
3.
Financing dividends: Röko AB includes declared dividends paid to non-controlling interest holders directly in the put and call option liability balance. These minority dividends are reported within the group cash flow statement as part of cash flow from financing activities. This presentation prevents minority cash distributions from reducing operating cash flows, thereby presenting a more favorable picture of cash generation before option exercises.
4.
Multiple distortions: Röko AB only owns an average of 75 percent of its underlying businesses despite consolidating 100 percent of their earnings. This structural gap means that investors relying on reported figures calculate a depressed true earnings multiple unless they adjust for the minority share. Incorporating the future purchase price of the remaining 25 percent stake is essential to accurately reflect the total economic price paid for the consolidated streams of EBITA.

Option mechanics and leverage impacts

5.
Significant liabilities: Röko AB reported that its total put and call option liabilities reached MSEK 2,779 as of September 30, 2026. The maturity structure of these obligations is long-dated, with MSEK 449 exercisable within 12 months, MSEK 1,105 between one and three years, and MSEK 1,224 after more than three years. This maturity profile indicates that the group faces sustained long-term cash outflows as minority shareholders gradually exercise their options.
6.
Debt conversion: Röko AB intends to fund the exercise of put and call options by drawing on its existing revolving bank credit facilities. This transaction converts a non-interest-bearing option liability into interest-bearing bank debt on the balance sheet. Because the fair value of these options is already included in Röko's financial net debt, the total net debt figure remains unchanged during this conversion.
7.
Refinancing friction: Röko AB faces the risk of rising interest expenses when non-interest-bearing option liabilities are converted into interest-bearing bank debt. However, this conversion will also improve future cash conversion because a smaller share of group profits will be distributed as dividends to minorities. This offset suggests that the overall impact on earnings per share will depend on the prevailing bank lending rates at the time of exercise.
8.
Contrary evidence: DNB Carnegie reported on May 5, 2025, that the balance sheet impact on net debt from exercising the put and call options is virtually zero. The broker explained that the fair value of those options is already fully incorporated into Röko's net debt definition, which would simply replace the liability with credit drawings. This implies that the conversion of option liabilities does not represent a sudden re-leveraging event for the group's balance sheet.

Peer comparison and sector context

9.
Visible deductions: Idun Industrier AB reports under the Swedish K3 accounting standard, which requires a visible deduction of the minority share in earnings on the face of the income statement. This GAAP difference complicates direct financial comparisons because Idun's reported net income reflects only its economic ownership of consolidated businesses. Consequently, Idun's reported earnings per share do not suffer from the same accounting-driven inflation as Röko's reported metrics.
10.
Minority shares: Idun Industrier AB owns approximately 85 percent of its consolidated EBITA, meaning that minority partners retain a 15 percent claim on earnings. This ownership structure compares to Röko's average ownership level of 75 percent, representing a larger minority leak at Röko. The higher minority retention at Röko creates greater economic earnings dilution over the long term unless the remaining stakes are fully acquired.
11.
Elevated leverage: Idun Industrier AB operates with put and call options tied to minority holdings that constitute approximately 1.0x of its ND to EBITDA ratio. This option-inclusive leverage pushes Idun's total net debt to EBITDA ratio to 2.9x on 2026 estimates, which is relatively stretched compared to peers. By comparison, Röko's interest-bearing net debt to EBITDA was 1.1x in September 2026, but its financial net debt including options reached a higher level.
12.
Lower ownership: Röko AB and Idun Industrier possess lower average ownership of their subsidiaries at 75 percent and 85 percent, respectively, compared to established peers. Proven compounders like Addtech, Lagercrantz, and Indutrade typically own between 95 percent and 100 percent of their consolidated businesses. This peer gap means that Röko and Idun have a weaker underlying cash conversion because of minority dividend leaks.
13.
Debt transition: Idun Industrier AB completed a refinancing in March 2025 by entering a new loan agreement with Danske Bank and SEB to optimize its capital structure. The company subsequently utilized bank credit to fund the early redemption of its outstanding senior unsecured bonds in June 2026. This transition demonstrates how serial acquirers can successfully migrate from expensive debt to bank facilities to support their acquisition engines.
14.
Other side of the story: The 100 percent consolidation of subsidiaries and the use of minority option structures provide strong incentive alignment between local entrepreneurs and group management. By allowing founders to retain a minority stake, Röko and Idun reduce upfront cash requirements for acquisitions while keeping local leaders motivated. This alignment mitigates operational integration risks, suggesting that the accounting complexity and cash leaks are justified trade-offs for superior organic growth.

This highly decentralized organizational model means that day-to-day operational decisions are delegated entirely to local management teams. While this structure ensures agility and speed of execution, it limits the central team's direct visibility into the daily activities of its subsidiaries. Consequently, any operational disruption at the subsidiary level can take time to address.

Board oversight: Röko AB does not maintain a dedicated audit committee to oversee financial reporting or risk management. The board of directors as a whole performs the tasks typically assigned to an audit committee under the Swedish Companies Act. While this consolidated approach streamlines decision-making, it places a heavy administrative burden directly on the board members. This lack of a separate committee limits independent financial scrutiny.
3.
Audit framework: Röko AB has historically operated without a dedicated internal audit function within its corporate structure. The board of directors evaluates the need for this function annually but has repeatedly deemed the ongoing internal control work sufficient. Instead of centralized audits, the group relies on annual self-assessments coordinated by the Chief Financial Officer and completed by subsidiary leaders. Relying on self-reporting increases the risk of undetected errors.

Reporting risks: Röko AB faces substantial internal control challenges due to the use of different accounting systems across its subsidiaries. Although all operating companies utilize the same group reporting software, the lack of standardized underlying systems prevents immediate central access to raw financial data. This systemic fragmentation increases the risk of receiving inaccurate or delayed financial information from decentralized units. Thus, the lean governance model poses ongoing financial reporting risks.
