Industrial Self-Generation and Cost Shifting

4.
Potentially destabilizing feedback: Rapidly rising network investment costs can trigger an economic feedback loop that undermines grid utility cost-recovery models. Grids that recover massive capital costs through higher retail network tariffs risk driving wealthy customers to deploy on-site solar and battery systems. This shift reduces the volume of grid-supplied electricity, which forces the utility to raise tariffs even further for remaining users. Ultimately, this cost-shifting dynamic widens the customer inequality gap between those who can afford self-generation and those who cannot.
5.
Accelerating behind-the-meter build: Large industrial and commercial consumers are actively deploying decentralized energy solutions to hedge against volatile electricity and grid connection tariffs. Corporate power purchase agreements are expanding rapidly across Europe as buyers seek to bypass grid congestion and secure low-carbon generation. While European corporate power purchase agreement prices fell 5% to 10% year-on-year, regulated network tariffs simultaneously increased 5% to 15% due to rising grid investments. These divergent pricing trends strongly incentivize behind-the-meter generation and hybrid battery installations to manage peak network charges.
6.
Indispensable grid backup: Despite the trend toward self-generation, industrial customers cannot entirely sever their relationship with the public grid due to intermittency issues. Industrial consumers still require reliable grid connections and backup capacity to handle periods of low renewable output. These self-generators remain dependent on the distribution network, which provides essential system balancing and flexibility during weather-driven generation deficits. Consequently, this necessity limits the absolute decline in grid connection requests and provides PGE with a stable source of standby capacity revenue.
