Section 3 – Peer Start-Up Precedents and Canadian Retail Pricing

Section summary
  1. Peer capacity start-ups in Canada and Europe reveal that large-scale food manufacturing plants face heavy upfront capital outlays and complex multi-year ramp-up phases.
  2. The highly consolidated Canadian retail grocery market demands strict cost-plus pricing formulas and robust automation to protect supplier margins against extreme price sensitivity.

Maple Leaf Foods Case Study

1.
Substantial capital: Maple Leaf Foods invested approximately $780 million in building its state-of-the-art poultry plant in London, Ontario. This massive 660,000 square foot facility was designed to consolidate four aging legacy plants in the region. Construction completed in November 2022 and transitioned to full volume ramp-up by late 2023. This investment enabled significant poultry network efficiencies that eventually supported the company's long-term margin improvement.
2.
Declining losses: Maple Leaf Foods experienced significant temporary start-up expenses during the early operational phases of its London facility. These start-up costs reached CAD 122.3 million in fiscal 2023 during peak transitions. Operational efficiencies and volume transfers subsequently reduced these startup expenses to CAD 20.6 million in 2024. By fiscal 2025, start-up expenses had successfully unwound to just CAD 3.3 million as the facility reached its target run rate.
3.
Ambitious construction: Ventum Financial Corp highlighted that Maple Leaf Foods invested over $1.0 billion in large-scale capacity expansion projects from 2019 to 2023. This aggressive build-out coincided with extreme macroeconomic volatility and supply chain disruptions. Despite these capital pressures, management completed the start-up phase by late 2023 and reduced leverage from 6.0x to 2.7x EBITDA by late 2024. The broker noted that this consolidation has successfully de-risked future profit targets through enhanced productivity.

Scandi Standard Oosterwolde Plant

4.
Efficient restart: Scandi Standard acquired Tyson Foods' damaged poultry manufacturing assets in Oosterwolde, Netherlands, during the first quarter of 2025. The group executed a rapid refurbishment program with a total capital investment of about 28 million Euros to resume operations. This strategic move replaced a planned capital investment of 30 million Euros in Denmark. The facility provided a scalable platform that expanded Scandi Standard's total ready-to-eat production capacity by approximately 90%.
5.
Phased expansion: Scandi Standard successfully launched its first production line in Oosterwolde during the third quarter of 2025. The initial kebab processing line entered operations ahead of schedule and experienced limited start-up costs. Management continued testing the remaining lines through 2026, commencing trials in the highly automated Factory C. Commercial production on the first large Factory C line began in October 2026 to supply major European retail and foodservice clients.
6.
Initial dilution: Scandi Standard faced nearterm profitability pressures in its ready-to-eat segment due to Oosterwolde start-up costs. The segment's operating margin diluted to 3.5% in fiscal 2025 compared to 5.7% in the prior year. However, profitability recovered sharply as the plant ramped up and achieved higher capacity utilization. Operating margins in the ready-to-eat division rebounded to 6.2 percent by the third quarter of 2026.

Canadian Pricing and Oligopolistic Dynamics

7.
Severe concentration: Canadian grocery retail is highly consolidated with five major national chains controlling approximately 80 percent of the market. This oligopolistic structure utilizes exclusionary contracting and property controls to extract value from food processors. Processors must adapt by negotiating formulabased pricing or dedicated supply agreements to secure shelf space. Consequently, entering Canada requires massive scale and highly competitive cost structures to withstand intense retail buyer power.
8.
Lagged pass-through: Morgan Stanley highlighted that Canadian food-price shocks transmit slowly through the distribution chain as intermediate inputs accumulate. Average franchisee profit margins in Canada declined by 160 basis points in 2025 due to intense commodity price inflation. Although grocery disinflation reached 1.7% in late 2026, persistent diesel and packaging costs make future price increases likely. Upstream processors are increasingly expected to pass these costs on to consumers to recover previously compressed operating margins.
9.
Damaged trust: Jefferies Research reported that rising grocery costs have driven extreme consumer frustration and widespread sensitivity in Canada. Surveys indicate that 92% of Canadian consumers have noticed shrinkflation, with 72% observing it specifically in retail grocery products. Nearly 90% of shoppers believe that consumer packaged goods companies are using inflation as an excuse to raise shelf prices. Consequently, many cash-strapped Canadian families are abandoning previously trusted brands or trading down to lower opening price points.
