Section 3 – Chinese Volume-Based Procurement and Centralised Bidding

Expanding Volume Based Procurement

1.
Aggressive expansion: China has significantly accelerated its nationwide centralized medical device procurement to control public healthcare spending. The National Health Committee established a tiered procurement framework that divides medical equipment into national, provincial, and municipal bidding categories. Consequently, volume-based procurement penetration grew to represent 19.4% of total imaging and radiotherapy procurement value in the first half of calendar year 2026. This ongoing regulatory expansion threatens the historical pricing premiums commanded by established multinational medical technology manufacturers in China.
2.
Broadening coverage: Centralized bidding programs are projected to capture an increasingly large portion of the Chinese medical device market over the next several years. Broker surveys indicate that seventy-two percent of industry respondents expect radiotherapy equipment procurement to reach twenty to forty percent coverage under volume-based bidding by calendar year 2028. Furthermore, fifty percent of those surveyed expect medical imaging categories to be over sixty percent covered by these state-led programs within the same timeframe. This rapid shift toward centralized bidding undermines transactional hardware pricing power across most core operating segments.
3.
Prudent forecasts: Siemens Healthineers maintains a cautious stance regarding the nearterm recovery of its operations in the Chinese medical technology market. The company reported that its regional sales dropped by approximately ten percent year-over-year during the third quarter of fiscal year 2026. This decline was primarily driven by the ongoing re-basing of the diagnostics market under volume-based procurement pressure. Consequently, the manufacturer has prudently guided to flat sales for both its Imaging and Precision Therapy segments in China for the full fiscal year.

Elevated costs: High construction expenses and complex installation demands represent a major hurdle for conventional magnetic resonance imaging suites. Standard 1.5 Tesla to 3.0 Tesla magnetic resonance scanners require a dedicated radiofrequency-shielded room typically costing more than 100k dollars to construct. Furthermore, hospitals must invest in liquid helium cooling systems, chilled-water infrastructure, and specialized power supplies. These structural requirements are driving up total installation costs to over 3 million dollars for traditional high-field setups.
6.
Helium free innovations: Siemens Healthineers utilizes its helium-independent DryCool technology to dramatically reduce installation complexity and bypass high construction costs. The Magnetom Flow 1.5T scanner requires only 0.7 liters of liquid helium permanently sealed in the system and eliminates the need for expensive quench pipes. This compact footprint allows the scanner to be installed in small outpatient rooms of 25 square meters without tearing down hospital walls. Consequently, this technology helps healthcare providers bypass substantial capital infrastructure outlays in their imaging suites.
7.
Peer comparisons: Koninklijke Philips is actively competing in the helium-free magnetic resonance imaging segment to defend its market share. Philips pioneered this field with its BlueSeal 1.5T system and shipped approximately 70% of its magnetic resonance systems year-to-date as helium-free. Philips is also expanding this technology by submitting its BlueSeal Horizon 3.0T platform to the US Food and Drug Administration for clearance. This compares with Siemens Healthineers which has deployed its DryCool technology to half of its ordered units to capture the shift toward outpatient facilities.
8.
Comparable cases: Historical precedents during prior economic downturns show that high-ticket capital equipment segments suffer more severe order delays than smaller clinical modalities. During the 2009 global financial crisis, medical imaging order volumes declined significantly as hospitals deferred expensive capital replacement cycles. However, major equipment providers offset hardware declines by expanding their recurring service bases. This precedent indicates that aftermarket service contracts provide stability when hospital capital budgets contract.

Service Contract Pricing and Aftermarket Resilience

9.
Aftermarket stability: Siemens Healthineers relies on its massive global installed base to generate highly resilient recurring aftermarket revenues. More than half of the typical long-term diagnostic imaging revenue stream is derived from service contracts rather than the initial hardware sale. These service agreements are typically structured as multi-year contracts spanning three to five years. This contractual backlog allows the manufacturer to maintain stable operating margins even during volatile hospital capital cycles.
10.
Contract indexation: Service contract structures frequently incorporate automatic price-escalation clauses to insulate manufacturers from cost inflation. Siemens Healthineers has confirmed that its contract pricing in the backlog automatically adjusts upward in response to persistent inflation. These escalation clauses are typically tied to the consumer price index plus an incremental percentage. This pricing mechanism ensures that the company can pass through labor and component inflation directly to its customer base.
11.
Competitor dynamics: Peer manufacturers like GE HealthCare are also leveraging service pricing and renegotiation strategies to defend their installed base. GE HealthCare actively renegotiates existing five-year contracts and undercuts itself to secure long-term client loyalty when facing low-cost competition. However, independent service providers represent a persistent threat to standard imaging and patient monitoring equipment service revenues. OEMs typically retain exclusive service rights for the first five years, but lose service contracts to independent providers as equipment matures.
12.
Switching barriers: The high cost of switching vendors and the critical need for machine uptime reinforce customer retention for Tier-1 medical equipment manufacturers. Siemens Healthineers has built a comprehensive global service infrastructure that locks in long-term contracts and guarantees rapid support. Clinicians face a learning curve of up to three months and severe operational disruptions if they switch to a different hardware provider. Consequently, these operational barriers create a strong competitive moat that secures high contract renewal rates.

Monetizing AI and Software Upgrades

13.
Software monetization: Siemens Healthineers is increasingly monetizing high-margin software upgrades on its installed base to bypass hospital capital constraints. The manufacturer leverages its advanced AI ecosystem, including its Deep Resolve image reconstruction technology, to sell separate software subscriptions. Deep Resolve utilizes deep learning algorithms to reduce magnetic resonance scan times by up to 70% while doubling image resolution. This software-driven upgrade allows hospitals to increase scanner patient throughput without investing millions in physical hardware replacements.
14.
Lucrative SaaS growth: Software-as-a-service and smart imaging applications represent a highly profitable recurring revenue stream for medical technology players. Morgan Stanley Research estimates that softwareas-a-service products contributed less than 3% of Siemens Healthineers' group revenues in 2024. However, these advanced software lines are growing at an estimated 30% compound annual rate while delivering lucrative 40% to 50% operating margins. Consequently, these digital offerings are projected to drive significant operating margin expansion over the mid-to-long term.
15.
Other side of the story: Siemens Healthineers' software monetization could face adoption hurdles if budgetconstrained hospital administrators aggressively prioritize basic capital needs over advanced AI suites. Jefferies Research indicates that price-sensitive hospital purchasing managers occasionally reject premium software packages to minimize near-term operational expenditures. If health systems decide to extend the useful life of their existing capital equipment without purchasing premium digital upgrades, the company's projected high-margin software growth may fail to offset physical equipment slowdowns in mature markets.
