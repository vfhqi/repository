Section summary

The evidence shows that Theon's pricing power is structurally strong but operationally constrained, with multi-dimensional pressures creating a complex and uncertain margin outlook over the next 18 months to 3 years. The Key Question can be partially answered with medium confidence, though critical gaps remain around exact framework pricing formulas, back-to-back supplier arrangements, and the net profitability impact of localized export operations. Theon has demonstrated exceptional pricing power through the Ukraine war period, with night-vision device prices rising 2.5x to 3.0x between 2022 and 2026, driven by severe component scarcity with 18-month lead times for image intensifier tubes. However, this pricing strength faces mounting headwinds from multiple directions. Deutsche Bank projects that joint European procurement through agencies like OCCAR will reach 40% of defense buying by 2030, rising from just 20% recently, which acts as a direct headwind to operating margin expansion compared to bilateral country contracts due to highly competitive pricing. The company's landmark one billion euro OCCAR framework for 100,000 Mikron binoculars represents the largest single night-vision procurement in NATO history, yet Deutsche Bank highlights that although this provides approximately 30% of group revenues over five years, it is dilutive to overall margin growth, prompting a 3% trim to EBIT forecasts through 2027. 1 The upstream supply chain presents both protection and vulnerability. Exosens holds a 70% European market share in tube manufacturing, and Bernstein projects Exosens' average tube selling prices will rise from 2,531 euros in 2025 to 2,898 euros by 2029. While Theon secured over 400,000 image intensifier tubes through 2030 via extended agreement with Exosens and acquired a 9.8% equity stake in Exosens for 268.7 million euros to secure long-term tube supply, the company faces conflicting pressures on cost absorption. Expert testimony conflicts on whether Theon absorbs tube cost inflation or passes it through, with some claiming partial

absorption while others argue low Greek labor costs eliminate the need for pass-through to core customers. Geographic margin dynamics reveal a critical tension in the business model. Export markets like Saudi Arabia and the UAE yield gross margins of roughly 30% compared to 20% for European sales, with Gulf customers described as not price-sensitive and paying exceptionally well. However, European sales rose to 80% in H1 2026 from 75% in 2025, while Rest of World declined to 14% from 19%, creating a negative mix effect that compresses average gross margins.

Meanwhile, Saudi Arabia's requirement for 50% domestic procurement by 2030 forces Theon to establish local assembly through its partnership with Saudi Arabian Military Industries, shifting manufacturing away from low-cost Greek operations and posing unfavorable risks to operating margins.

The company's manufacturing cost advantage appears durable but eroding. Theon's minimum wage in Greece is only one-eighth of Germany or France, providing a formidable labor cost advantage, yet employee wages rose by 10.87 million euros in H1 2026, with hiring at German joint ventures and Middle Eastern sites compressing overall EBIT margins due to historically higher wage markets.

Contract rigidity compounds these pressures, as the OCCAR contract utilizes pre-negotiated option clauses with minimal dynamic inflation indexation, meaning unplanned component price increases must be absorbed by the manufacturer, contrasting sharply with Elbit Systems' new contracts containing indexed pricing clauses that provide crucial insulation from labor and material inflation.

The strategic pivot toward platform optronics and next-generation systems offers a pathway to margin preservation but introduces near-term dilution. Digital and thermal products yield over 40% gross margins due to the absence of costly image intensifier tubes, freeing the manufacturer from single-source dependency on Exosens, and the acquisition of Kappa Optronics for 75 million euros adds specialized platform capabilities and approximately 40 million euros in 2026 revenues.

However, Deutsche Bank argues the platform business will be less margin accretive in early stages, with Kappa yielding approximately 20% operating margin below Theon's historical group margin, initially dragging consolidated EBIT. The aggressive M&A strategy carries financial risk, as the HGH acquisition pushes forward net leverage to 3.0 times net debt to EBITDA, surpassing the 2.5 times target ceiling, with HGH priced expensively at over 7 times enterprise value to sales requiring bridge loan financing.

Deutsche Bank's base case assumes that Theon will maintain EBIT margins above 26% through its execution period, supported by operational leverage from the OCCAR framework and strategic supplier relationships. ING's research explicitly assumes zero pricing pressure in core night-vision goggles given persistent scarcity dynamics. Bernstein's projections model steady component price escalation at roughly 5% to 6% annually for Exosens tubes, which they assume can be absorbed without severe margin compression.

These broker assumptions appear moderately optimistic relative to section findings, which reveal that Theon's long-term framework agreements lack dynamic indexation clauses, leaving margins vulnerable to component cost spikes, with fixed pricing rigidity meaning the company must absorb unexpected input-cost inflation internally, directly threatening its 26% mid-term EBIT margin target. The combination of rising European volume procurement at lower margins, potential tube price acceleration beyond forecasts, wage inflation in international operations, and platform integration dilution suggests that consensus margin expectations face material downside risk unless the high-margin export mix stabilizes or next-generation products scale faster than currently modeled. The most significant evidence gap remains the exact pricing and escalation mechanics embedded in the OCCAR framework, which governs approximately one-third of near-term revenues but whose specific adjustment terms remain commercially confidential. Similarly, the precise cost-sharing arrangements between Theon and Exosens under their back-to-back supply agreements are opaque, with contradictory expert testimony on whether inflation is fully passed through or partially absorbed.

Finally, the net margin impact of Gulf localization requirements – including ongoing wage inflation for Saudi and Emirati operations–remains unquantified in public filings, creating uncertainty around whether premium export pricing can sustainably offset elevated localized operating expenses. Resolution of these gaps would significantly improve confidence in the margin outlook and clarify whether Theon can defend its mid-twenties EBIT margin targets through the current multi-year framework execution cycle.

Section 2 – Procurement Dynamics: Agency Volume Tenders vs Bilateral Awards

Section summary
  1. Joint European procurement represents a major structural shift toward consolidated high-volume defense buying.
  2. Increased reliance on collaborative frameworks like OCCAR threatens to dilute operating margins despite boosting backlog visibility.

The Rise of Joint Defense Procurement

1.
Shifting defense procurement models: European defense acquisition is rapidly transitioning toward centralized purchasing frameworks. Deutsche Bank estimates that joint procurement through agencies like OCCAR will reach 40% of European buying by 2030, rising from just 20% recently. This structural shift means that manufacturers must adapt to highly competitive procurement channels that prioritize volume over premium bilateral pricing.
2.
Aggregated buyer power: Large purchasing bodies use centralized acquisitions to streamline administrative complexity. These joint procedures typically attract a higher number of bids, which intensifies pricing pressure on defense suppliers. This means that although suppliers gain higher volume visibility, the heightened competitive environment prevents them from holding premium prices.
3.
Bilateral premiums versus agency discounts: National bilateral awards historically allowed defense contractors to negotiate customized terms with high margins. However, central purchasing bodies aggregate demand to leverage massive scale and demand significant unit price discounts. According to Deutsche Bank, agency tenders are highly price-competitive, which means they act as a direct headwind to operating margin expansion compared to bilateral country contracts.
4.
Inefficient historical precedents: Collaborative procurement frameworks have historically led to increased development costs and delivery delays. For example, the multinational development of the Eurofighter Typhoon by the UK, Germany, Italy, and Spain was plagued by the juste retour principle, which allocated work based on funding contributions. This political compromise resulted in highly inefficient manufacturing setups and elevated unit costs compared to single-nation programs.
5.
Interoperability and standardization goals: Joint procurement is heavily incentivized by European policymakers to reduce military fragmentation. European Defence Agency has established targets where up to 35% to 40% of equipment spending should occur through coordinated collaborative purchases to ensure cross-border standardization. This push encourages multi-national programs but forces manufacturers to accept lower, standardized pricing structures across participating armed forces.

Section 3 – Supply Chain Scarcity: Tube Lead Times and Exosens Dynamics

Section summary
  1. Extreme scarcity of image intensifier tubes restricts night-vision device production and drives massive price increases.
  2. Theon secures its component pipeline and protects margins through strategic equity investments and long-term supply agreements.
  3. Low manufacturing wages in Greece provide Theon with a significant structural cost advantage over Western European competitors.
  4. Dynamic indexation terms protect peer margins from material inflation, whereas Theon faces contractual rigidity on multi-year frameworks.
  5. Expanding production outside of Greece and escalating headcount will partially compress future gross margins unless offset by high-margin software.

Extreme Scarcity and Lead Times

1.
Severe tube bottlenecks: Long lead times for critical components restrict production capabilities across the night-vision industry. A former regional manager at Theon noted on 1 April 2026 that customers face an 18-month waiting period for image intensifier tubes. Theon remains vulnerable to these supply constraints because tubes represent approximately 60% to 70% of the value of a finished night-vision device. Consequently, tube scarcity limits immediate shipment volumes, making secure component access the most decisive factor for generating future earnings.
2.
Device price inflation: Nightvision system prices have experienced unprecedented increases due to persistent component scarcity. A former regional manager at Theon reported on 1 April 2026 that night-vision device prices grew 2.5x to 3.0x between 2022 and 2026. This pricing surge was driven by extreme demand outstripping global manufacturing capacity, which allowed integrators to elevate their contract values. This upward pricing trend has directly boosted industry-wide gross margins since the start of the Ukraine war.
3.
Component price pass- through: The direct contribution of tube pricing to overall device margins remains highly dependent on contract structures. Deutsche Bank Research reported on 1 October 2025 that image intensifier tubes account for 60% to 65% of device costs. While integrators passed these rising component costs onto end customers, they successfully preserved a healthy 31% to 32% gross margin on night-vision goggles. This indicates that device price increases reflect both underlying component cost pass-through and real margin expansion.
