Section 1 – The Key Question and Why It Matters

Section summary
  1. The resolution of India's bulk Scotch tariff dispute will determine whether premium spirits manufacturers can retain substantial import duty savings or face retroactive back-duties.

The Central Inquiry and Significance

1.
Crucial question: "How is India's customs dispute over the classification of bulk Scotch imports (the tariff applied under heading 2106 against the 75% rate in the India-UK trade agreement) likely to be resolved and when, what share of Pernod Ricard India's cost of goods and of its blended Indian-made foreign liquor brands depends on bulk Scotch, and what do the disclosures of United Spirits, Radico Khaitan, Allied Blenders and Tilaknagar show about the cost and margin effect of each outcome?"
2.
Severe regulatory challenges emerged as Indian tax authorities initiated investigations into whether bulk Scotch imports qualify for the lower 75% tariff under the new trade pact. The regulatory review focuses on the potential misclassification of bulk Scotch imports to avail lower duties under the trade agreement. If the lower classification is disallowed, spirits manufacturers will face significant upward pressure on import costs, which directly threatens their projected premiumisation-led margin expansion.

Section 2 – India's Customs Dispute and Potential Resolutions

Section summary
  1. The classification dispute on bulk Scotch imports threatens to permanently double basic customs duties if raw concentrates are categorized as food preparations instead of spirits.
  2. The Ministry of Commerce and the Finance Ministry are actively seeking a technical solution to respect free trade commitments while complying with historical judicial precedents.

Customs Tariffs and Classification Conflict

1.
Conflicting tariff codes: Indian customs officials have disrupted bilateral trade concessions by seeking to reclassify bulk Scotch imports under a newly introduced food preparations category. According to the Global Trade Research Initiative, the Directorate of Revenue Intelligence directed customs in November 2026 to shift shipments from Heading 2208 to code HS 21069051. This technical reclassification targets bulk Scotch concentrates that are subsequently diluted and blended in India rather than sold directly. Importers argue that the liquid remains authentic Scotch whisky regardless of its packaging or alcoholic strength. and Finance: Policy Changes (India) 48 classification shift threatens to disrupt the premium spirits supply chain across the domestic market.
2.
Escalating duty burdens: The proposed reclassification of imported bulk spirits from Heading 2208 to Heading 2106 leads to an immediate doubling of basic import tariffs. The Global Trade Research Initiative reported on November 25, 2026, that classifying bulk Scotch under HS 21069051 raises the basic customs duty from 75% back to 150%. Once a 5% consumption levy is calculated on the combined import value and duty, the total tax burden rises to 162.5% of the shipment's value. This effective rate represents a higher duty than the 150% tariff that applied before the trade pact took effect. Consequently, the tariff concession is completely negated for bulk importers.
3.
Contrary evidence: The Commerce Ministry has clarified that the current cargo hold-up is a narrow administrative issue rather than a rollback of trade agreement commitments. Commerce Secretary Rajesh Agarwal stated on Monday that India remains fully committed to its trade obligations under the bilateral trade pact. Negotiators clarified that there is no pullback on tariff concessions, and the government is actively seeking a resolution with the Finance Ministry. British importers expressed satisfaction with the ongoing engagements and the proposed path forward. Therefore, the long-term trade benefits may remain intact despite the administrative friction.
4.
Historic legal precedents: The legal friction over bulk spirit imports is rooted in longstanding judicial battles between the Indian Revenue Department and domestic importers. Commerce Secretary Rajesh Agarwal noted that the current dispute stems from a Bombay High Court judgment in 1991 and a Supreme Court ruling in 2004 regarding tariff lines. These judicial decisions supported the industry's right to import bulk Scotch under the residual category HS 220890 instead of code HS 220830. This precedent allowed importers to bypass the Revenue Department's preferred classification for decades, but it has now created unexpected tariff anomalies under the new trade pact.

Enforcement Actions and Port Delays

5.
Severe port blockages: The immediate consequence of the customs reclassification dispute is the physical detention of Scotch shipments across major Indian entry points. News reports on November 24, 2026, confirmed that more than 80 containers belonging to Pernod Ricard, Diageo, and Bacardi remain stranded at ports. These stranded shipments represent thousands of liters of premium bulk spirits intended for the holiday production season. Because bulk imports represent 80% of all UK Scotch exports to India, the port blockade poses an immediate threat to manufacturing timelines. Disrupted shipments could severely restrict the blending operations of domestic brands.
6.
Arduous release conditions: Importers seeking the release of their seized bulk Scotch must comply with high financial demands from customs authorities. News reports on November 24, 2026, revealed that provisional release requires importers to pay 75% of the goods' value upfront to the customs department. Additionally, companies must provide bank guarantees covering the remaining disputed tariff balance while investigations continue. These bank guarantees put immediate pressure on the liquid funds of spirits manufacturers. This financial requirement acts as a major cash drain on the industry.
7.
Elevated peer exposure: The impact of the customs crackdown varies significantly among major listed alcoholic beverage companies in India. Nuvama Institutional Equities reported on November 13, 2026, that Tilaknagar Industries disclosed a seizure of goods worth 301 million Indian rupees during the DRI probe. In contrast, United Spirits reported a much lower seizure value of 64.50 million rupees. Both companies maintained that the port actions would not have a material impact on their immediate business operations. However, the highly unequal seizure values highlight the varying degrees of regulatory risk across peers.
8.
Strained corporate liquidity: The financial requirements for provisional cargo release pose an asymmetrical threat to smaller domestic spirits manufacturers. Industry observers noted that Pernod Ricard and Diageo have started arranging the necessary funds and bank guarantees to secure their shipments. However, smaller domestic manufacturers face greater pressure on their working capital because of their limited balance sheet strength. The requirement to lock up capital in bank guarantees could restrict their operational flexibility. Consequently, smaller players may face production delays that larger multinationals can easily avoid.

Regulatory Positions and Expected Timelines

9.
Divergent ministerial views: The path to resolving the tariff dispute is complicated by conflicting priorities within the Indian federal government. Jefferies reported on November 11, 2026, that while the Commerce Ministry has assured the industry it would honor the trade pact, the Finance Ministry appears to be demanding the 150% tariff on bulk imports. The Finance Ministry seeks to protect local tax revenues and classify bulk Scotch under code HS 2106. Conversely, the Commerce Ministry recognizes that bulk imports support domestic blending, bottling, and packaging jobs. Resolving these internal policy differences is crucial to restoring regulatory certainty.
10.
Prompt technical solution: The Indian government is moving rapidly to resolve the administrative friction and release stranded raw materials. Commerce Secretary Rajesh Agarwal announced on November 23, 2026, that the government expects to find a technical solution within days to satisfy both ministries. To prevent immediate disruptions, the Commerce Department has asked the Directorate of Revenue Intelligence to release seized consignments through standard customs processes. This intervention is designed to protect the domestic supply chain while a permanent tariff code alignment is finalized. Importers hope this stop-gap release will prevent severe production bottlenecks.

79 (India) Other side of the story: Even if the government issues a rapid technical fix, the risk of retroactive tax claims remains a major financial threat. Nuvama Institutional Equities warned on November 13, 2026, that a key risk is the possible back-duty on imports cleared at the 75% rate since July 15, 2026. If the higher 150% tariff is applied retroactively, it will completely erase the projected margins of blended whisky brands. Thus, a swift

Section 3 – Pernod Ricard India's Reliance on Bulk Scotch

Section summary
  1. Pernod Ricard India relies heavily on bulk Scotch imports to blend its premium domestic whiskies, which account for three-quarters of its net sales.
  2. Potential changes in bulk Scotch import tariffs directly threaten the operating margins of core Indian-made foreign liquor brands like Royal Stag and Blenders Pride.

Brand Portfolio and Scotch Reliance

1.
Elevated business exposure: Pernod Ricard India has built a highly lucrative premium spirits business, which the research analyzed using 15 relevant sources, 5 companies, and 2 historical cases. Out of these, 11 sources, 4 companies, and 1 precedent were used, while Brown-Forman was excluded because it lacks domestic Indian manufacturing operations. These local premium whiskies represent the largest driver of corporate sales across the Indian market. Consequently, any regulatory or tariff changes on bulk imports directly affect the company's cost structure and earnings.
2.
Dominant brand positioning: Pernod Ricard India's flagship local whiskies, Royal Stag and Blenders Pride, together contribute approximately 70% of the company's total domestic net sales. This extensive local market footprint has provided a solid financial platform for the company to expand its imported premium labels. To further streamline its business, the company recently divested its lower-margin Imperial Blue brand to focus entirely on its premium offerings. This portfolio rebalancing is estimated to be highly accretive to the company's Indian operating profit.

3.
Intense blending requirements: Pernod Ricard India blends its core Indian-made foreign liquor brands with imported bulk Scotch, making its cost structure highly dependent on international trade terms. These domestic blended whiskies represent approximately 75.7% of the company's total spirits sales by volume within India. While bottled-in-origin Scotch remains unaffected, approximately 80% of all Scotch imports arrive in India in bulk containers for local blending. Therefore, bulk Scotch remains the single most critical imported ingredient in maintaining the flavor profile of major local whisky lines.
4.
Contrary evidence: Some industry participants argue that the customs dispute will not permanently damage company margins because the local trade group expects a technical resolution from the commerce department. The Indian Commerce Ministry has assured British trade representatives that India remains fully committed to bilateral tariff concessions negotiated under CETA. Furthermore, current shipping blockages are being addressed through provisional cargo releases at ports to prevent immediate production halts. Consequently, the near-term volume disruption is expected to be resolved before causing permanent damage to corporate earnings.

5.
Superior operating profitability: Pernod Ricard India consistently generates superior operating profit margins compared to its domestic Indian distilling competitors. The company historically achieves a premium operating margin in the mid-20s percentage range, which sits significantly above local peers. This superior profitability benchmark is driven entirely by its premium focus on high-margin local whiskies and imported Scotch blends. In contrast, local peer Radico Khaitan reports much lower operating margins because of its higher exposure to lower-priced popular segments.
6.
Transformational asset acquisition: Pernod Ricard's current dominant market position in India stems from its strategic acquisition of Seagram's local business operations in 2001. This historical transaction allowed the French spirits major to expand its Indian net sales more than ten-fold over the subsequent two decades. The integration of Seagram's established domestic manufacturing infrastructure also provided the foundational blending and bottling facilities. This past precedent demonstrates that domestic blending operations remain the primary driver of the group's long-term organic growth in emerging markets.
7.
Volatile tariff implications: A permanent disallowance of the preferential 75% trade tariff on bulk Scotch imports would immediately increase raw material costs for Pernod Ricard India. Because the company banked trade benefits to expand margins rather than passing price cuts to consumers, higher import costs would squeeze profitability. If bulk imports are permanently taxed at the 150% rate, the resulting cogs inflation would contract operating margins across the core domestic portfolio. This margin compression would directly drag down consolidated earnings per share.
8.
Other side of the story: While higher customs duties on bulk Scotch represent a substantial cost headwind, the company's accelerating premiumisation and the recent divestment of lower-margin brands could offset the financial impact. The sale of the low-margin Imperial Blue brand is estimated to be pro forma 150 basis points accretive to Indian operating margins. Furthermore, robust double-digit growth in imported luxury brands like Jameson and Jameson Irish whiskey continues to shift the domestic revenue mix toward highly profitable categories. Therefore, structural premiumisation may successfully defend consolidated earnings even if bulk Scotch tariff concessions are completely lost.

Section 6 – If the Answer Differs from the Common View

Section summary
  1. A permanent locking in of the 150% tariff rate on bulk Scotch would compress spirits manufacturers' operating margins and impair projected earnings per share growth.
  2. High bulk Scotch import duties threaten to disrupt the domestic spirits supply chain and undermine the federal government's local manufacturing initiatives.

Impact on Margin and Earnings

1.
Deteriorating margin prospects: Pernod Ricard and domestic peers will face substantial gross margin compression if the Indian government permanently locks in the higher tariff rate. Ambit Capital reports that the ongoing bulk import dispute carries a negative earnings impact of 3% to 4% for major liquor manufacturers. Permanent exclusion of bulk imports will force companies to absorb the unexpected cost increases or implement steep retail price hikes. Consequently, this structural headwind would severely compress projected operating margins across the premium Indian spirits portfolio.
2.
Extreme portfolio vulnerability: Pernod Ricard India relies heavily on bulk Scotch imports because domestic Indian-made foreign liquor brands make up three-quarters of its total sales. Jefferies Equity Research explains that bulk Scotch is a critical raw material used to improve these high-volume local whiskies. A permanent tariff of 150% would immediately inflate raw material costs for these high-margin domestic lines. Therefore, the loss of negotiated trade concessions would directly impair consolidated earnings per share over the next three years.
3.
Adverse retroactive liabilities: Spirits manufacturers face substantial balance sheet risks from potential retroactive back-duties on imports cleared since July 2026. Nuvama Institutional Equities warns that tax authorities may levy these retroactive claims on shipments cleared at the lower 75% rate. These retroactive claims would instantly drain cash reserves and force companies to register massive contingent liabilities. This retrospective tax shock would severely damage near-term corporate liquidity and disrupt capital allocation plans.
4.
Negative pricing shifts: Spirits manufacturers will be forced to alter their pricing strategies if bulk Scotch concessions are permanently lost. Jefferies Research indicates that manufacturers have previously chosen to bank the tariff savings with higher margins rather than reducing IMFL retail prices. If higher import taxes are locked in, companies must decide between absorbing the severe cost inflation or raising retail prices. Implementing steep price hikes would risk undermining premium segment volume growth across the highly competitive Indian market.

Competitive Dynamics and Competitor Risks

5.
Uneven competitive impact: Smaller domestic manufacturers like Tilaknagar and Allied Blenders face far greater working capital strain than larger multinational competitors. News reports confirm that provisional cargo release requires paying 75% of the consignment value upfront alongside securing bank guarantees. These high financial requirements tie up valuable cash reserves and heavily strain corporate liquidity. Consequently, smaller players face severe production delays and operational bottlenecks that can erode their domestic market share.
6.
Shifted competitive advantage: A permanent bulk Scotch tariff would shift the competitive advantage toward players with strong premium non-whisky portfolios. Radico Khaitan continues to witness strong premiumisation momentum driven by its rapidly expanding luxury vodka and single malt portfolios. Systematix Institutional Research indicates that Radico's luxury products are positioned at higher price points that are less dependent on imported bulk Scotch. Therefore, non-whisky premiumisation would insulate certain domestic distillers from tariff shocks that severely impact blended whisky manufacturers.
7.
Severe inventory disruption: A prolonged tariff dispute threatens to cause long-term supply chain disruptions for blended spirits manufacturers. Jefferies Research warns that dozens of bulk containers remain held at ports, creating immediate production risks. Prolonged customs blockages will eventually exhaust existing raw material reserves and lead to widespread retail stockouts. Therefore, companies face severe supply shortages unless they arrange expensive bank guarantees to secure cargo releases.
8.
Contrary evidence: The Indian Commerce Ministry remains fully committed to honoring the bilateral trade agreement and resolving the customs dispute rapidly. News reports confirm that the Commerce Department has asked the Directorate of Revenue Intelligence to release seized consignments through standard processes. Furthermore, United Spirits management has stated that current port actions will not cause material operational disruptions to holiday production. Consequently, administrative friction may be resolved before causing any lasting damage to corporate profitability.

Government Relations and Domestic Manufacturing

9.
Strained bilateral trade: A permanent reversal of bulk Scotch tariff cuts would severely strain relations between multinational spirits manufacturers and the Indian government. Industry groups fear that excluding bulk Scotch from concessional rates risks undermining the core market-access benefits negotiated under the trade agreement. Jefferies Research indicates that bulk imports represent the vast majority of Scotch trade value, making it a critical component of bilateral agreements. Consequently, a negative resolution would damage investor confidence in future trade pacts and bilateral economic commitments.
10.
Undermined manufacturing goals: Excluding bulk Scotch imports from lower tariff rates directly contradicts India's domestic manufacturing objectives. The Confederation of Indian Alcoholic Beverage Companies warns that high bulk tariffs encourage importing finished bottled spirits over domestic value addition. This shift would harm local blending, bottling, and packaging jobs across the spirits supply chain. Therefore, a permanent high levy would undermine the federal Make in India initiative and damage local employment growth.
11.
(New Delhi) Other side of the story: While a permanent 150% tariff represents a serious margin risk, structural premiumisation and state-level excise reforms could successfully offset the negative impact. Jefferies Research reports that favorable tax rationalisation in key states like Karnataka has driven rapid volume acceleration and expanded pricing flexibility. Furthermore, the ongoing transition toward higher-margin premium segments like single malts provides a robust hedge against raw material inflation. Therefore, the long-term premiumisation trajectory could still defend overall corporate earnings development even under a worst-case customs outcome.
