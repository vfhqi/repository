[Materiality of] Investment Thesis (BB 1) Rating = D

Thesis. CVS Group is a capital-allocation case dressed as a regulatory-clearing case, and at 1221p it is not compelling. The regulator has gone and the multiple did not move, because the organic engine stalled and the chief executive is leaving; what remains is £70.0m of buybacks and £50.0m a year of acquisitions turning 2 percent organic growth into about 7 percent a year of adjusted earnings-per-share growth, which at 12.2x earnings pays about 8 percent a year against our 20 percent bar.

  • The change now acting on the company is internal and financial, not operational.
    • Three internal forces are strong on the framework's own test, and all three are uses of capital rather than changes to the operating engine.
    • £70.0m of buybacks in twelve months (£20.0m completed January 2026, £50.0m launched May 2026) retire about 8 percent of the market capitalisation of £832m at a 24-month-forward multiple of about 12x.
    • Acquisitions restarted on both fronts at about £50.0m a year: Australia at about 6x EBITDA (61 sites now, from 57 at June 2026) and the first UK deal since the regulatory pause, a two-site, nine-vet practice for an initial £15.0m.
    • Leverage deliberately raised to 1.63x net debt to bank-test EBITDA from 1.18x, against a guardrail of below 2.0x that management is willing to exceed temporarily, on a £350.0m facility refinanced to May 2030.
  • The external forces net to a headwind, and the one strong tailwind has already landed.
    • The Competition and Markets Authority's final decision (24 Mar 2026) and legally binding order (22 Sep 2026) brought no divestments and no service-price caps, which removed the force that could have broken the case
    • the shares are down 0.7 percent over twelve months regardless.
    • Demand is soft: like-for-like sales growth 2.1 percent in FY2026, about 1.5 percent in the second half, on flat-to-negative volumes
      • Healthy Pet Club membership fell 2 percent to 508,000 while subscription revenue rose 3.5 percent, so price is up and members are down.
    • Clinical labour, 34.3 percent of revenue, is short and inflating
      • total employment costs were 52.3 percent of revenue, and the April 2025 payroll-tax and wage legislation added about £11m a year that is now in the base.
    • The transparency remedies push medicine sales towards online pharmacies priced 50 to 60 percent below clinics
      • most of the chronic-medicine shift is already in the base, and fees of £473.1m against goods of £200.1m limit the exposure.
  • The change has travelled from choices through actions into the financial statements, and no further along the 'Six Layer Inputs-outputs' chain.
    • Revenue rose 5.9 percent and the diluted share count fell to 68.1m from 71.7m, but the key operating metrics (volumes, membership, cash conversion at 70.6 percent from 76.9 percent) have not turned, and the sell-side's estimates for FY2027 and FY2028 have drifted down 3.0 and 4.2 percent over twelve months.
  • 'Financial Outputs' are stable rather than improving.
    • Revenue growth is at its five-year trend, the adjusted EBITDA margin is flat at 19.9 percent against 20.0 percent, cash conversion fell and net debt rose
    • the one lever that lifts earnings per share in FY2027 is the buyback, and it is a one-off unless renewed.
  • What we think it earns: adjusted earnings per share of 85.9p in FY2026 rising to 93p in FY2027, 99p in FY2028 and 104p in FY2029, about 6.6 percent a year, against consensus of 95.4p, 104p and 111p, about 8.9 percent a year.
    • We sit 2 to 6 percent below consensus, inside the brokers' range, because the second-half run-rate makes consensus revenue growth of 4.9 percent for FY2027 a stretch, because interest rises as £50.0m a year of acquisitions is funded from a free cash flow that fell, because the consensus margin path to 20.7 percent by FY2029 assumes a pricing pass-through the transparency regime makes harder, and because consensus depreciation falling from £41.1m to £23.7m by FY2029 is a thin-coverage artefact we do not follow.
    • Roughly 2 percent a year of the growth comes from the share count and 2 percent from acquired revenue
      • the organic volume contribution is about zero and nothing comes from below EBIT.
  • What it costs: 1221p is 12.2x our 24-month-forward earnings per share of 100.25p, the two-year median of 12.09x for the post-CMA regime and the 29th percentile of the stock's five-year range whose median is 13.69x.
    • The shares are cheap against their own history and fair against their growth, for reasons we can name.
  • The 'Modal return' is about 7.6 percent a year over eighteen months and 6.8 percent a year over three years, an F against the framework's anchors.
    • The earnings growth carries the return, the multiple is the swing factor and the cash return is immaterial.
    • A cash return of about 1 percent a year, earnings growth of about 5.5 percent a year on the 24-month-forward measure, and a re-rating worth about 1.8 percent in total on a blended multiple of 12.4x against 12.2x now.
    • The bar is cleared only on consensus earnings at the five-year median multiple (20.8 percent a year) or on our earnings at the ten-year 35th-percentile multiple of 14.80x (21.0 percent a year)
      • each needs a re-rating we rate possible, not probable.
    • On the plausibly worse drop-through from EBITDA to earnings per share, the modal return falls from 7.6 to 3.0 percent a year, which shows how thin the earnings leg is beneath the buyback.
  • The pattern it fits is 'Major Corporate Change', at a C, because the input half is present and the output half is missing.
    • The clearing pattern a reader reaches for first has already been tested: the concern cleared on 24 Mar and 22 Sep 2026 and the multiple stayed at the two-year median, which says the market's concern is now the organic engine and the empty chair.
  • The chain holds at the inputs and breaks at the outputs, and the return is where it shows.
    • The case clears the bar only if consensus earnings are delivered and the multiple returns to 13.7x, a combination we put at about 16 percent, or if the price falls to about 1050p on our path.

  • The case therefore is real but not material at this price.

    • It costs nothing on the valuation to wait, because the two catalysts that could re-rate the shares, the H1 FY2027 like-for-like print on 4 Mar 2027 and the chief executive appointment, are dated and observable.

  • Rating = D, because the inputs are material ('Internal forces' at B, 'Change Forces' at C) but the outputs they produce are not improving ('Financial Outputs' at D) and the return they produce is below the bar ('Modal return' at F)

    • the parts bind at the output, and the whole is a D rather than an F because the downside is bounded by a low multiple and an accretive capital allocation, and the upside branch, though only possible, is identifiable and dated.
    • Not F, because an F says a bottom-quartile thesis, and a consolidator with a cleared regulator, a live buyback and a 6x acquisition runway is not that.
    • Not C, because a C needs a modal return near 13 percent a year, and there is no honest path to it at 1221p without a re-rating we cannot call probable.

Most plausible competing hypothesis, and our response.

  • The case against, at its strongest: the shares are a private-market arbitrage with a catalyst.
    • The whole group trades at about 7x EV/EBITDA (an enterprise value of £990m on FY2026 EBITDA of £142m; Deutsche Bank's 7.6x), below the 9x to 10x paid for UK practices, the 10x received for Crematoria in 2025 and the 6x CVS itself pays for sub-scale Australian clinics before synergies.
    • The buyback retires 8 percent of the equity at that multiple
      • the regulator is gone with no structural remedy, so UK acquisitions resume at reset vendor expectations
      • Australia compounds from about £20m of EBITDA towards the £105m to £135m+ the company and Deutsche Bank describe.
    • The subscription base (£95.5m, +3.5 percent) and referral demand are defensive
      • seven of nine brokers say Buy with a median target of £16.00, 29 percent above the price
      • the three-year earnings-per-share decline was three external shocks (cyber, regulator, payroll tax), all past.
    • On consensus earnings per share (+11 percent, +9 percent, +7 percent) and a re-rating to 14x to 15x, the return is 25 percent a year, an A.
      • Converium Capital made this case in May 2026, and the board's £50.0m buyback half-conceded it.
  • What we concede.
    • The EV/EBITDA discount to transaction multiples is real
    • the buyback is accretive at 12x earnings
    • the regulator's outcome was the best plausible one
    • Australia's runway is long and its entry economics are good
    • the downside on the multiple is bounded at about 11 to 22 percent with the regulator settled
    • and the sell-side is on the other side of us.
  • The cover test, shown.
    • The claim inside the bull case is that the Veterinary Practices division alone covers the enterprise value, and it holds only at UK transaction multiples.
    • Divisional EBITDA on the brokers' adjusted basis is consensus £149m for FY2027 before the intercompany and central charge of £11.2m, so £138m net, set against an enterprise-value multiple on EBITDA, like for like.
    • At 6x, the lowest multiple in the comparison, £138m gives £828m, 84 percent of the £990m enterprise value: not covered.
      • Stripping the R&D tax credits inside adjusted EBITDA (£15.1m in FY2025), £123m at 6x gives £738m, 75 percent: not covered.
    • At 9x, Jefferies' historical range for UK practices, £138m gives £1,242m, 125 percent: covered, with about £250m to spare
      • at the 10x of the Crematoria sale, 139 percent.
    • On a post-tax basis for the whole company, our FY2027 adjusted net income of about £62.5m against a market capitalisation of £832m is 13.3x
      • at Pets at Home's 10.9x calendar-2026 price-to-earnings ratio the equity would be worth £681m, 82 percent of today's: not covered.
    • So the cover is a bound only if a 423-practice UK estate would fetch what a four-vet practice fetches from a corporate buyer, which a two-year acquisition pause and a 30 percent local share cap make untestable in the horizon.
  • Why we still hold our view, first: the EV/EBITDA cheapness does not convert into price-to-earnings cheapness.
    • About 40 percent of EBITDA leaks to depreciation and interest before adjusted profit (adjusted profit before tax £84.9m on EBITDA £141.5m) and a further 60 percent of that to the reported line (£32.0m), so at 12.2x adjusted earnings for 7 percent growth the shares are fair, not cheap, and the reported price-to-earnings ratio is about 50x.
  • Second: the shocks may have been external, but the three-year record includes them because the model is exposed to them.
    • Labour is 52.3 percent of revenue and legislated, the regulator can return, and the acquired growth carries contingent consideration the adjusted line excludes (£8.9m of business combination costs in one half).
  • Third: the market has had six months since the final decision and three days since the order to close the private-market gap, and did not.
    • It fell 6.2 percent on the results instead.
  • Fourth: the bull's return needs both consensus earnings and a 14x to 15x multiple, and we put the joint odds at about 16 percent, unlikely.
    • Consensus earnings delivered at 45 percent, plausible, since our path is 2 to 6 percent below
    • times the five-year-median-or-better branch at 35 percent, possible
    • the two being close to independent.
  • What would make us wrong quickly: a chief executive the market rates, named before the H1 FY2027 print, with the print at 3 percent or better
    • in that branch the shares are at 1485p to 1600p within eighteen months and we would have been neutral on a 15 to 21 percent-a-year stock.
