Advance mechanics and working-capital movements Bravida’s FY2025 operating cash flow was SEK1,453 million, compared with SEK1,896 million in FY2024, while working capital consumed SEK359 million, mainly because the net balance of contract assets and contract liabilities deteriorated and trade payables declined. Contract assets were SEK2,310 million at December 2025, against contract and similar liabilities of SEK3,174 million, so the reported liability surplus was approximately SEK864 million.

This balance is not equivalent to unrestricted cash because contract liabilities include advances for work that still has to be performed, whereas contract assets represent revenue earned before Bravida has an unconditional right to invoice.

Bravida’s June 2026 balance sheet showed trade receivables of SEK6,027 million, contract assets of SEK2,935 million and contract and similar liabilities of SEK3,324 million. Compared with December 2025, receivables increased SEK256 million and contract assets increased SEK625 million, while contract liabilities increased only SEK150 million, implying a calculated deterioration of approximately SEK731 million in the combined contractasset and receivable position relative to advances. The H1 2026 cash-flow statement is consistent with that interpretation, showing SEK662 million of cash absorbed by trade and other operating receivables and SEK409 million of total working-capital consumption.

Management described the underlying mechanism directly in Bravida’s Q2 2026 earnings call of 13 July 2026, stating that normal advances are typically 5% to 15% of contract value, are larger in absolute terms on large projects, and decline as work is performed. On the approximately SEK4.3 billion Green Mountain assignment, a 5% to 15% advance would equate to roughly SEK215 million to SEK645 million if applied to the full disclosed order value, although Bravida has not confirmed that such an advance exists or that the percentage applies to this contract.

The same calculation would imply a possible advance range of approximately SEK110 million to SEK330 million on the SEK2.2 billion atNorth contract, but this is an analytical sensitivity rather than a disclosed project cash balance.

DNB Carnegie’s 14 July 2026 Q2 review expects good H2 cash inflow from prepayments and resolved disputes, but explicitly warns that greater exposure to large contracts should increase cash-flow volatility. Its view supports a likely near-term cash tailwind from project advances, but it does not provide a FY2027 operating-cashflow number or disclose how much of the Green Mountain, atNorth or EcoDataCenter advances is consumed in each forecast year.

Therefore, the evidence supports a directional cash-flow pattern rather than a broker consensus range for FY2027 operating cash flow. Base hypothesis: Bravida delivers the identified Green Mountain, atNorth and EcoDataCenter work broadly on schedule, achieves a data-centre margin above the core business, and restores the wider group toward DNB Carnegie’s FY2027 and FY2028 margin assumptions. This case is supported by Bravida’s order backlog of SEK20,372 million, which was up 25% year on year in the second quarter of 2026, and by 44% year-on-year orderintake growth. Confidence is medium rather than high because Bravida has not disclosed contract-level margins, payment schedules or secured replacement awards after the named projects complete.
3.
Upside hypothesis: Bravida uses the current projects to win follow-on phases, recurring maintenance and additional technical work, allowing data-centre revenue to remain material after 2028 even as individual construction assignments finish. The commercial logic is credible because EcoDataCenter has already used Bravida across eight facilities and Bravida has prior experience with Meta-related data-centre work, although no post-2028 service run-rate has been disclosed. The upside therefore depends more on customer retention and repeat awards than on simply completing the present backlog. How the bear case reaches EPS Margin slippage is the most direct earnings channel because DNB Carnegie models data-centre revenue at a 10% EBITA margin and the rest of Bravida at 7% in FY2027 and FY2028. If data-centre revenue were SEK2,400 million in FY2027, as DNB Carnegie assumes, a five-percentage-point margin shortfall would reduce data-centre EBITA by approximately SEK120 million, calculated from the broker’s segment scenario. The effect on group EPS would be smaller than the effect of a group-wide margin miss, but it could still be material because the datacentre contribution is intended to support both earnings growth and the premium valuation multiple. Revenue risk is more likely to arise through timing than cancellation, because delays in permitting, grid connection, procurement or commissioning can push work into later periods without immediately eliminating the underlying customer requirement. Dell’Oro said on 14 October 2026 that power, long-lead equipment and community opposition were stretching project timelines, and that siting and permitting had become the most serious near-term brake on data-centre expansion. For Bravida, a delay would defer revenue recognition and cash receipts while leaving labour and project-management costs in place, increasing the probability of negative operating leverage. The closest relevant construction precedent is Fasadgruppen’s first-quarter 2026 report, which showed that postponed project starts left the cost base in place and produced negative leverage. That precedent is not Bravida-specific and concerns building-envelope contracting rather than data-centre installations, but it demonstrates how schedule slippage can hurt margins even when demand has not permanently disappeared. The same mechanism would be more consequential for Bravida if large projects require dedicated white-collar teams and specialist subcontractors before revenue milestones are reached. Cash conversion and customer advances The cash downside can precede the EPS downside because Bravida’s management has described advances as temporary support that are consumed as work progresses. Bravida reported only SEK96 million of operating cash flow in the second quarter of 2026 and negative working-capital movement of SEK409 million in the first half, despite the large order intake and backlog. If Green Mountain and atNorth move through labour- and procurement-intensive stages before new milestone payments arrive, FY2027 operating cash flow could materially undershoot the positive cash-conversion narrative without an immediate equivalent reduction in reported EBITA. A relevant external precedent is Hexatronic’s third-quarter 2026 result, where operating cash flow was SEK228 million but an SEK88 million working-capital outflow arose mainly from higher receivables following strong sales. Hexatronic’s data-centre sales rose 107% in that quarter, yet management explicitly said seasonal effects and project timing made the period exceptionally strong and expected activity to normalize in the following quarter. The precedent supports caution against assuming that rapid data-centre revenue growth automatically produces proportionate cash generation for Bravida.

A cash shortfall would raise financing and valuation risk even if Bravida remained solvent, because weaker conversion could constrain dividends, acquisitions or the ability to mobilize replacement projects. The available evidence does not disclose Bravida’s FY2027 operating-cash-flow forecast, net-debt sensitivity or the amount of advances attached to each named contract, so no defensible precise cash-flow or leverage estimate can be made. Stakeholders able to change the outcome

Green Mountain, atNorth and EcoDataCenter have the strongest ability to change Bravida’s near-term revenue phasing because customer decisions on scope, commissioning, acceptance and follow-on awards determine when work is executed and whether service revenue follows construction. Developers also have an incentive to renegotiate timing or pricing if financing costs, power availability or tenant commitments weaken, particularly because data-centre projects require substantial upfront capital and reliable grid access.

Skanska, YIT, Caverion, Assemblin and other Nordic contractors can alter Bravida’s replacement-backlog probability by competing for new data-centre and infrastructure work. YIT’s DNB Carnegie coverage states that data-centre revenue had grown from almost zero to approximately 17% of contracting-segment revenue on an LTM basis by the second quarter of 2026, but also warns that grid constraints, permitting delays, weaker AI demand and local labour availability could disrupt future awards.

This indicates that the opportunity is large but competition for technically credible contractors is also likely to intensify. Suppliers and subcontractors can shift profitability through availability, price and delivery performance, because compressed construction schedules magnify the effect of a late transformer, cooling system, connector or specialist trade. Onnec’s August 2026 research found that 92% of operators were being forced to compress build timelines and 75% were making design decisions before infrastructure requirements were fully understood.

Those conditions increase the risk that Bravida bears unrecovered redesign, overtime, rework or commissioning costs. Regulators and grid operators can slow the outcome without opposing data-centre investment in principle, because licensing, environmental review, power allocation and community acceptance govern whether projects reach construction. The European Investment Bank’s 2025/2026 investment report identifies Sweden as having more than 5GW of pipeline projects, while it also describes grid-connection lead times as the fundamental bottleneck and notes that Nordic demand is competing with broader electricity-system constraints.

Indicators that would decide the scenarios The first decisive indicator is whether Bravida’s quarterly adjusted EBITA margin improves independently of exceptional items and whether data-centre activity earns more than the core business. A margin remaining near the 5.9% adjusted second-quarter benchmark would challenge the assumed 7% non-data-centre margin, while a sustained margin above that level would support the recovery case.

The second indicator is cash conversion after advances have been consumed rather than when they are received. Repeated working-capital outflows, rising receivables or falling contract liabilities would support the bear hypothesis, whereas operating cash flow returning toward Bravida’s longer-run approximately 100% conversion would support the base case. The third indicator is replacement order intake before EcoDataCenter’s current work ends in the first quarter of 2028 and before Green Mountain approaches completion in 2028 or 2029.

New awards from the existing customers would support the upside case, while a slowing backlog-to-sales ratio or dependence on uncommitted pipeline would make a 2028–2029 installation gap more probable. The fourth indicator is the 2027 Swedish wage settlement and Bravida’s demonstrated ability to pass labour inflation through fixed-price or recently tendered work. A wage increase without corresponding price or productivity gains would reduce core EBITA, while stronger Swedish pricing and restructuring savings would weaken the bear case.

Contrary evidence: The strongest evidence against a severe downside scenario is that independent industry indicators continue to show strong data-centre demand, including ABB’s triple-digit data-centre order growth and Schneider Electric’s record backlog with visibility extending into 2027 and 2028. Schneider Electric also reported triple-digit data-centre demand in the third quarter of 2026 and raised its FY2026 growth and margin guidance, suggesting that the broader infrastructure cycle remains supportive even if individual Nordic projects are delayed. This evidence reduces the probability of a demand collapse, but it does not resolve Bravida’s contract-level margin, cash-conversion or customer-concentration risks. side of the story: The common view could prove conservative if Bravida’s secured projects generate reliable margin, customer advances are replaced by milestone receipts, and repeat work arrives before the current backlog rolls off. Strong Nordic power demand, continuing data-centre investment and Bravida’s established customer relationships make that outcome plausible, while the principal unresolved question is whether the company can convert sector growth into durable group EBITA and cash flow rather than a temporary project peak.
