Section 2 – Maersk Historical Working Capital Behavior

Section summary
  1. Maersk historically managed its working capital proactively to support operating cash flows during cyclical downturns.
  2. Cash releases from trade receivables frequently offset inventory build-ups and trade payable reductions when freight rates collapsed.

1.
Significant relief: A.P. Møller - Mærsk experienced a severe economic contraction during the 2009 financial crisis as container freight rates plummeted. To support liquidity, Maersk achieved a substantial USD 565 million reduction in working capital in its container shipping segment. This working capital release was highly beneficial because the container segment recorded a massive USD 2.1 billion loss for the year.
2.

However, the company faced cash outflows from inventories of DKK 1.37 billion as slow steaming and reduced network speed lengthened transit times and increased stockpiles. These opposing forces meant that cash generation was highly reliant on aggressive customer collection and payment deferral.
3.
Vessel speed reductions: Maersk implemented wide-scale slow steaming in 2009 to take out approximately 5% of its operating capacity. This operational shift saved significant amounts of bunker fuel and lowered operating expenses. However, slow steaming also required careful coordination to prevent customer service issues during the sudden pickup of activity in late 2009.
4.
Bunker cost relief: Maersk benefited from a 43% lower average fuel price in the first nine months of 2009 compared to the same period of 2008. This price decline reduced Maersk's total fuel costs substantially and eased working capital requirements. Even though fuel prices rose sequentially in the third quarter of 2009, the introduction of super slow steaming protected operating cash flows.

This cash generation remained solid despite a 2% decline in USD revenues caused by falling container freight rates. To mitigate the decline, Maersk aggressively pursued super slow steaming to reduce vessel speed and lower bunker fuel consumption.
6.

This initiative helped counter the high bunker fuel prices that acted as a major financial headwind. These efficiency gains ensured that total bunker costs fell by 1% even as average fuel prices rose by 7% during the year.

This cash absorption demonstrates that working capital dynamics can vary significantly across Maersk's diverse business segments.

Section 3 – FactSet Quarterly vs Year-End Working Capital Discrepancies

Section summary
  1. Accounting classification variances and seasonal shipping cycles explain the discrepancy between FactSet quarterly metrics and Maersk year-end reports.
  2. The inclusion of long-term deposits within interim receivables represents the largest quantitative distortion in quarterly working capital calculations.

Balance Sheet Reclassifications

Significant classification mismatches occur because Maersk includes cash-like assets within its interim operating metrics. Note 2 of the third-quarter 2026 report states that the receivables line of USD 23.6 billion includes

1.
USD 12.9 billion of term deposits with maturities over three months. FactSet often includes this entire balance in operating working capital, which severely inflates quarterly net working capital. This distortion disappears in audited year-end statements where these deposits are correctly grouped with liquidity reserves, which alters perceived earnings per share trends.
2.
Structural reporting adjustments also alter historical receivables and liabilities. Maersk reported in its 2024 annual report that contract liabilities, which were previously offset directly against trade receivables, were restated and presented separately as liabilities. This change modified the balance sheet presentation of contract assets and liabilities of USD 352 million and USD 748 million respectively. Such retrospective restatements create significant retrospective discrepancies in FactSet databases because quarterly historical runs are not always updated in sync with year-end adjustments.
3.
Audit verification differences arise because interim financial statements are prepared under less stringent review procedures. Maersk noted in its third-quarter 2026 interim report that its condensed financials have not been audited or reviewed by independent auditors. While these figures comply with IAS 34, year-end audits frequently force the reclassification of accrued liabilities. This lack of interim auditing can lead to sudden corrections in the fourth quarter, which impacts the accuracy of quarterly cash flow projections.

Seasonal Patterns and JV Impact

4.
High seasonal volatility characterizes Maersk's working capital cycle because of peak third-quarter shipping volumes. Maersk's chief financial officer noted during a historical earnings call that receivables and inventories build up significantly during the summer peak before unwinding rapidly in the fourth quarter. FactSet's quarterly annualized calculations often miss this cyclicality, projecting an unsustainable cash drain into the next year. This seasonal noise distorts quarterly cash conversion ratios until year-end reports provide a stabilized view of operational cash flow.
5.

APM Terminals and Ocean segment partners like the Gemini Cooperation utilize equity-accounting, where Maersk only records its share of profit in joint ventures. This accounting treatment means that terminal operational balance sheets are not consolidated line-by-line. FactSet's database can mischaracterize these investments, leading to discrepancies when comparing the parent company's operational working capital with the group's consolidated year-end reports.
6.
Contrary evidence: Some analysts argue that quarterly discrepancies are caused primarily by manual data collection errors rather than structural classification issues. However, Maersk's third-quarter 2026 balance sheet confirms that the inclusion of USD 12.9 billion in term deposits within interim receivables is a deliberate financial reporting policy. This structural allocation explains the bulk of the USD 5.0 billion variance between quarterly and year-end net working capital. Investors must account for this deliberate accounting treatment when modeling Maersk's liquidity.

Substantial evaluation risks exist for investors who rely blindly on unadjusted quarterly net working capital figures. The systematic inflation of interim receivables by over USD 12 billion can make Maersk's operations appear highly cash-absorbent during down-cycles. Adjusting for these non-operational term deposits reveals a far more resilient underlying cash conversion. Resolving these FactSet data discrepancies is critical because accurate working capital projections directly impact earnings per share valuations and dividend safety assessments.

The most significant gaps in the evidence base center on three critical dimensions that prevent investors from building fully predictive models of future working capital behavior. First, quarterly segment-level working capital movements remain undisclosed for all three carriers across the historical down-cycle periods examined. While the analysis identified that Maersk's interim receivables include USD 12.9 billion of term deposits with maturities over three months, which FactSet often includes in operating working capital and severely inflates quarterly net working capital, the underlying quarterly movements in trade receivables, bunker inventories, and trade payables for individual business segments such as Ocean, Logistics & Services, or Terminals are not separately reported in interim statements.

This gap means investors cannot isolate whether working capital releases originated primarily from the container shipping operations or from non-Ocean activities with fundamentally different cash conversion dynamics. Second, intra-quarter timing effects and seasonal patterns are documented only anecdotally rather than systematically. Maersk's chief financial officer noted during a historical earnings call that receivables and inventories build up significantly during the summer peak before unwinding rapidly in the fourth quarter, and FactSet's quarterly annualized calculations often miss this cyclicality, projecting an unsustainable cash drain into the next year.

However, the precise magnitude of these seasonal swings across different trade lanes and service types remains unmapped. Without monthly or bi-monthly working capital snapshots, investors cannot distinguish between structural working capital absorption that signals deteriorating cash conversion and temporary seasonal bulges that reverse within the same fiscal year. This ambiguity is particularly problematic when modeling near-term liquidity stress during rapid freight rate normalizations.

Third, peer comparability is severely constrained by divergent accounting treatments and disclosure granularity. Hapag-Lloyd experienced a unique working capital drain in 2016 due to the utilization of CSAV provisions, where the difference between reported EBITDA and operational cash flow was driven by the utilization of provisions for disadvantageous contracts, which absorbed operational cash rather than releasing it and suppressed overall operating cash flow conversion during the downturn.

Similarly, COSCO Shipping Holdings generated positive operating cash flow of RMB 6.717 billion in the 2015 down-cycle, which was heavily supported by government subsidies for vessel demolition and replacement, introducing non-commercial cash flows that distort direct comparisons with Maersk's purely operational working capital releases. The absence of standardized segmentlevel working capital reporting across International Financial Reporting Standards and Chinese Accounting Standards makes it impossible to conduct apples-to-apples comparisons of inventory management efficiency, customer payment terms, or supplier financing strategies.

Closing these evidence gaps would require several concrete disclosure enhancements from the carriers and improved data collection by financial information providers. First, quarterly segment-level cash flow statements that separately report working capital movements for Ocean, Logistics, Terminals, and other divisions would enable investors to isolate the true cash generation capacity of core container shipping operations during downturns.

These disclosures should break out trade receivables, inventories (including bunker fuel stocks), and trade payables at the segment level, allowing analysis of whether specific business units absorb or release cash during rate collapses. Second, carriers should provide monthly working capital metrics or at minimum bi-monthly snapshots during periods of extreme market volatility, similar to the enhanced disclosure practices adopted by some capital-intensive industries during financial stress. This granularity would eliminate the distortions caused by intra-quarter seasonal peaks and provide early warning signals of deteriorating cash conversion before yearend results are published. Third, standardized reconciliation schedules between interim and audited year-end working capital figures should be mandated, explicitly identifying the impact of term deposit reclassifications, joint venture consolidation adjustments, and audit corrections. Maersk reported in its 2024 annual report that contract liabilities, which were previously offset directly against trade receivables, were restated and presented separately as liabilities, modifying the balance sheet presentation of contract assets and liabilities of USD 352 million and USD 748 million respectively, and such retrospective restatements create significant retrospective discrepancies in FactSet databases.

Requiring carriers to publish bridge tables that explain quarter-to-quarter and year-end adjustments would dramatically improve the reliability of quarterly working capital analysis. Fourth, financial data providers like FactSet should implement automated flags that identify when interim receivables include nonoperating cash equivalents such as term deposits, enabling users to construct adjusted operating working capital metrics that reflect true operational cash absorption or release.

Finally, peer-comparable disclosures of bunker hedging positions, customer payment term structures, and supplier financing arrangements would enable investors to understand why working capital behaves differently across carriers during identical market conditions. Hapag-Lloyd utilized systematic bunker hedging up to a maximum of 80% of its bunker costs to protect its operating cash flows, providing cost predictability compared to Maersk's unhedged spot consumption approach, though this structure limited near-term fuel cost savings when crude oil prices crashed unexpectedly. Understanding whether structural hedging policies, trade lane mix differences, or customer contract terms drive divergent working capital outcomes is essential for predicting which carriers will demonstrate superior cash resilience in future down-cycles. Without these enhanced disclosures, investors must continue to rely on imperfect proxies and incomplete retrospective data that may not accurately predict working capital behavior when the next severe freight rate collapse inevitably arrives.

Section 7 – If the Answer Differs from the Common View

Section summary
  1. If Maersk's working capital behaves worse than consensus expectations in future down-cycles, earnings per share and operating cash flow will deteriorate rapidly, forcing painful choices between capital returns, capital expenditure, and balance sheet strength. The power to shift these outcomes rests primarily with container shipping customers who control payment timing, bunker suppliers who dictate fuel pricing and credit terms, and logistics competitors who can accelerate or decelerate competitive intensity. The consensus view embedded in current analyst forecasts assumes that Maersk can consistently achieve parity between cash flow from operating activities and EBITDA through net working capital releases during downturns, as demonstrated in 2023 when the company recorded USD 9.6 billion in both operating cash flow and EBITDA, supported by a USD 417 million net working capital release. However, this historical pattern may not hold if structural changes in customer payment behavior, bunker fuel market dynamics, or competitive positioning fundamentally alter the working capital cycle during the next severe freight rate collapse.

The first divergent scenario centers on trade receivables behavior. Historical down-cycles demonstrate that Maersk experienced massive receivables contractions that released USD 1.87 billion in cash during 2023 as freight rates fell by 50% on average. If customers extend payment terms aggressively in the next downturn or if default rates rise materially, this receivables release mechanism will fail. Container shipping customers possess substantial negotiating power during severe overcapacity periods because carriers compete desperately for volume. Large beneficial cargo owners such as retail chains, automotive manufacturers, and electronics producers can demand extended credit terms as a condition for maintaining shipping volumes.

If receivables fail to contract proportionally with falling revenues, Maersk's operating cash flow will undershoot EBITDA by billions of dollars, directly pressuring earnings per share through either higher net interest expenses on increased borrowing or dilutive equity issuance.

The second divergent scenario involves bunker inventory dynamics and supplier credit. Maersk accumulated USD 300 million in fuel inventories in the fourth quarter of 2019 to secure low-sulfur fuel ahead of IMO 2020, creating a working capital headwind that was mitigated by high cash conversion elsewhere. In future downcycles, if bunker suppliers tighten credit terms and demand faster payment while fuel price volatility increases, Maersk will face simultaneous cash absorption from both higher inventory levels and accelerated payables.

Bunker suppliers possess concentrated market power, particularly in key bunkering hubs like Singapore, Rotterdam, and Houston. If these suppliers perceive rising credit risk during shipping distress, they can unilaterally shorten payment windows from typical thirty-day terms to cash-on-delivery or advance payment structures. This supplier behavior would force Maersk to maintain larger fuel inventory buffers and accelerate cash outflows, potentially absorbing several hundred million dollars of additional working capital precisely when freight revenues are collapsing. - Earnings Calls The third divergent scenario concerns trade payables compression.

If trade payables decrease faster than trade receivables, cash is absorbed rather than released, leading to diminished earnings per share due to rising net interest expenses. Consensus models typically assume that Maersk can stretch vendor payments during downturns to preserve cash. However, if logistics competitors face simultaneous distress, suppliers across the container shipping ecosystem may demand faster payment from all carriers to protect their own liquidity.

Terminal operators, equipment lessors, stevedoring companies, and port authorities all face correlated revenue pressures when shipping volumes decline. These vendors may collectively tighten payment terms industry-wide rather than accepting extended receivables from financially stressed carriers. If this occurs, Maersk will lose its traditional ability to defer payments, forcing the company to fund working capital absorption through debt drawdowns or asset sales.

The competitive dimension introduces additional complexity. The initiation of the Gemini Cooperation between Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd in February 2025 reorganizes transport networks and routes, which could alter historical working capital patterns permanently by releasing capacity but also introducing new operational complexities. If the Gemini alliance fails to achieve anticipated network efficiencies or if competing alliances like 2M or Ocean Alliance accelerate capacity rationalization faster than Gemini, Maersk's relative competitive position will weaken.

Hapag-Lloyd as Maersk's Gemini partner possesses significant power to influence outcomes because any operational miscoordination within the alliance will harm both carriers' working capital efficiency. Conversely, if Gemini achieves superior slot cost optimization and network speed compared to competitors, Maersk could sustain better freight rates and working capital conversion than industry averages, protecting earnings per share even during broader market weakness.

The structural overcapacity threat amplifies all these risks. Long-term vessel oversupply remains the primary threat to sector earnings, with continued deliveries of large containerships expected to compress freight rates back to historical break-even levels, meaning investors must track working capital resilience because normal operating cash flow will likely turn into cash burn soon. If working capital absorption accelerates while EBITDA collapses toward breakeven, Maersk will face severe cash burn that threatens its ability to maintain dividends and share buybacks.

Maersk's decision to suspend its share buy-back programme in late 2023 reflects management's concern over future cash flows, as working capital releases provided only short-term liquidity. If the next downturn proves deeper or longer than historical precedents, consensus expectations for sustained positive free cash flow will prove severely optimistic.
