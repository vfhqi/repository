Section 2 – Operating Cost Structures and Stacking Economics

Section summary
  1. A Norwegian-shelf semi-submersible operator's cost base is predominantly fixed during active operations, but stacking can reduce cash burn by up to ninety percent.
  2. Cold stacking delivers deeper daily savings than warm stacking, though it requires substantial upfront preservation capital and over twelve months of reactivation lead time.

Daily active rig operating costs

1.
Rig operating expenditures: Active harsh-environment semi-submersibles command premium daily operating cost levels because of high labor and maintenance requirements on the Norwegian Continental Shelf. Dolphin Drilling reported in August 2026 that its active moored semi Paul B. Loyd Jr. averaged $90,000 per day in operating expenditures. Meanwhile, its benign deepwater semi Blackford Dolphin reached $143,000 per day in the same period. These baseline expenses demonstrate that running active rigs requires substantial active daily cash generation to cover high regulatory and personnel costs.
2.
Variable cost distribution: Personnel expenses and technical maintenance represent the primary components of active rig daily running costs. A former Chief Financial Officer at Dolphin Drilling stated in January 2026 that typical active floater operating costs range from $125,000 to $150,000 per day when including local G&A and spares. Crew salaries and technical support make up the largest share of this active expenditure. Consequently, any reduction in active crew size immediately lowers daily operational cash requirements for the drilling contractor.
3.
Regional opex benchmarks: Direct operational spending varies widely by region due to differences in labor regulations and maritime insurance requirements. Deutsche Bank Research reported in March 2018 that Ocean Rig guided daily active operating costs of $130,000 in Norway, $140,000 in Brazil, and $110,000 in Angola. These figures exclude idle units and demonstrate the persistent high cost of operations in the North Sea. Therefore, offshore drillers face unfavourable regional cost inflation in highly regulated markets.

Warm stacking and layout economics

4.
Warm stacking opex: Warm stacking allows drilling contractors to idle a rig temporarily while maintaining the asset in a deployable state. Deutsche Bank Research noted in March 2018 that Transocean spent between $30,000 and $45,000 per day to warm-stack its core deepwater assets. This compared to normal daily active operating costs of $130,000 to $140,000 per day in the Gulf of Mexico. Consequently, warm stacking offers partial cost relief without sacrificing near-term dayrate opportunities.
5.
Dynamic stacking progression: Stacking expenditure can adjust dynamically as reactivation preparations commence and class renewal work gets underway. Dolphin Drilling reported in August 2026 that lay-up expenses for the Borgland Dolphin increased to $26,000 per day. This increase was planned due to class renewal and reactivation preparations. Thus, even warm-stacked assets experience escalating maintenance cost profiles prior to contract commencement.
6.
Warm stack timelines: Reactivating a warm-stacked rig is relatively fast but still requires significant lead time and unstacking capital before earning dayrates. Jefferies Research highlighted in January 2017 that a warmstacked asset requires three months lead-time to re-crew and up to $5 million in un-stacking costs. Rigs typically reach full operating cost levels approximately 45 days before going on hire. This means that warm stacking retains operational responsiveness but still presents upfront working capital drag.

Cold stacking cost reductions

7.
Cold stacking opex: Cold stacking provides a much deeper reduction in daily running costs by removing the offshore crew and placing the rig in layup. Seadrill reported in February 2025 that cold-stacked rigs run at approximately $5,000 per day. A former Vice President at Maersk Drilling confirmed in March 2023 that coldstacked floaters cost between $5,000 and $10,000 per day in harbor storage. Therefore, cold stacking preserves cash effectively during multi-year downturns.
8.
Heavy entry costs: The process of cold-stacking a drilling unit requires severe upfront capital for preservation work and crew severance. Seadrill's management stated in February 2025 that cold-stacking incurs a one-off cost of $6 million to $10 million depending on the location. These expenses cover technical preservation, towing, and contract terminations. Consequently, drilling contractors face a substantial cash outflow barrier when entering deep layup.
9.
Rig preservation methods: Optimization of cold stacking costs depends heavily on clustering assets and deploying remote telemetry monitoring systems. An expert call with a former Transocean Operations Manager in March 2026 revealed that Transocean clustered idle rigs in Greece to minimize costs. These vessels have telemetry onboard and are inspected by a rotating crew. This technique shows that digital monitoring reduces onshore support overheads during layup.

Reactivation capital and timelines

10.
Reactivation capital requirements: Bringing a cold-stacked semi-submersible back to active service requires significant capital expenditure that continues to rise. An independent oil and gas consultant estimated in March 2026 that cold-stacked reactivations now cost up to $150 million. This matches the $75 million to $150 million reactivation cost estimates provided by Transocean for its cold-stacked units in June 2025. Therefore, contractors face punitive reactivation capital requirements before generating revenue.
11.
Reactivation lead times: Reactivating long-term idle assets is further constrained by compounding equipment lead times and shipyard capacity bottlenecks. Morgan Stanley Research noted in July 2022 that idle asset reactivation requires at least 12 months to return to the active fleet. This long duration stems from supply chain disruptions and critical component delays. Thus, protracted timelines delay earnings contribution from stacked fleets.
12.
Reactivation failure risks: Stacking conditions and deferred maintenance can cause reactivation projects to suffer major budget overruns and operational failures. An expert call with a former Transocean Reliability Manager in March 2026 showed that reactivations average $50 million to $75 million. Many operators failed to properly invest in preservation during the down-cycle because cash flows were constrained. This means poorly preserved rigs face higher risk of expensive structural remediation.
