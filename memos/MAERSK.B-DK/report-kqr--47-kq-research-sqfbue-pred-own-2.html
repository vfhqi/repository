Section 2 – Historical Precedents of Container Shipping Downturns

Section summary
  1. Container shipping downturns in 2009, 2012 to 2016, and 2023 indicate that capacity rationalization is a lagged response to severe operating losses.
  2. Severe operating deficits historical precedents show that scrapping and idling only engage when carrier balance sheets face extreme cash drain.

The Great Recession Adjustments of 2009

1.
Severe financial distress: A.P. Møller - Mærsk experienced a massive container segment deficit of USD 2.1 billion during the 2009 global financial crisis. This severe earnings contraction occurred because container freight rates plunged 28% to an average of USD 2,370 per FFE. To mitigate these losses, the company scrapped eight older container vessels and reduced its time-charter capacity by approximately 9% net.
2.
Aggressive capacity containment: Hapag-Lloyd managed the 2009 shipping crisis by implementing coordinated capacity reductions within its grand alliance framework. The German carrier was in a favorable supply position because it had no new vessel deliveries scheduled over a period of eighteen months. This operating pause allowed the company to stabilize its cash flows by idling vessels rather than initiating premature demolition.
3.
Sudden market contraction: COSCO SHIPPING Holdings suffered a net loss attributable to equity holders of RMB 7.47 billion during the 2009 fiscal year. The global financial crisis severely impacted its main container shipping trade, dragging segment operating profits down to highly unprofitable levels. This heavy loss forced the carrier to rationalize its operating fleet through lay-ups and negotiations with leasing providers.
4.
Widespread industry idling: Container shipping operators idle capacity jumped to approximately 10% of the global fleet as cargo volumes collapsed in late 2009. The industry also implemented slow steaming to absorb another 5% of excess tonnage and lower bunker fuel expenses. Despite these operational measures, scrapping remained historically constrained due to the relatively young age of the global container fleet at the start of the recession.

The Extended Structural Gluts of 2012 to 2016

5.
Protracted pricing erosion: A.P. Møller - Mærsk experienced a persistent structural downturn between 2012 and 2016 due to severe overcapacity. This long-term supply imbalance culminated in Maersk Line reporting an underlying loss of USD 384 million for the full year of 2016. This negative result was driven by loss- making long-term contracts and low spot rates, which ultimately forced the company to cut its dividend by 50%.
6.
Accelerated fleet recycling: Maersk Line responded to the severe 2016 downturn by returning chartered vessels and accelerating the scrapping of older tonnage. The average age of container ships sent to the scrap yard fell to a record low of around 20 years during this period. Prior to acquiring Hamburg Süd, Maersk maintained an average fleet age of 9.2 years, which highlighted its preference for younger, fuel-efficient ships.
7.
Drastic fleet restructuring: COSCO SHIPPING Holdings disassembled five container vessels in 2016 to lower its average fleet age and improve fuel efficiency. This scrapping program removed 224,560 deadweight tons from the water but triggered an unaudited net loss of RMB 341 million from the disassembly. These structural adjustments took place as the group recorded a massive consolidated net loss of RMB 9.22 billion for the first nine months of 2016.
8.
Persistent margin pressure: Hapag-Lloyd recorded a disappointing group loss of slightly over USD 150 million in the first half of 2016 due to a severe decline in freight rates. Operating revenues fell by USD 1 billion as spot rates remained depressed. To survive the downcycle, the company pursued mergers with United Arab Shipping Company and participated in extensive alliance reorganizations.
9.
Contrary evidence: Hapag-Lloyd managed to generate a positive operating profit of EUR 126 million for the full year of 2016 despite the severe industry downturn. This positive EBIT performance was supported by a 33% decline in bunker fuel prices and successful cost-cutting programs. This shows that some top-tier carriers remained operating profit positive even during the worst year of the 2012 to 2016 oversupply cycle.
10.
Sustained supply adjustments: Global containership demolition activity surged to a historic high of approximately 650,000 TEU to 702,200 TEU during the 2016 downturn. According to Alphaliner, the industry's idle capacity peaked at 7.4% of the global fleet, which represents approximately 1.5 million TEU of inactive capacity. This massive supply adjustment was reinforced by the collapse and bankruptcy of Hanjin Shipping, which temporarily improved spot rate sentiment in late 2016.

Post Pandemic Normalization and 2023 Downcycle

11.
Rapid earnings normalization: A.P. Møller - Mærsk experienced a severe correction in the second half of 2023 as pandemic-induced supply chain bottlenecks fully resolved. Although Maersk recorded higher transport volumes, intense pricing pressure from rapid fleet expansion eroded ocean container margins to unsustainable levels. In response, the carrier launched a cost-containment program that cut its global workforce by approximately 10,000 jobs.
12.
Challenging fleet demographics: Hapag-Lloyd experienced a significant drop in its 2023 earnings as global container transport capacity grew by 8% while demand increased by only 0.4%. The carrier entered this downcycle with a capacity-weighted average fleet age of 11.2 years, which was slightly above the peer average of 10.9 years. Despite these demographics, Hapag-Lloyd did not scrap its older vessels because pandemic-era long-term charter contracts delayed vessel recycling.

Resilient cash buffers: COSCO SHIPPING Holdings container shipping net profit fell to RMB 22 billion in 2023, representing a steep decline from the record RMB 137 billion reported in 2022. The carrier operated an active fleet with an average age of 13.5 years and an orderbook-to-fleet ratio of 23.9% by late 2023. However, COSCO's robust cash position prevented any immediate pressure to decommission its older, less efficient vessels.

Section 3 – Container Fleet Age Profile and Orderbook Deliveries Beyond 2028

Section summary
  1. Global container ship deliveries are scheduled to reach unprecedented levels through 2028 before tapering off.
  2. Demographics of the global container fleet show that a substantial portion of smaller feeder vessels will face intense scrapping pressure as they exceed 25 years of age.
  3. Our confidence in this capacity demographic assessment is high because the newbuild delivery schedules and current vessel age profiles are documented in company reports and broker registries.

This wave of newbuilds will significantly expand industry capacity unless unprecedented scrapping levels absorb the tonnage or slow steaming is expanded.
2.
Mounting capacity overhang: Global containership orderbook-to-fleet ratios have surged to approximately 41.8% driven by the massive contracting wave during the post-pandemic era. Deutsche Bank Research noted on November 2, 2026, that this ratio had previously hovered near 10% in 2021 before the order surge began. The huge influx of new vessels will pressurize freight rates starting in 2027 as the new ships enter service.

Bernstein Research reported on October 9, 2026, that although delivery schedules peak between late 2027 and late 2028, carriers have already started placing long-dated orders for 2029 delivery. Section 4 – Decarbonization and Carbon-Intensity Regulations

Section summary
  1. Regional environmental mandates such as EU ETS and FuelEU Maritime are placing direct financial pressure on older container ship operations.
  2. Ship recycling decisions will increasingly concentrate around mandatory five-year special survey drydockings as retrofitting costs for non-compliant vessels rise.

Regional and International Mandates

1.
Accelerated emissions taxing: Regional environmental regulations are imposing tangible costs on ocean shipping operations as European authorities fully implement the Emissions Trading System for commercial vessels. Shipping companies are required to surrender carbon allowances to cover 100% of their verified emissions from regional voyages starting in 2026, up from a 70% threshold in 2025. This progressive phase-in creates unavoidable carbon compliance expenses that heavily penalize less efficient, older container ships operating on European shipping lanes.
2.
Stricter fuel intensity: The FuelEU Maritime regulation implemented in January 2025 requires a continuous reduction in the lifecycle greenhouse gas intensity of marine energy. Vessels failing to meet these progressive reduction targets are subject to severe remedial penalties proportional to their compliance deficit. Non-compliance with these intensity rules can trigger steep financial compliance penalties of 2,400 Euros per metric ton of fuel equivalent.
3.
Tightening efficiency metrics: The International Maritime Organization uses its Carbon Intensity Indicator rating scale from A to E to continuously evaluate active fleet operational efficiency. Underperforming vessels rated as D for three consecutive years or E for a single year must submit corrective action plans to retain their certifications. These tightening operational standards compel shipowners to retroactively upgrade or decommission their older, carbon-intensive container vessels.
4.
Contrary evidence: Elevated charter rates and geopolitical route diversions have temporarily suppressed container ship scrapping despite tightening environmental mandates. Jefferies Research reported on October 22, 2026, that shipowners remain highly reluctant to recycle vessels because lucrative charter options keep older tonnage profitable. This strong freight environment has delayed necessary long-term capacity corrections by keeping less efficient ships operational across global networks.
5.
Volatile regulatory transition: Shipowners are facing significant investment uncertainty because the International Maritime Organization has postponed the formal adoption of its Net-Zero Framework. Deutsche Bank Research reported on October 21, 2026, that this delayed decision has temporarily slowed down the momentum for discretionary vessel engine retrofits. However, the long-term decarbonization trajectory remains in place as regional rules continue to escalate the operating cost burden for non-compliant fleets.

Section 5 – Industry Cash Positions and Peer Capacity Dynamics

Section summary
  1. Container shipping lines accumulated massive cash reserves during the pandemic super-cycle and subsequent disruptions.
  2. Unprecedented balance sheet liquidity enables carriers to prolong a capacity game of chicken rather than scrapping.
  3. Major liners are deploying their robust cash buffers into strategic fleet expansions, mergers, and terminal acquisitions.

Because carriers can easily afford the cash burn of maintaining idle vessels, they will delay decommissioning older ships. This tactical capacity management has driven up spot rates despite a growing global fleet. However, this strategy only provides temporary relief unless carriers commit to permanent vessel demolition.
