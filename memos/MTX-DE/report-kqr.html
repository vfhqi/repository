Section 2 – The GTF Durability and Grounding Loop

Section summary
  1. Progressive resolution of the Geared Turbofan powder metal contamination has started to reduce grounded narrowbody fleets from their historic peaks.
  2. Shrinking spare engine pools and normalizing delivery mixes represent a significant headwind to commercial original equipment margins through the end of the decade.

Grounding Recovery and Fleet Metrics

1.
Severe disruption: The Geared Turbofan powder metal quality escape created a severe operational bottleneck that peaked with 711 grounded narrowbody aircraft in October 2025. This forced parking rate represented 37% of the active PW1100G fleet at its maximum point of distress. Stored GTF-powered aircraft subsequently declined to 535 units by September 2026 as maintenance activities progressed. This recovery is expected to gradually improve narrowbody values as forced groundings decline.
2.
Sustained headwinds: Affected operators have experienced extreme capacity constraints and operating inefficiencies because of long turnaround times. Commercial aircraft shop visits still reportedly take up to 365 days compared to just 60 days before the pandemic. Competing LEAP-powered narrowbody fleets maintained a low 3.6% storage rate during the same period. Turkish Airlines reported that its parked fleet would remain elevated until at least the middle of 2027. These prolonged groundings indicate that MRO bottlenecks are more persistent than consortium partners originally projected.

Severe bottlenecks: Replacing the affected high-pressure turbine disks is heavily constrained by global forging and raw material capacity. These severe component shortages have significantly delayed the turnaround times of engines waiting for major overhauls. Consequently, full resolution of the grounding cycle requires several years of industrial ramp before maintenance networks can normalize.
5.
Improving outlook: Airline confidence is expected to gradually rebuild as the Geared Turbofan powder metal inspections are completed. The consortium has successfully transitioned all new-build production engines to full-life components. Operators are currently receiving upgraded Geared Turbofan Advantage engines designed to permanently restore time on wing durability.

Spare Engine Margins and Partner Stakes

6.
Elevated demand: The critical shortage of operating engines forced the consortium to prioritize spare engine deliveries over new aircraft installations. This strategic reallocation pushed spare engines to an abnormal 20% of the installed Geared Turbofan fleet by late 2025. MTU and its partners generated excellent profits because spare engine sales carry premium margins relative to loss-making installed deliveries.
7.
Declining contribution: The gradual stabilization of grounded fleets is driving a rapid normalization of spare engine requirements. Long-term forecasts show the spare engine ratio falling to 18% in 2028 and stabilizing at 15% by 2030. This normalisation represents a major margin headwind for MTU as high-margin spare shipments decline over the next three years.
8.
Shared exposure: Financial impacts of the Geared Turbofan durability issues are distributed strictly according to pre-defined program stakes. Pratt and Whitney holds the largest program share of approximately 60%, while MTU maintains an 18% risk-and-revenue-sharing partnership. This cooperative structure exposes MTU to direct obligations for program refund liabilities and warranty expenses.
9.
Complex contracts: The vast majority of Geared Turbofan operators maintain long-term fleet management program agreements with the consortium. These power-by-the-hour contracts transfer the financial risk of premature maintenance from the airlines directly to the engine manufacturers. Consequently, early component degradation causes maintenance costs to exceed initial estimates and severely depletes program economics.
10.
Onerous agreements: The consortium has been required to pay substantial cash compensation to customer airlines to cover parked fleet expenses. Total customer compensation payments for the Geared Turbofan recall campaign are expected to reach billions of dollars. MTU's individual share of these gross payments is expected to total hundreds of millions of euros before ending in 2026.

Unfavorable profitability: Geared Turbofan profitability continues to lag significantly behind established industry narrowbody peers. Pratt and Whitney commercial operating margins historically sit in the low 20s, whereas GE Aerospace achieves margins up to 40% on its commercial engine lines. This structural profitability gap is exacerbated because new-build engines generate negative margins exceeding $1 mm per unit.

In late 2026, approximately 25% of the Geared Turbofan fleet was parked compared to a stable 3.6% storage rate for LEAP engines. This persistent capacity constraint forces airlines to extend older narrowbody operations to maintain their schedules.

The subsequent implementation of redesigned turbine blades took several years but successfully eliminated aircraft on ground events by 2026.

While cash conversion is projected to normalize by 2030, the ongoing capital expenditures for MRO expansion and pre-financed shop visits represent an enduring drag on free cash flow that justifies the company's valuation discount.

Section 3 – The Older Fleet Extension and V2500 Shop Visit Loop

Section summary
  1. Severe aircraft delivery shortfalls force airlines to keep older narrowbody fleets flying, driving heavy maintenance demand for V2500 engines.
  2. Normalization of fleet deliveries will eventually trigger higher retirements, creating a used serviceable material tailwind that lowers long-term spare parts pricing.
  3. Surging fuel prices and airline capacity adjustments have elevated parking rates for legacy narrowbody engine platforms.
  4. Growing aircraft retirements and alternative component adoption provide high-margin material alternatives that challenge traditional OEM pricing power.

Mechanics of Older Fleet Extensions

1.
Elevated aircraft: Narrowbody aircraft delivery shortfalls have forced global airlines to extend the operating lives of older fleets instead of retiring them. According to Deutsche Bank on November 3, 2026, there are 1,700 narrowbody aircraft over 23 years of age remaining in active service compared to only 750 units in the year 2019. This structural age shift has created an immediate requirement for extensive legacy engine overhauls. Consequently, this prolonged fleet utilization keeps mature narrowbody engines flying much longer than operators originally planned, supporting MTU's commercial service segment.
2.
Strong aftermarket: Pratt and Whitney commercial aftermarket activities are experiencing strong tailwinds as a direct result of these older aircraft extensions. Jefferies on November 17, 2026, reported that commercial aftermarket revenues rose 10% at Pratt and Whitney, supported by higher legacy shop visits. Airlines are prioritizing these repairs because they cannot secure new single-aisle delivery slots from major manufacturers until past the year 2032. This sustained operational demand drives high-volume engine maintenance and consistent parts consumption across legacy fleets.
3.
Strong commercial: Woodward commercial services sales expanded significantly in fiscal year 2026 due to legacy narrowbody fleet extensions. Woodward reported in its November 19, 2026, annual filing that lagged aircraft retirements have driven massive demand for repairs and spare parts on legacy narrowbody platforms. This trend has sustained high spare line replaceable unit sales throughout the year. This mature fleet utilization bolsters long-term service revenues for narrowbody equipment suppliers despite elevated fuel costs.
4.
Contrary evidence: V2500 engine flight hours declined 10% year-over-year in late 2026 as high jet fuel prices led some airlines to trim capacity. Bernstein reported on September 8, 2026, that global V2500 active fleet hours fell 10% year-over-year as airlines prioritized more fuel-efficient narrowbodies. This decline suggests that legacy aftermarket momentum could soften earlier than consortium partners anticipate, especially if macroeconomic growth slows.

V2500 Shop Visit Economics

5.
Stable schedule: Pratt and Whitney commercial engine divisions are executing a stable schedule of V2500 shop visits with progressively heavier maintenance work scopes. Executive Vice President Christopher Calio stated in an RTX earnings call on April 22, 2025, that the company expects about 800 annual V2500 shop visits with heavier overhauls due to fleet aging. These older engines require more intensive repairs because they are approaching late-lifecycle maintenance. This mix shift supports high commercial spare-parts margins for partnership members like MTU.
6.
High-cost architecture: V2500 engine components are structurally more expensive to maintain during overhauls than competing narrowbody platforms. According to a Deutsche Bank research note dated April 10, 2026, a typical V2500 has two high-pressure turbine stages compared to only one stage on the CFM56. This complex engine architecture means that operators must replace a higher volume of expensive life-limited parts during each major shop visit. This high component intensity generates exceptional parts revenue growth for MTU, which controls a major share of these spare shipments.
7.
Resilient outlook: MTU Aero Engines commercial MRO segments continue to benefit from a stable but high-workscope legacy maintenance environment. Deutsche Bank reported on October 29, 2026, that about half of the active global V2500 fleet is currently approaching its second major shop visit. These late-stage overhauls carry significantly higher pricing and broader component replacement requirements than initial quick turns. Consequently, these deep maintenance events ensure highly resilient aftermarket sales for MTU through the end of the decade.
