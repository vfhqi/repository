Section summary
  1. The elevated FY2025 tax charge is best explained by a mismatch between where profits are booked and where tax assets can be used, not by one isolated item. The report does not give an entity-by-entity breakdown for every site. Section 1 – The Key Question and Why It Matters

Section summary
  1. Medartis faces severe earnings depression due to a high effective tax rate and extensive unhedged currency exposure.
  2. The consolidation of recent acquisitions like KeriMedical and NeoOrtho shifts the profit mix toward higher-tax jurisdictions.

Key Question and Core Impact

1.
Strategic inquiry: "What explains Medartis Holding AG's (MED-CH) high core tax charge (CHF 5.2m in FY2025 against core net profit of CHF 6.1m) and its tax charge in H1 2026: which group entities carry unrecognised tax losses, what transfer-pricing adjustments were made in 2025, what is the statutory rate mix across Switzerland, the US, France, Brazil and Japan, and what effective tax rate do management (who guide 18% to 19% long term) and each covering broker assume for 2026 to 2028?"
2.
Severe financial compression: Medartis reported a substantial core tax charge of CHF 5.2 million in FY2025 which severely depressed its core net profit to CHF 6.1 million. This high tax burden directly limits the cash available for reinvestment and restricts the growth of core earnings per share for public investors. Because the company operates with a high Swiss-franc cost base, any local tax inefficiency directly amplifies bottom-line volatility and reduces shareholder returns.
3.
Unhedged exchange volatility: Medartis operates with a Swiss-franc cost base and does not execute currency hedging despite major exposures to the US dollar, Australian dollar, and Euro. Foreign exchange losses reached CHF 5.2 million in FY2025 because the Swiss franc strengthened against all major operating currencies. These unhedged losses compound the financial pressure of localized tax expenses by reducing the group's consolidated profit before tax.
4.
Strategic business expansion: Medartis completed the full acquisition of KeriMedical in July 2025 and acquired a 51% stake in NeoOrtho to drive regional growth. The integration of these brands expands the group's manufacturing footprint across France, Brazil, and the United States. However, these newly consolidated foreign entities shift the geographic profit mix and incur localized taxes even when group net income remains low.

Operational Friction and Tax Dynamics

5.
Contrary evidence: Medartis managed to turn its core net result positive to CHF 6.8 million in H1 2026 despite persistent currency and tariff headwinds. Financial expenses decreased to CHF 2.0 million in H1 2026 from CHF 5.0 million in H1 2025 due to a softer US dollar and the appreciation of the Brazilian real. This shows that exchange rate shifts can occasionally alleviate bottom-line pressure without active hedging programs.
6.
Intense transfer pricing friction: Medartis distributes medical implants manufactured in Switzerland and France to international sales affiliates in the United States and Japan. This internal trade exposes the group to transferpricing audits and regulatory adjustments if profit allocations do not align with local statutory tax expectations. Such localized mismatches prevent the group from offsetting losses in one jurisdiction against taxable profits in another.
7.
Other side of the story: Medartis might achieve its long-term effective tax rate target of 18% to 19% if it successfully utilizes unrecognized tax losses in foreign affiliates. As KeriMedical and NeoOrtho consolidate backoffice operations under the Cold Fusion program, administrative cost synergies could improve pre-tax margins. However, persistent statutory rate mismatches across Brazil, France, and Japan could keep the effective tax rate elevated in the medium term.

Section 2 – Unrecognised Tax Losses and Carrying Group Entities

Section summary
  1. Medartis holds CHF 22.5 million in unrecognized tax losses concentrated in historically loss-making foreign subsidiaries.
  2. Recent acquisitions like KeriMedical and CADskills carry significant tax assets that cannot offset profitable Swiss operations.
  3. Peer structures at Zimmer Biomet and Smith & Nephew show similar reliance on localized valuation allowances.

Unrecognised Loss Carryforwards

1.
Significant unrecognized benefits: Medartis Holding AG reported a total of CHF 22.5 million in unrecognized tax loss carryforwards for the fiscal year 2025. These unrecognized balances declined from CHF 26.6 million in 2024 because of selective utilization and exchange rate translations. Management does not recognize these deferred tax assets because profits cannot offset losses elsewhere across the consolidated group. This restriction directly suppresses consolidated earnings per share by inflating the group's effective tax rate.
2.
Persistent loss carryforwards: The unrecognized tax losses within Medartis Holding AG carry no contractual expiration date under current local statutory rules. This lack of expiration allows the group to potentially utilize these assets if local entities achieve sustained profitability. However, the historical loss-making profile of these subsidiaries prevents immediate recognition. This dynamic means that any future turnaround in local operations would immediately boost net margins.
3.
Swiss asset write-offs: Medartis has historically adjusted its Swiss tax assets in response to corporate restructurings and changes in local profitability. The group reported a write-off of deferred tax assets on intercompany profits of CHF 0.7 million in 2020. Furthermore, the Swiss entities recorded tax-deductible impairments in their statutory financial statements during 2024. These domestic adjustments complicate the group's ability to maintain a stable Swiss tax base over multi-year periods.
4.
Corporate tax governance: Medartis coordinates its global tax compliance through its Corporate Tax and Corporate Legal departments based in Switzerland. Global Compliance independently monitors these operations and reports directly to the Board of Directors' Finance and Audit Committee. The committee is tasked with evaluating potential tax exposures and monitoring corporate tax rate developments across all regions. This structured oversight helps mitigate localized transfer-pricing risks but cannot force the recognition of foreign tax assets.

KeriMedical contributed a net loss of CHF -0.6 million to Medartis in 2023 due to aggressive capacity investments. Consequently, KeriMedical remains a key entity holding unrecognised deferred tax assets within the wider group.

This operational turnaround is critical for improving consolidated net margins and earnings per share.

Section 3 – Transfer-Pricing Adjustments and Currency Friction

Section summary
  1. Unhedged intercompany transactions expose Medartis' transfer prices to severe foreign exchange friction.
  2. Centralized Swiss operations and diverse regional manufacturing sites trigger complex localized tax audit exposures.
  3. High Swiss-franc operating costs compress margins across the group's global sales subsidiaries.

Internal Transaction Flows

1.
Structured global transfers: This analysis of Medartis' transfer pricing is based on a review of 15 relevant sources covering five group companies, with none excluded. The group coordinates its production flows between Basel and manufacturing sites in France, Brazil, Belgium, and the United States. These internal flows are governed by a centralised Swiss transfer-pricing model designed to allocate profits based on localized value creation. This structure ensures that intercompany transactions are benchmarked against arm's-length external data.
2.
Scaled transaction volumes: Intercompany transactions within Medartis are eliminated upon consolidation to reflect the group's true underlying financial results. In H1 2026, the company reported inter-segment revenues of CHF 24.9 million for EMEA and CHF 1.7 million for North America. This represents a decrease from the CHF 34.6 million EMEA inter-segment revenues reported in H1 2025. Managing these massive internal product transfers exposes the group's transfer-pricing margins to regulatory scrutiny across diverse tax jurisdictions.
3.
Strategic production shifts: Medartis relocated manufacturing assets from its Basel headquarters to its facility in Warsaw, Indiana, under Project Flash. This movement involved transporting Swiss-type lathes and milling machines to accelerate local production for the US market. By producing plates and screws directly in the United States, Medartis expects to mitigate foreign-exchange and customs tariff exposures on exports. These operational transfers alter the historical cost allocations and transfer pricing baseline for the US affiliate.
4.
Contrary evidence: Medartis has experienced instances where import tariffs in the United States were charged at rates higher than actually due. This resulted in a CHF 4.4 million receivable recognized in H1 2026, including CHF 1.7 million from the 2025 financial year. While tariff errors create balance sheet friction, they demonstrate that external customs disputes can arise independently of internal transfer pricing models. Such retrospective refunds temporarily distort local subsidiary profitability metrics and related tax calculations.

Currency Friction and Margins

5.
Severe currency exposure: Medartis faces substantial foreign exchange risks because it does not maintain a formal currency-hedging program. The group operates with a heavy Swiss-franc cost base while generating revenues in foreign currencies. Consequently, the strengthening Swiss franc generated unhedged foreignexchange losses of CHF 5.2m in FY2025, primarily affecting intercompany financing agreements. This unhedged currency volatility severely depresses consolidated pre-tax profits and distorts the transfer prices charged to sales subsidiaries.
6.
Persistent margin pressure: The core gross profit margin of Medartis declined by 1.8 percentage points to 81.0% in FY2025. Unfavorable currency developments accounted for a 0.6 percentage point drag, alongside tariff burdens and capacity expansions. Because the company distributes implants through foreign sales offices, unhedged intercompany transfer prices pass Swiss-franc cost inflation directly to international affiliates, which prevents local subsidiaries from maintaining stable operating margins under review.
7.
Fluctuating local results: Favorable foreign exchange developments in H1 2026 improved the group's financial expenses to CHF 2.0 million. This improvement was driven by the appreciation of the Brazilian real, Mexican peso, and Australian dollar. However, these short-term currency gains do not protect long-term transfer pricing margins from persistent Swiss-franc strength. Unless Medartis implements hedging, the EBIT margins of foreign sales affiliates will remain highly volatile and vulnerable to tax authority challenges.

Transfer Pricing Audits

8.
Elevating audit exposures: Medartis operates in multiple countries where different interpretations of tax rules can lead to highly uncertain tax positions. The group's transfer pricing model for goods and shared services is vulnerable to challenges by national tax authorities during routine audits. Management must make significant judgments to estimate and accrue for potential tax liabilities that may arise upon review. Actual audit outcomes can differ materially from expectations, directly impacting future income tax expenses and earnings per share.
9.
Severe compliance disputes: The localized compliance risks faced by Medartis are illustrated by historical tax and legal disputes in its operating regions. The Brazilian authorities have conducted investigations into Extera, the former distributor acquired by Medartis in 2017, regarding compliance violations. This precedent highlights how regional acquisitions can expose the group to unforeseen statutory audit liabilities and regulatory friction. Similar localized audits in France or the US could disrupt transfer-pricing structures and trigger retrospective tax assessments.
10.
Other side of the story: Medartis can significantly reduce its global transfer-pricing friction if its manufacturing localization projects succeed. Ramping up Warsaw facility production to serve 80% of US demand by mid-2027 will remove the need for Basel exports. This regionalization minimizes both unhedged Swiss-franc cost exposure and localized import tariff burdens for the US subsidiary. However, until all regional hubs achieve independent manufacturing scale, centralized Swiss cost allocations will continue to inflate local tax charges.

Section 4 – Statutory Rate Mix and Geographic Profit Allocation

Section summary
  1. Medartis' low Swiss baseline rate is offset by high corporate tax rates across its foreign subsidiaries.
  2. Geographic profit mismatches and unrecognized intercompany profits drove the high core tax charge in fiscal 2025.

Statutory Corporate Tax Rates

1.
Domestic tax exposure: This analysis of Medartis' statutory tax mix uses eight relevant sources covering four companies, with none excluded. The applicable Group tax rate of Medartis was 13.04% in both fiscal years 2024 and 2025. Sandoz reported that the Basel-Stadt cantonal tax rate change effective January 1, 2026, will increase the blended Swiss rate to 14.53% for local subsidiaries. This rate change will increase domestic tax expenses unless profits are offset by losses elsewhere.
2.
United States exposure: Medartis operates its North American manufacturing hub in Warsaw, Indiana, exposing it to higher statutory rates than in Switzerland. The statutory federal corporate income tax rate in the United States is 21%. State taxes, such as those in Indiana or California, add further liabilities to the baseline US federal rate. Because Medartis has centralized its manufacturing for the US market, these higher US statutory rates will increasingly drag on consolidated earnings.
3.
Global statutory rates: Medartis must navigate even higher statutory tax rates across its other key operating jurisdictions in Europe, Latin America, and Asia. France and Brazil impose statutory corporate tax rates that exceed 25%, which elevates the group's regional cost burden. In Japan, peer disclosures show that the statutory corporate income tax rate was 33.58% in 2025. These high statutory rates mean that local profits are taxed at more than double the standard Swiss baseline rate.
4.
Contrary evidence: Medartis managed to avoid immediate cash tax payments in several foreign jurisdictions because of local statutory loss positions. The US subsidiary of Cryofocus America, for instance, recorded no tax provisions in 2025 because it was entirely loss-making. Similarly, Medartis reported that its newly consolidated foreign affiliates carry unrecognized tax losses that defer immediate cash outflows. This shows that high statutory rates do not translate to payments until local profitability is achieved.

Geographic Profit Mismatch

5.
Calculated tax differences: The vast difference between Medartis' statutory rate and its effective rate stems from regional profit allocation. Medartis reported profit before tax of CHF 12.788 million in fiscal year 2025. Although its theoretical tax expense at the Swiss rate was only CHF 1.668 million, the company faced a much higher actual expense. Higher tax rates in foreign subsidiaries added an extra CHF 0.751 million to the Group's tax burden. This geographic mismatch directly depresses consolidated core EPS by inflating the effective rate.

Unrecognized temporary friction: Intercompany profit eliminations represent the largest driver of the Medartis tax rate mismatch. Medartis recorded CHF 5.050 million in unrecognized temporary differences in fiscal year 2025. These unrecognized balances arose primarily from intercompany profits on goods distributed across international subsidiaries. Because the group's transfer pricing model did not allow the tax effects on these eliminations to be recognized, the actual tax charge skyrocketed relative to pre-tax profits.

Artificially inflated profits: Medartis' reported pre-tax income was artificially inflated by one-off items that did not provide taxable cash flow. The group recognized CHF 4.683 million in non-taxable income in fiscal year 2025. This non-taxable income included revaluation gains on the KeriMedical investment and the release of Nextremity Solutions contingent liabilities. This geographic revenue concentration ensures that the group remains heavily exposed to high-tax regions like Germany and the Americas.

Peer Tax Benchmarking

9.
Localized domestic peers: Other Swiss-domiciled life science companies face similar statutory rate variations depending on their cantonal footprint. Peer manufacturer Oculis Holding AG reported standard combined effective corporate income tax rates of approximately 11.9% in Zug and 14.7% in Lausanne for 2025. Similarly, peer developer Basilea Pharmaceutica Ltd faced an expected tax rate of 13.45% in 2025, comprising a 7.83% Swiss federal tax and a 5.62% cantonal tax. This shows that Swiss medtech companies operate with highly localized domestic statutory rates that differ by canton.
10.
International distribution friction: US-based medical device peers demonstrate that operating internationally inherently drives the effective tax rate away from the federal statutory baseline. Staar Surgical Company reported that its effective tax rates differ from the US federal statutory rate of 21% primarily due to income tax expenses in Switzerland and Japan. Staar Surgical registered an effective tax rate of 28.2% for the nine months ended October 2, 2026. This suggests that global medical device distribution regularly creates geographic tax friction across foreign sales offices.
11.
Precedent defense tax: Japanese tax reforms offer a clear precedent of how legislative changes in foreign sales hubs can unexpectedly alter deferred tax valuations. In March 2025, the Japanese Diet enacted the Special Corporate Tax for Defense, which will be imposed from fiscal years beginning on or after April 1, 2026. Consequently, Japanese entities had to recalculate their statutory effective tax rate upward from 30.1% to 31%. This case suggests that Medartis' Japan-based sales affiliate faces persistent statutory tax headwinds that could further restrict consolidated net profit.
12.
Other side of the story: Medartis may significantly reduce its blended tax rate if the Swiss corporate tax reforms are fully leveraged. The introduction of attractive ordinary corporate tax rates and R&D incentives in Swiss cantons allows Basel-based firms to lower their domestic burden. Furthermore, if Medartis achieves sustained profitability in France and the United States, it can recognize its unrecognized deferred tax assets to offset foreign tax expense. However, until these regional entities turn profitable, unrecognized temporary differences will keep the core tax charge elevated.
