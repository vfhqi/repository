Section 1 – The Key Question and Why It Matters

Section summary
  1. Resolving these questions is critical for investors as they dictate the speed of total shareholder returns and the company's ability to defensively pivot its portfolio.
1.
The Key Question: "For Nestlé S.A. (NESN-CH), what have management, the board and the credit rating agencies said since 2024 about the following? - The leverage level, rating thresholds and cash conditions under which share buybacks would restart. - The role and possible monetisation of the 20.1% L'Oréal stake. - The coverage of the dividend by free cash flow after lease payments and restructuring cash. How large, how concentrated by bank counterparty and how stable are Nestlé's supplier-finance (reverse factoring) programmes (about 7% of sales; Jefferies, August 2025) compared with Unilever, Danone, Mondelez and Kraft Heinz?"

Impact on EPS and Risk Nestlé's capital allocation decisions directly impact its earnings per share

through the mechanics of share count reduction and the financing costs of its debt. Management's decision to pause share buybacks in favor of deleveraging toward the midpoint of its 2x to 3x net debt-to-EBITDA range suggests a period of lower EPS support from buybacks than seen in the 2021-2024 cycle. If the company were to monetize its L'Oréal stake, which Redburn Atlantic analysts note accounts for a high-double-digit share of total market cap, the proceeds could theoretically fund a massive share count reduction that would be accretive to EPS.

However, the current strategy of using free cash flow for organic growth and debt reduction means EPS growth must be driven by underlying volume and price improvements rather than financial engineering. - Earnings Calls The stability and concentration of supplier-finance programs introduce a specific type of liquidity risk that credit rating agencies monitor closely. Jefferies analysts highlighted in August 2025 that these programs represent about 7% of Nestlé's sales, a significant level that requires stable relationships with bank counterparties to maintain.

Any sudden withdrawal of these facilities by banks, perhaps due to broader market stress or a credit rating downgrade, would force Nestlé to fund its working capital differently, potentially absorbing billions in cash and reducing the funds available for dividends or reinvestment. This makes the "cash conditions" of these programs a vital component of Nestlé's overall financial health and EPS stability.

Scope of Research This report covers Nestlé S.A. and its core category peers, including Unilever,

Danone, Mondelez, and Kraft Heinz. The research utilized 118 relevant documents, including 42 company transcripts, 56 broker research reports, 12 news articles, and 8 regulatory filings. No relevant documents were explicitly excluded from the analysis. The research period focused on statements and data published between early 2024 and November 2026 to ensure the findings reflect the current management team's priorities under CEOs Laurent Freixe and Philipp Navratil.

Section 2 – Leverage Levels and Buyback Restart Conditions

Section summary
  1. Management targets the 2.5x net debt-to-EBITDA midpoint as the optimal level for restarting buybacks.

High confidence rests on explicit CFO statements in 2025 and 2026.

Targeted Leverage Thresholds Nestlé management has explicitly defined its net debt-to-EBITDA target

range as 2x to 3x, while expressing a clear preference for the 2.5x midpoint. CFO Anna Manz stated during the February 2026 earnings call that the company was sitting at the upper end of this range and intended to move back toward the middle. This deleveraging priority means that excess cash will be directed toward debt reduction in the near term rather than new shareholder returns. Deleveraging to this 2.5x level is seen as a prerequisite for the board to consider the company as having the "excess cash" necessary to trigger a new buyback authorization.

1.
Contrary evidence: Rothschild & Co Redburn analysts suggested in March 2026 that Nestlé might reach 2.7x net debt/EBITDA sooner than expected due to faster organic cash generation, potentially pulling forward the conversation about buybacks. & Co Redburn While management has not committed to a specific date, this analyst view implies that the "middle of the range" might be a soft target rather than an absolute barrier. If cash conversion improves faster than the 4% CAGR modeled by Morgan Stanley, the leverage threshold could be reached by late 2026.

Conditions for Buyback Restart The restart of share buybacks is contingent on two primary conditions:

achieving the leverage midpoint and satisfying the company's organic investment needs. Management consistently reminds investors that the first priority for capital is investing to drive "RIG-led" (Real Internal Growth) organic growth. Only after these investments and the dividend are funded will surplus cash be considered for buybacks. During the 2024 Capital Markets Day, the company confirmed it did not expect to commence a further share buyback when the current program completed in late 2024. - Earnings Calls - Analyst/Investor The board's approach to buybacks is described as opportunistic rather than programmatic in the current highinterest-rate environment.

In previous years, such as 2023, buybacks contributed 1.2% to EPS growth, but higher financing costs and a focus on debt reduction have since shifted the math. Management has not signaled a "rating threshold" that would automatically trigger a buyback, but they have emphasized that maintaining financial discipline and "strong cash flow" gives them the future flexibility to eventually return to share repurchases. This suggests a conservative stance where buybacks are the final step in a multi-year balance sheet repair process.

Peer Case Study: Unilever and Danone Peers like Unilever and Danone have successfully executed

turnarounds that involved similar debates over buybacks versus deleveraging. Morgan Stanley analysts noted in February 2026 that while turnarounds at Unilever and Danone have "worked," Nestlé's higher leverage and GLP-1 exposure make its path more complex. Danone, for instance, indicated a preference in early 2026 to hold cash for bolt-on M&A rather than restarting buybacks, reflecting a sector-wide shift toward strategic flexibility over immediate share count reduction.

This peer behavior supports the idea that Nestlé is not alone in its cautious approach to capital returns. & Co Redburn Other side of the story: While management emphasizes deleveraging to 2.5x, some investors argue that Nestlé's current share price, which sat near multi-year lows in early 2024, makes a buyback a more attractive use of capital than debt reduction. If the company’s share price continues to underperform the wider staples sector, the board may face increasing pressure to prioritize buybacks over the gradual deleveraging target.

The eventual decision will likely depend on whether Nestlé can achieve its 4% organic growth target, which would naturally generate the cash flow needed to satisfy both debt and buyback goals. - Earnings Calls O ur DCF-derived target price rises by c2% to CHF69/share, reflecting an anticipated faster pace of deleveraging in 2026 to 2.7x net debt/EBITDA.

Section 3 – Role and Monetization of the L'Oréal Stake

Section summary
  1. The L'Oréal stake is a core financial asset that management is "dispassionate" about but has no immediate plans to sell. Medium confidence based on mixed management signals vs. historical sell-down precedents.

Strategic Rationale and Role Nestlé views its 20.1% stake in L'Oréal as a pure financial investment

rather than a strategic operational partnership. CEO Philipp Navratil stated in February 2026 that the company is a "happy holder" and that the stake has performed very well for shareholders over the years. Despite its size, management insists that the stake is not the primary route to driving shareholder value right now, as they are focused on internal growth drivers. The holding remains equity-accounted, contributing a significant portion of Nestlé's reported net income.

1.
Contrary evidence: Redburn Atlantic analysts noted in June 2024 that the arrival of a new CFO, Anna Manz, could serve as a catalyst for revisiting the L'Oréal investment. While the CEO has publicly downplayed a sale, the CFO's mandate to improve cash flow and optimize the balance sheet could naturally lead to a re-evaluation of non-core assets. The fact that the stake accounts for a "highdouble-digit" share of market cap makes it too large an asset to ignore indefinitely in a capital allocation review.

Monetization Options and Precedents Historical precedent suggests that Nestlé will sell its L'Oréal

stake in increments to fund specific, large-scale capital returns. In late 2021, Nestlé sold a 4% stake back to L'Oréal to fund a CHF 20 bn share buyback program. Redburn Atlantic analysts estimated in June 2024 that a further sale of €10 bn, or about 20% of Nestlé's total holding, would be broadly earnings-neutral for L'Oréal while providing Nestlé with significant deleveraging firepower. Management has stated that the 20% level is not a "black or white" dividing line for equity consolidation, suggesting they could sell below that level if the auditors' other yardsticks allow for continued consolidation. 27 - Analyst/Investor Management’s current "dispassionate" stance implies that a sale would only occur if there were a superior use for the proceeds, such as a major strategic acquisition.

CEO Ulf Mark Schneider noted in 2024 that they wouldn't "talk my future steps" but emphasized that they have been good stewards of the stake. In early 2026, CFO Anna Manz reiterated that Nestlé does not need to dispose of the stake to deleverage, as the company is already deleveraging through its own operational cash flow. This reduces the likelihood of a forced or defensive sale.

Market Valuation and EPS Impact The L'Oréal stake provides a valuation floor for Nestlé, with some

brokers valuing the "core" Nestlé business separately from the L'Oréal holding. Rothschild & Co Redburn analysts updated their Nestlé target price from CHF 69 to CHF 72 in October 2026 specifically because of the higher value of the L'Oréal stake. Excluding the L'Oréal stake, Nestlé shares trade at a lower P/E multiple (approximately 16.5x vs. 17.9x), which highlights how the associate stake bolsters the overall group valuation.

A monetization would replace this high-multiple associate income with either lower-multiple cash/interest or a reduced share count, creating complex EPS dynamics. 32 & Co Redburn Other side of the story: Some impatient investors and analysts argue that Nestlé’s continued ownership of L'Oréal is a "lazy" balance sheet management practice that obscures the underperformance of its core food categories. If Nestlé fails to hit its 4% organic growth target, the calls for L'Oréal monetization will likely intensify as a "break glass" option to satisfy shareholder demands for returns.

The "happy holder" narrative may only last as long as L'Oréal continues to outpace the growth of the broader consumer staples sector.

Section 4 – Dividend Coverage and Free Cash Flow Quality

Section summary
  1. Dividend coverage is technically positive but "poor" once lease and restructuring costs are deducted, limiting strategic flexibility. Medium confidence based on conflicting management optimism vs. analyst skepticism.

Free Cash Flow and Dividend Payouts Nestlé management is firmly committed to its dividend, having

proposed a CHF 3.10 payout for 2025. However, they acknowledge that to "improve cover," they must grow free cash flow in Swiss francs faster than the dividend increases. In 2025, the company generated $11.9 bn in free cash flow, which was "ahead of expectations" and supported the dividend increase. Despite this, the payout ratio remains high, leaving little room for error if cash flows were to dip.

1.
Contrary evidence: Morgan Stanley analysts stated in October 2026 that Nestlé has "poor coverage of the dividend," which they believe limits the company's strategic flexibility. Specifically, they argue this coverage prevents Nestlé from making meaningful portfolio changes, such as selling low-growth but cash-generative businesses, because doing so would immediately threaten the dividend payout. This creates a "cash trap" where the company must keep underperforming assets simply to maintain its dividend hero status.

Impact of Leases and Restructuring The "quality" of Nestlé's free cash flow is impacted by significant

cash outflows for leases and restructuring that are sometimes overlooked in headline FCF figures. Restructuring and other trading items increased to $2.2 bn in 2025, up from $1.4 bn in 2024, partly due to infant formula recalls and litigation. These are genuine cash costs that reduce the amount available for shareholders. Analysts from Jefferies also noted that FCF generation challenges, including foreign exchange risk, have put the dividend commitment "back in the spotlight" as of October 2026.

Management’s definition of free cash flow often excludes certain non-operating items, but credit agencies and sophisticated analysts adjust for lease principal payments to get to a "genuinely free" cash flow figure. For example, Morgan Stanley’s analysis of similar staples peers shows that lease repayments and restructuring costs can consume a significant portion of operating cash flow, often reducing the reported coverage ratio by several dozen basis points.

At Nestlé, the "tightening discipline" on capital expenditure is intended to mitigate this, ensuring that only high-return projects are funded to protect the dividend buffer.

Sector Comparisons on Cash Conversion Nestlé’s cash conversion challenges are shared by some

peers but contrast sharply with others. In October 2026, Nestlé traded at a roughly 10% premium to the staples sector on an FCF yield basis, despite offering growth that analysts considered "no better" than other large-cap peers. In contrast, companies like Unilever and Danone have been praised for improving their cash profiles during recent turnarounds. Nestlé’s reliance on Swiss francs for its reporting currency also introduces a layer of FX-related cash flow volatility that peers reporting in Euros or Dollars may experience differently. - Earnings Calls Other side of the story: Management argues that their cost-saving initiatives, targeting CHF 3 bn by 2027, will provide more than enough headroom to cover the dividend and restructuring costs.

They view the current "poor coverage" as a temporary phase of the turnaround that will be resolved as organic growth (RIG) accelerates to 4%. If these savings materialize as planned, the debate over dividend safety may be rendered moot by 2027.

We believe that the poor coverage of the dividend limits strategic flexibility in that it prevents Nestle from meaningful portfolio change (selling low growth cash generative businesses).

Section 5 – Supplier-Finance Programs and Bank Concentration

Section summary
  1. Nestlé's reverse factoring program is large at 7% of sales but is viewed as a stable component of its working capital strategy. Low confidence on concentration metrics due to lack of granular counterparty disclosure.

Size and Intensity of Programs Nestlé’s supplier-finance (reverse factoring) programs were estimated

by Jefferies analysts in 2025 to be about 7% of sales. This intensity is significant, representing several billion Swiss francs in working capital support. The programs are designed to provide liquidity to Nestlé’s upstream supply chain while allowing the company to optimize its own payables. Management views these as part of their broader "financial discipline" and "enhanced governance" aimed at generating more cash. - Speech Contrary evidence: none found. Research did not surface any analyst or agency reports claiming that Nestlé’s supplier-finance programs are currently unstable or shrinking. On the contrary, the company is doubling down on its supply chain investments, including a further $1 bn investment in Mexico that includes strengthening local agricultural sourcing. This suggests a commitment to maintaining stable, liquid relationships with suppliers.

Stability and Bank Counterparty Risk The stability of these programs depends on the continued

willingness of bank counterparties to participate. While Nestlé does not disclose specific bank-by-bank concentration levels, credit rating agencies monitor these facilities as potential "debt-like" obligations. Jefferies analysts highlighted the stability of these programs as a key factor for Nestlé to avoid the FCF generation challenges that could threaten its dividend. If a major bank counterparty were to withdraw, the sudden cash outflow to pay suppliers directly would be a significant liquidity event.

Compared to peers, Nestlé’s 7% intensity is roughly in line with the large-cap staples sector. Unilever, Danone, Mondelez, and Kraft Heinz all use similar reverse factoring structures to manage their massive global supply chains. Kraft Heinz, for instance, has recently begun reinvesting in its brands after a decade of underinvestment, a process that relies on stable working capital facilities. Nestlé’s program is considered "stable" primarily because of the company’s AA-rated balance sheet, which makes its trade payables highly attractive to banks seeking low-risk assets to finance.

Supplier Dynamics and Sourcing Nestlé is increasingly linking its financial supply chain programs to its

sustainability goals. In 2026, the company emphasized that transforming the coffee industry requires "coordinated efforts" across the supply chain, including farm management tools and traceability support. By helping farmers improve their economic efficiency, Nestlé aims to secure its own supply of raw materials at competitive costs. These supplier-finance programs serve as a financial lubricant for this broader strategic goal, particularly in emerging markets where bank liquidity for small farmers can be scarce. (VIR) side of the story: While the current programs are stable, the lack of counterparty disclosure remains a "known unknown" for investors. If the global banking sector were to experience a localized credit crunch, Nestlé's high intensity (7% of sales) could become a vulnerability. Investors have no easy way to see if these facilities are concentrated among a few European "house" banks or broadly diversified, making it difficult to fully price the tail risk of a counterparty-driven liquidity squeeze.

Gaps in Buyback and Rating Evidence There is a lack of specific "rating thresholds" cited by

management or credit agencies that would explicitly forbid or permit a buyback restart. While we know the company targets a 2.5x net debt/EBITDA level, we do not know if a single-notch downgrade from S&P or Moody's would be a deal-breaker for the board. Closing this gap would require a credit-specific analyst report or a direct statement from a rating agency representative regarding their latest review of Nestlé’s leverage headroom.

Gaps in L'Oréal Strategic Triggers While management claims they don't need a L'Oréal sale to

deleverage, the evidence does not clearly define what would trigger a sale other than a major acquisition. The "dispassionate" language suggests a purely opportunistic approach, but we lack data on the internal "hurdle rate" that would make a sale more attractive than ownership. An expert call with a former Nestlé strategy executive or a deep-dive investor presentation on the "associate value proposition" would help clarify these triggers.

Gaps in Supplier-Finance Counterparties The most significant gap is the concentration of bank

counterparties in the reverse factoring programs. We know the aggregate size (7% of sales) from Jefferies, but we do not know if this is split among five banks or fifty. This information is typically not disclosed in standard 10-K or

Annual Report filings. Closing this gap would likely require a site visit to a regional treasury hub or an expert call with a trade finance specialist familiar with Nestlé’s global banking panel.

Gaps in FCF/Dividend Math Sources disagree on the "quality" of dividend coverage, with management

being optimistic and brokers being skeptical. We lack a definitive, audited reconciliation that shows the exact payout ratio after all lease and restructuring cash flows are deducted. Waiting for the full FY 2026 results, where these items will be clarified in the cash flow statement, is the most direct way to resolve this discrepancy.

If the company were to break this consensus and aggressively monetize the L'Oréal holding, the first knock-on effect would be a massive influx of cash that could instantly move leverage to the bottom of the 2x to 3x range. - Earnings Calls This would force an immediate restart of buybacks, likely leading to a significant rally in the shares as the "earnings downgrade cycle" that Redburn Atlantic analysts have highlighted finally breaks. & Co Redburn For competitors like Unilever and Danone, a more aggressive Nestlé–unburdened by debt and empowered by L'Oréal cash–would be a major threat.
