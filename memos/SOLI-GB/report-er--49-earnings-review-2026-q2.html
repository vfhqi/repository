Section 1 – Brief company description and history

Section summary
  1. Solid State entered the reviewed FY25/26 results as a recovering specialist electronics group, with defence and artificial-intelligence infrastructure demand lifting growth, but with the expectation bar already raised by an April profit upgrade and a valuation discount that assumed execution risk.
1.
Specialist positioning, diversified: Solid State was established in 1971, joined AIM in 1996 and supplies durable components, assemblies and manufactured systems for critical applications, with products ranging from ruggedised computing and communications to battery power, imaging and electronic components.
2.
Three-division model, established: The Group operated through Systems, Power and Components, with FY25/26 revenue split approximately 40% Systems, 21% Power and 39% Components, giving it broader exposure than a pure defence supplier but more operational complexity than a single-market electronics company.
3.
Systems, highest growth: Systems revenue rose 47.8% to £62.5m in FY25/26, chiefly because a £23.3m communications programme delayed from the previous year was delivered in the first half, making reported growth materially stronger than the underlying recurring run-rate.
4.
Power, improving mix: Power revenue increased 15.6% to £31.8m, while gross margin rose to 35.8% from 31.7% as Solid State replaced lower-margin work with higher-value battery and power programmes, a sharper improvement than the Components division’s recovery from the electronics downturn.
5.
Components, cyclical recovery: Components revenue increased 8.1% to £59.8m, helped by improving conditions, United States design wins, industrial internet-of-things communications modules and powerswitching demand linked to artificial-intelligence data centres.
6.
End-market concentration, rising: Defence and security accounted for approximately 47% of Group revenue in FY25/26, up from approximately 30% in FY24/25, so the business was more exposed to defence spending and programme timing than its broader industrial, medical and transport description suggested.
7.
Demand quality, uneven: Management reported strong demand in defence, security, artificial-intelligence data-centre infrastructure, industrial internet of things and medical markets, while domestic transport was more mixed, leaving Solid State better positioned than general industrial electronics but less diversified than the headline sector list implied.
8.
Geographic reach, international: FY25/26 revenue was generated 45% in the United Kingdom, 23% in North America, 28% in Europe and 4% elsewhere, although management cautioned that United Kingdom customer purchasing hubs can conceal the ultimate international destination of sales.
9.
Scale, still modest: The Group served more than 2,000 customers in over 60 countries and employed more than 470 people across 13 locations, a smaller operating footprint than large listed defence peers but one suited to specialist, design-in opportunities and customer-specific engineering.
10.
Three-year financial arc, volatile: Revenue moved from £163.3m in FY23/24 to £125.1m in FY24/25 before recovering to £154.1m in FY25/26, showing that contract timing and the electronics cycle created more volatility than the underlying structural-growth narrative implied. 31 Jul SOLID STATE PLC (00771335) - Group Of Companies' Accounts Three-year revenue and profit recovery from FY24/25 to FY25/26.
11.
Profit recovery, incomplete: Adjusted operating profit moved from £17.0m in FY23/24 to £6.0m in FY24/25 and £9.6m in FY25/26, while adjusted operating margin recovered from 4.8% to 6.2% but remained below the 10% medium-term ambition and the 10.4% achieved in FY23/24.
12.
EPS recovery, not yet normalised: Adjusted diluted earnings per share increased from 6.2p in FY24/25 to 11.0p in FY25/26, but remained below the 20p sustainable adjusted EPS goal for 2030, meaning investors were underwriting further margin, utilisation and organic-growth delivery rather than treating FY25/26 earnings as mature.
13.
Order visibility, improved but lumpy: The open order book was £102.4m at 31 May 2026 versus £103.4m a year earlier, but excluding the prior-year £19.0m NATO communications order it represented an £18.0m underlying improvement, which supported expectations for growth despite the absence of another major communications order in the reported figure.
14.
History, acquisition-led expansion: Solid State had made five acquisitions in the preceding five years, including Gateway Electronic Components, while the company described its current pipeline as focused on modest antenna-related bolt-ons because organic opportunities were then more attractive than large transactions.
15.
Pre-results forecast bar, already raised: Before the final results, the company defined market expectations as the average of Cavendish, Berenberg and Zeus forecasts, namely £145.0m revenue and £7.2m adjusted profit before tax for FY25/26, rising to £149.4m and £8.1m respectively for FY26/27.
16.
Zeus, positive but cautious: Zeus’s April 2026 note had raised FY25/26 revenue to £152.2m and adjusted profit before tax to £7.9m, but left FY26/27 earnings unchanged because geopolitical disruption, component lead times and working-capital requirements could offset the apparent growth opportunity.
17.
Berenberg, constructive valuation: Berenberg initiated coverage in December 2025 with a Buy rating and a 150p target price, assuming conservative capacity ramp-up but forecasting mid- to high-single-digit growth from FY28 and continued margin expansion, while no contemporaneous Cavendish target or rating was available in the reviewed evidence.
18.
Share-price evidence, incomplete: The available evidence records Solid State at 139.5p on 8 April 2026 and 199p in Zeus’s 29 June results note, but no reliable pre-results series was available to calculate the required three-, six- or twelve-month performance against the FTSE All-Share or AIM benchmark. Other side of the story: The bullish interpretation could be wrong if the apparent recovery mainly reflects the delayed £23.3m communications shipment and temporary defence timing, because FY25/26 revenue still remained below FY23/24 and the 6.2% adjusted margin remained well below both the prior 10.4% and the 10% ambition.

Section 3 – Key moving parts and changes

Section summary
  1. The most important earnings moving part is the transition from a lumpy communications-led recovery to broader defence, power, artificial-intelligence and systems growth, with EPS upside dependent on converting capacity investment into sustainable margins while absorbing leadership, supply-chain and programme-timing risk.
1.
Leadership reset, stabilising: Gary Marsh’s death in November 2025 created an immediate succession risk, but John Macmichael’s permanent appointment as Chief Executive in March 2026 and Jon Baxter’s move to lead Components reduced the expected disruption to operations and therefore to near-term EPS delivery.
2.
Board depth, strengthened: Victor Chavez’s appointment as Deputy Chair, followed by his planned transition to Chair, added senior defence and aerospace experience that should improve customer access and strategic oversight versus the previous board structure, although it also leaves investors testing whether succession can preserve the founder-era culture.
3.
Investor access, broadened: Solid State appointed Zeus as joint broker alongside Cavendish in October 2026 and announced a November Capital Markets Day, changes that should widen research distribution and improve the market’s understanding of the three-division model rather than directly change current-year EPS.
4.
Portfolio structure, clarified: Power was separated from Systems as a third reporting division, giving management clearer accountability for the battery business and making its 15.6% FY25/26 revenue growth and 35.8% gross margin more visible, with potential to improve future EPS through better mix and execution.
5.
Acquisition pace, moderated: Management continues to pursue acquisitions, but the near-term pipeline is concentrated on modest antenna-related bolt-ons because identified organic opportunities currently offer a lower-priority-adjusted risk than larger transactions, reducing immediate acquisition-driven EPS upside while limiting balance-sheet risk.
6.
Q-Par integration, progressing: The United States Q-Par antenna acquisition remains strategically relevant because it provides an approved distribution channel and supports local scale, but its historical third-party revenue of only $0.4m and profit before tax of $0.2m mean the main three-year EPS opportunity is indirect through cross-selling and onshoring rather than a material immediate contribution.
7.
Gateway contribution, modest: Gateway expanded Solid State’s components offering and was expected by Zeus to provide only a 1.5% uplift to FY26 earnings forecasts, so the acquisition strengthened the product portfolio but did not materially alter the central EPS case compared with organic recovery and operating leverage.
8.
Margin recovery, incomplete: Adjusted operating margin improved from 4.8% to 6.2% in FY25/26, but the company’s medium-term goal remains approximately 10%, meaning the next three years require further facility utilisation, product mix improvement and cost absorption before EPS can approach the potential implied by the target.
9.
Capacity investment, near-term drag: Ashchurch, antenna manufacturing, Power testing and other capability investments are intended to support higher-value growth, but management expects the investment programme and low initial utilisation to moderate short-term margin progression, making FY26/27 EPS more dependent on revenue conversion than on cost reduction alone.
10.
Capital expenditure, elevated: Solid State expects capital expenditure to exceed £7.5m over the next two financial years, compared with £0.2m spent on treasury shares in FY25/26, so cash-funded capacity expansion should support three-year EPS growth but may delay deleveraging and constrain further acquisition capacity.
11.
Leverage, improving: Net debt declined from £7.4m to £4.2m in FY25/26, better than Zeus’s £4.3m expectation, which reduces interest and funding risk for future EPS, although working-capital requirements and planned capital expenditure could prevent a rapid return to net cash.
12.
Dividends, recovering: The proposed full-year dividend rose from 2.5p to 2.75p per share and coverage improved from 2.5 times to 4.0 times, signalling confidence in the earnings recovery, but the allocation priority remains investment in growth rather than buybacks or aggressive distributions.
13.
Communications timing, normalising: The £23.3m communications programme delayed from FY24/25 was recognised in FY25/26, reversing the prior earnings shortfall and lifting Systems revenue, but the absence of another major communications order in the May 2026 order book means future EPS must increasingly come from recurring and newly won programmes.
14.
Defence demand, broadening: Defence and security accounted for approximately 47% of FY25/26 revenue, while orders expanded across communications, batteries, antennas and ruggedised systems, increasing the probability of above-market growth but also making EPS more sensitive to government approval, programme cadence and geopolitical spending decisions.
15.
Power momentum, accelerating: Power revenue rose 15.6% to £31.8m after Solid State exited lower-margin work and won higher-value autonomous, drone and defence programmes, creating a more attractive mediumterm EPS mix than the prior commodity-heavy portfolio.
16.
Components recovery, established: Components revenue increased 8.1% to £59.8m as industrial conditions improved and United States design wins emerged, while demand for industrial internet-of-things modules and artificial-intelligence data-centre power switching created a second growth leg beyond defence.
17.
Order visibility, supportive: The order book was £102.4m at 31 May 2026, broadly stable after excluding the prior year’s £19.0m NATO communications order, and most orders were expected to ship within 18 months, supporting the probability of forecast revenue delivery while leaving component availability as the key execution constraint. EPS ambition, raised in clarity: Management now frames the long-term objective as at least 20p sustainable adjusted EPS and also identifies a £250m revenue milestone by 2030, compared with 11.0p adjusted diluted EPS in FY25/26, implying substantial required growth in revenue, margin and share of higher-value programmes.
18.
Supply-chain risk, increasing: Artificial-intelligence infrastructure demand and geopolitical instability have lengthened semiconductor lead times and created shortages, so the same structural demand that can lift Solid State’s revenue may also delay shipments, raise memory costs and compress gross margin if customer price recovery lags.
19.
Political exposure, persistent: The earlier defence-order delay demonstrated that UK strategic reviews can move revenue between financial years, while the company still warns that delays to the UK Defence Investment Plan may affect the pace of order-book growth and revenue conversion over the next three years.
20.
Other side of the story: The apparent shift to diversified, higher-quality growth could still prove mainly a timing effect, because FY25/26 benefited from the delayed communications shipment and management has not yet demonstrated that the new facilities can sustain approximately 10% margins without another large programme.

Revenue, margins and earnings

5.
Headline revenue was strong but timing-dependent: H1 revenue rose 38.6% to £85.7m against £61.8m a year earlier, but £23.3m came from communications revenue delayed out of the prior year, so the headline increase materially overstated the recurring growth rate investors needed for FY26/27.
6.
Underlying growth was modest: After excluding the delayed communications programme and foreign- exchange effects, management reported underlying revenue growth of approximately 3.6% to 4%, which was below the 16.1% full-year revenue growth embedded in Zeus’s FY26 forecast and therefore did not independently justify raising estimates.
7.
The growth mix was better than the headline organic number: Defence and security demand offset continued weakness in industrial and transport markets, while Power and Components also improved, giving Solid State a broader recovery than a pure one-contract rebound but not yet proving a full industrial-cycle recovery.
8.
Gross margin missed the medium-term ambition: H1 gross margin was 31.0%, essentially flat against 31.1% a year earlier and below management’s stated ambition to exceed 10% adjusted operating margin at Group level, so the earnings improvement came mainly from volume and operating leverage rather than a major margin re-rating.
9.

10.
Adjusted profit before tax was ahead of the comparable period but not forecast-changing: H1 adjusted profit before tax rose 96% to £4.9m from £2.5m, while Zeus’s full-year forecast remained £7.2m because the firsthalf result was already anticipated to be heavily weighted by the communications shipment.
11.
Adjusted EPS showed unusually strong first-half weighting: H1 adjusted diluted EPS was 6.5p, slightly more than the 6.2p achieved in the whole of FY24/25, but Zeus still forecast only 9.6p for FY25/26 because the delayed communications contribution was not expected to repeat at the same level in the second half.

17.
Shareholder returns were raised without buyback support: The interim dividend increased 11% to 0.92p per share and full-year dividend guidance rose to 2.75p with 3.5 times coverage, while no H1 shares were repurchased and only modest treasury purchases for the employee share scheme were contemplated, making the higher dividend the principal capital-return signal.
18.
Leverage remained manageable but not yet eliminated: Net debt fell from £7.4m at the start of FY25/26 to £7.1m at the interim stage, and management expected approximately 0.4 times net debt to earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation for the full year, but the £2.4m FY26/27 net-debt consensus still required a stronger second-half cash release.
19.
Other side of the story: The results can be read as a cautious miss on earnings quality rather than an underlying beat, because the principal revenue uplift came from a shipment delayed from FY24/25, underlying growth was only about 4%, gross margin was flat, net debt exceeded the expected year-end level at half year and neither Solid State nor Zeus raised the FY26 or FY27 profit forecasts.

Main durability risk, unresolved: The annual report says most of the order book was expected to ship within 18 months, but delivery remained subject to supply availability, so the results improved confidence in nearterm revenue coverage without proving that the business has escaped its historic dependence on irregular programme timing.

Cash outcome, supportive but investment-heavy: FY26 generated £13.5m of operating cash and 141% adjusted operating cash conversion, compared with 173% in FY25, while inventory rose to £30.5m from £28.2m and planned capital expenditure may exceed £7.5m over the next two financial years, making cash conversion and returns on capacity investment more important than the earnings beat alone.
10.
Balance-sheet effect, modestly favourable: Net debt declined from £7.4m in FY25 to £4.2m in FY26 and was marginally better than Zeus’s £4.3m expectation, reducing financing risk and supporting continued investment, although strategic inventory and capital expenditure could slow the return to net cash.
11.
Valuation implication, rerating potential: At the £199 share price used by Zeus, Solid State traded on 17.2 times FY27 EPS and 15.7 times FY28 EPS, compared with 18.2 times for the UK defence and industrial technology sector and 19.3 times for broader European peers, so the earnings upgrade strengthened the case for multiple expansion if delivery continues.
12.
Single most important change, operating leverage: The most important change is that Solid State has shown the first credible evidence that revenue growth can flow through to EPS, because adjusted operating profit rose 60.0% to £9.6m while revenue rose 23.2% to £154.1m, although the delayed communications programme means the next test is whether this leverage persists without another equivalent shipment.
13.
Strongest case against, timing reversal: The strongest contrary case is that FY26 largely reversed a prior-year delay rather than established a new earnings base, because £23.3m of revenue came from the delayed communications programme, FY27 contains no comparable major communications order and the company says component shortages may affect project timing and viability.
14.
Consensus-ignored reading, margin matters more: The less obvious reading is that the investment case may depend more on facility utilisation and divisional margin mix than on defence revenue growth, because Systems achieved a 14.3% adjusted operating margin with communications support while low utilisation at the integrated systems facility and antenna investment continued to weigh on profitability.
15.
What would change my mind, conversion evidence: The constructive conclusion would be overturned by a falling or repeatedly delayed order book, persistent supply-related shipment failures, inventory growth without corresponding revenue, adjusted operating margin remaining near 6.2% or renewed net-debt growth; the next annual or interim disclosure should make these indicators observable through order conversion, divisional margins, utilisation and cash flow.
16.
Next question, profitable delivery: The next disclosure must show how much of the £102.4m order book has converted into shipped revenue and whether conversion has improved margins and utilisation, particularly in integrated systems and antennas, because that evidence will distinguish contracted visibility from merely deferred revenue timing.
17.
Second question, supply and cash sensitivity: The next disclosure must quantify the effects of memory prices, component lead times and strategic inventory on gross margin, working capital, free cash flow and net debt, because the April trading update gave no EPS, cash-flow or balance-sheet detail and left FY26/27 guidance to market expectations rather than formal company targets.
18.
Other side of the story: The reviewed earnings may ultimately prove to be a temporary rebound rather than the beginning of durable EPS compounding if defence procurement is delayed, the £23.3m communications contribution is not replaced, new facilities remain under-utilised or supply inflation absorbs the expected operating leverage, leaving Zeus’s 11.6p FY27 and 12.7p FY28 EPS forecasts vulnerable despite the initial beat.
