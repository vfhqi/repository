EXECUTIVE SUMMARY (BLUF) 31 findings

AIB Group has delivered exceptional profitability through the recent monetary tightening cycle, with net interest income surging from €3,841 million in FY23 to €4,129 million in FY24 before moderating to €3,748 million in FY25 as central bank rates began to ease, while gross customer loans climbed to €74.5 billion by June 2026. Leadership changes have introduced near-term execution uncertainty, notably CFO Donal Galvin's unexpected departure on 26 May 2026, though the transition was managed through the H1 2026 results announcement on 30 July 2026, alongside Deputy Chair Brendan McDonagh's exit on 30 April 2026. The core investment thesis rests on AIB's ability to sustain elevated returns through structural hedging, maintain low deposit betas amid rising digital competition, and diversify revenue streams via wealth management acquisitions while navigating a declining interest rate environment.

Outliers worth noting

The comprehensive analysis of AIB Group relative to its European banking peers reveals several statistical outliers and unexplained anomalies that warrant investor attention. These divergences highlight structural characteristics that distinguish AIB's operating model from continental norms, representing both unique competitive advantages and potential vulnerabilities in the Irish retail banking landscape. The most striking anomaly emerges in net interest margin performance, where AIB's first-half margin of 2.68% maintains a substantial 108 basis point premium over the European average.

This extraordinary spread persists despite the convergent monetary policy environment across the Eurozone, suggesting fundamental structural differences in either asset mix, funding composition, or competitive dynamics. The underlying driver appears to be AIB's exceptionally low deposit beta of 20%, which stands in sharp contrast to typical European retail banks experiencing materially higher pass-through rates on policy rate changes.

The sustained nature of this pricing power anomaly raises questions about the sustainability of such margins as foreign digital entrants intensify their deposit competition strategies in the Irish market. [Cross-ref: Section 6, Section 9]

Market structure dynamics present a fourth unexplained anomaly, where the complete exit of KBC and NatWest left 20% market share available for remaining incumbents, yet customer loan growth increased just 3% in H1 2026, highlighting slower mortgage market expansion than expected. This muted volume response to reduced competition defies standard industrial organization theory, which would predict aggressive market share capture by surviving players following major competitor exits.

The disconnect between structural capacity availability and actual lending growth suggests either severe supply-side constraints in the Irish housing market, regulatory friction limiting credit expansion, or strategic capital allocation decisions prioritizing profitability over volume growth. [Cross-ref: Section 3, Section 7]

Sell-side rating distributions constitute a fifth anomaly, with 89% Overweight and 11% Equal-weight ratings, representing an unusually bullish consensus lacking any negative or underweight recommendations. The consensus price target distribution ranges from €9 to €12.10, indicating exceptionally tight analyst agreement. This absence of bearish views diverges from typical European banking coverage, where analyst opinions normally distribute more evenly across the rating spectrum.

The uniformly constructive positioning occurs despite net interest income being likely to decline as ECB rates fall, suggesting potential groupthink risk or anchoring bias in the analyst community. The statistical clustering of optimistic forecasts may indicate underappreciation of cyclical earnings peak risks or emerging competitive threats from digital banking entrants.
