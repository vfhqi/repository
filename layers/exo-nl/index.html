The external environment is split. The largest asset sits in the most favourable end-market in the portfolio, with multi-year visibility and widening competitive separation; the dividend-paying industrial holdings sit in the least favourable, where rising competition, inventory and input inflation have removed most of their dividends; and the holding-company regime is risk-off with discounts at records. The external forces explain why NAV per share has lagged the MSCI World by about 13.5 points in 2025 and 15.7 points in H1 2026, and they do not offer a dated external catalyst for recovery inside six months. The steer to the Consumer: model NAV as Ferrari plus a cyclical industrial block plus a healthcare block, treat the industrial dividends as near zero (Stellantis) or depressed (CNH) through 2027 with recovery as an upside scenario, and treat the discount as externally stuck unless EXOR itself supplies the mechanism.

C-02

To what extent, and in which direction, is competitive intensity changing?

C+4568th percentile

Layer 1 · External change forces

The Investment Analyst's heading Competitive intensity: extent and direction of change

Competitive intensity is rising where EXOR is weakest and falling where it is strongest. In mass-market autos it is rising sharply: Chinese makers took European share from 0.2% in January 2020 to 9.8% to 11.9% in 2026, Stellantis is the only large European maker with major share loss (1.4 million units over ten years), and price competition is visible in Stellantis's 32% self-registration share in Germany and US incentives of $4,800 per unit against an industry $3,500. In agricultural machinery AGCO gained about 200 basis points of tractor share during the downturn and Deere dominates precision technology (59% of precision-enabled tractors), while CNH is still repairing share lost to a strike and quality problems in 2022 to 2024. In imaging, Philips is third in a stable triopoly (17% against Siemens 30% and GE 28%) but loses up to 3% of share in standardised Chinese categories, and ResMed holds about 90% of the US sleep market Philips vacated.

In ultra-luxury, intensity is falling: Aston Martin lost £189 million at adjusted operating level in 2025 and Porsche's operating return on sales fell to 1.1%, so Ferrari's separation from its peers is widening. For EXOR as a buyer of assets, competition for large, high-quality listed stakes and private assets is intense (private equity, sovereign funds, GBL's move to private assets), which is one reason management is waiting. Net: rising intensity bears on perhaps a fifth of NAV and on most of the dividend shortfall; falling intensity protects the largest single asset.

C-05

How are input costs, input availability and supplier concentration changing, and is the net effect a headwind or a tailwind?

F3821st percentile

Layer 1 · External change forces

The Investment Analyst's heading Input costs, availability and supplier concentration: net effect

The net effect is a headwind concentrated in the industrial holdings. Stellantis faces memory-chip, printed-circuit-board and raw-material inflation it describes as several billion euros across 2027 and 2028, enough to offset much of its €2.4 billion savings programme; it also suffered production halts at Pomigliano and Mirafiori from a single-source engine-control chip shortage, and relies on single-sourced parts while European supplier margins average 8% and three quarters of suppliers expect below 5% in 2026. Philips reports persistent component, freight and energy inflation and offsets it with dual sourcing and pre-buying. At the holding level, the relevant input is the cost of money: EXOR's debt is fixed at about 2.7% with no maturity before 2028, so rising rates do not reach its interest bill in the forecast window; the drag is the reverse, since management repaid a €200 million private placement early because cash earned less than the debt cost. For the model, input inflation matters mainly through Stellantis margins and therefore through the timing of any Stellantis dividend.

C-07

To what extent is a macro force acting on the company, on its sector and on its industry, and at which level does it bite hardest?

D3840th percentile

Layer 1 · External change forces

The Investment Analyst's heading Macro forces: company, sector and industry, and where they bite hardest

Macro bites hardest at the holding-company level, through the discount, and second at the industrial holdings. The Fitness for Fighting digest reads the regime as risk-off beneath an index at its high, with Fragility at Double Underweight and Momentum at Underweight; listed holding companies fell 2.4% (median) over three months and discounts widen in risk-off. EXOR's NAV is 68% listed, so a 10% fall in listed values moves NAV by about €3.5 billion (the company's own sensitivity), and the discount tends to widen at the same time (it reached 44% in 2022, 40% in 2016, and 24.4% NAV falls in 2011).

Rates act through two channels: high consumer and farm borrowing costs depress Stellantis and CNH demand, and the discount rate pressures holding-company and luxury multiples. Currency matters at both levels: a 10% stronger euro against the dollar cuts reported NAV by €559 million (the company's disclosure), translation exposure is unhedged by policy, and forecasts conflict (Morgan Stanley sees EUR/USD at 1.08 by mid-2027; Nordea sees 1.14 in late 2027 and 1.20 by end-2028). Wealth concentration is the one macro tailwind (UHNW pools growing about 5% a year), and it supports Ferrari. Net: the macro backdrop is a moderate negative, and its main transmission is to EXOR's discount rather than to Ferrari's earnings.

The Investment Analyst's heading Other material external dynamics

Four further external dynamics matter. (1) Activist pressure at a holding EXOR was selling (Iveco): Petrus, Syquant and Davidson Kempner (20.4% combined) pushed Tata Motors to raise its offer from €14.10 to €14.40 per share, adding to EXOR's proceeds but delaying final settlement. None of these changes the base case, but (2) and (3) are fat-tailed governance risks that a model should carry as scenarios rather than in the central path.

At the holding level suppliers are irrelevant: EXOR's only "supplier" is capital, and its debt is fixed-rate, long-dated and A- rated. At the sector level (European listed holding companies), the evidence in the sector digest is precise: a record discount is the size of the prize, not the trigger; discounts close only where a dated mechanism forces a price (a sale above book, a buyback of several percent of the shares, or a collapsed structure). (d) Guidance, consensus and the narrative are not behind our assessment in a favourable direction: the brokers' median target of €110 already assumes a discount of about one third, against our base case of no narrowing, and the insights in our work that the market does not have mostly explain why the discount persists.

2. Why the Financial Analyst's model differs from the second set's cross-check, and the fix. The model's net income is 13% to 15% above the second set's in every year (FY2026 €983.4m against €872m; FY2027 €358.8m against €312m; FY2028 €430.5m against €373m; FY2029 €570.7m against €496m; FY2030 €668.2m against €583m). There is one cause. The second set derived its FY2026 revenue growth input (plus 30.5%) by dividing its intended revenue of €1,031m by FactSet's annual FY2025 revenue of €790m. The model builds each year from the half-year periods, and FactSet's two half-years of FY2025 sum to €881m (€744m plus €137m), not €790m. The model therefore produced €881m times 1.305, which is €1,149.7m, and because every later year is a growth rate on the year before, revenue is 11.5% too high in all five years (€516m against €463m in FY2027, and so on). Net income is overstated by more than revenue (about 15%) because interest is a fixed euro amount. This is an input error, and I correct it: FY2026 revenue growth becomes plus 17.0% (€1,031m divided by €881m). With that one change the model reproduces the second set's revenue and net income in all five years, and the two "phasing" notes on revenue (FY2026) and on the FY2027 margin disappear, because both were knock-on effects (the model had to raise the second half of 2026 from the intended €75m to €194m to reach the wrong year total, which then inflated the second half of 2027).

Two smaller gaps remain and are not input errors. (i) FY2026 earnings per share: the model's €5.01 against the second set's €4.31 is a 16% gap where net income differs by 13%. The remainder is consistent with the model adding the two half-years' EPS: FactSet's H1 2026 actual of minus €5.53 (the IFRS loss, which includes the fair-value loss) on about 202.9m shares, plus a forced second half on about 199.8m shares. The second set's own divisor of 202.2m shares is also slightly high (it is the opening count; the average is nearer 201m). The right FY2026 figure on the net cash earnings basis is about €4.31 to €4.34. (ii) The FY2026 margin phasing note (periods moved by 1,072.9 points) arises because FactSet's H1 2026 "EBIT" is minus €1,114m, which includes the fair-value loss; to reach a full-year margin of 95.1% the model has to give the second half of 2026 an operating profit of about €2.1bn on €75m of revenue. The year is right; the half-year split is meaningless. The Financial Analyst should restate the H1 2026 actual on the net cash earnings basis (revenue €956m, operating profit about €931m, net income about €872m, EPS about €4.30) so that the second half of 2026 is about nil, the FY2026 EPS is about €4.31, and no half-year carries a fair-value item.

That range was formed when EXOR consolidated Stellantis, CNH and PartnerRe and reported EPS of €8 to €29 a share. Since 2024 the only forecastable "EPS" is net cash earnings of €1.60 to €3.00, which captures about one sixth of the value EXOR creates in a normal year (net cash earnings of about €1.60 to €2.40 a share against NAV per share growth of about €11 to €12 a year). 5. Where I think differently from the first and second sets.

  • The stub is not a floor. Both sets argue that because the market values the non-Ferrari assets at about 10 cents per euro, further widening "requires Ferrari itself to fall". The evidence is against this: in H1 2026 Ferrari's weight rose and the discount still widened to a record, because Stellantis fell; and Prosus traded for years below the value of its Tencent stake alone (Rothschild & Co Redburn, May 2025: a 19% discount to the listed stake even with everything else at zero). On Jefferies' figure of about €64bn for Ferrari's market value (4 November 2026), EXOR's stake is worth about €13.6bn against an EXOR market value of about €14.3bn, so the stub may already be valued at about €0.7bn, 3 to 4 cents per euro (my calculation; verify against a price feed before quoting). A negative stub is possible.
  • The share price has fallen about twice as far as NAV per share on the way down. 2025: NAV per share minus 8.1%, total shareholder return minus 18%. H1 2026: NAV per share minus 3.9%, share price about minus 7.7% (my calculation from the reported discounts). The cause is a discount that widens as NAV falls, not the stub arithmetic. The second set's "8.5% on the share price for each 10% on Ferrari" is about right in a drawdown for that reason; it should not be used for the upside, where the evidence is mixed (2024: NAV per share up about 9%, total return minus 2%).
  • EXOR's own buybacks have added to NAV per share and have not narrowed its discount. About €2.5bn repurchased and about 14% of the shares retired since 2021, while the discount went from about 39% to about 57%. The earlier sets' precedent of GBL narrowing from 27.9% to 20.9% conflicts with GBL's own September 2026 figure of 28.3% as cited in the co-movement report, and Wendel bought back 9% of its capital in 2026 with its discount "still at 50%". I therefore put the probability of a return of €2bn or more in March 2027 at about 10% to 12% (second set: about 20%), and I hold the discount flat in the base case where the second set lets it drift from 55% to 50%.
  • The upturn in EPS revisions is noise (first set, C-74, scored 60): three brokers on three definitions.
