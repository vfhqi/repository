Section 4 – Historical Scrapping Triggers: Freight Rates and Survey Costs

Section summary
  1. Low spot freight rates and escalating drydocking expenditures represent the primary economic catalysts for product tanker demolitions.
  2. High asset values and persistent trade disruptions have suppressed ship scrapping to historic lows since 2022.
  3. Regulatory alignment under the Hong Kong Convention and TORM transition to green recycling are structurally altering residual valuation models.

Scrapping Trends and Freight Rate Triggers

1.
Accelerated demolitions: Hafnia Limited notes that product tanker scrapping was exceptionally high in 2021, with 3.70 mm deadweight tons demolished due to weak spot markets. However, demolitions slowed dramatically to only 32 ships in 2023 and 21 in 2024 as charter rates rebounded. This severe drop in vessel retirements has allowed older tankers to remain active, which potentially threatens TORM plc future earnings unless sustained trade disruptions absorb capacity.
2.
Inefficient operations: OPEC reports that dirty and clean tanker freight rates began 2021 close to the bottom of the five-year range as fuel costs rose. This severe market imbalance forced owners of older product tankers to exit the market rather than operate at a loss. These historical rate bottoms highlight the minimum threshold required to trigger widespread fleet demolitions.
3.
Depressed earnings: DNB Carnegie highlights that historical scrapping remains heavily dependent on prevailing spot rates and bunker costs. Product tanker charter rates reached historical lows during the 2021 downturn, forcing owners to sell inefficient ships for recycling. If standard MR spot earnings fall below operating breakeven levels, demolitions typically accelerate to remove excess tonnage from the market.
4.
Countercyclical retirement: Jefferies Research states that ship scrapping is highly countercyclical, with owners preserving vintage vessels during high-rate cycles. Older vessels have been earning lucrative returns since 2022, which has kept scrapping levels near zero. Once market rates eventually soften, owners will lose the incentive to operate older units and accelerated vessel scrapping will resume.
5.
Contrary evidence: Deutsche Bank Research reports that tanker scrapping is likely to remain completely irrelevant to the supply story over the medium term. High charter rates continue to incentivize owners to keep older product tankers on the water for as long as possible. This implies that TORM plc might face prolonged competition from older non eco trading units even if regional demand softens.

Special Survey Expenses and Capital Decisions

6.
Discrete milestones: Morgan Stanley Research outlines that special classification surveys occur every five years, creating critical capital allocation decision points. At 15 years, steel renewal and machinery expenditures begin to rise alongside stricter charterer vetting. By age 20, owners must decide whether to fund another fiveyear cycle or retire the vessel for scrap.
7.
Escalating costs: Incred Capital reports that the cumulative cost of classification surveys rises sharply as a vessel ages. By the time a tanker reaches 15 years of age, cumulative survey costs total approximately USD 10.50 mm. This figure balloons to USD 20.75 mm by age 20, which represents about 23% of the cost of a newly built vessel.
8.

These capital expenditures represent standard drydocking requirements for mid-sized product carrying tankers. These fixed costs reduce TORM plc net cash generation on older vessels unless spot rates remain well above breakeven levels.
9.
Capital barriers: HC Wainwright highlights that passing the fourth special survey at 20 years of age requires extensive and unpredictable upgrades. Owners must commit significant capital to ballast water treatment systems and emissions compliance retrofits to maintain a license to operate. If freight rates decline, these prohibitive drydocking capital expenditures make scrapping the only viable option for non-eco fleets.

This doubled frequency significantly increases off-hire days and recurring operational expenditures. These frequent drydockings depress TORM plc operating margins on older conventional product tankers unless freight rates remain exceptionally strong.
