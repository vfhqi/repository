Section 3 – Long-Term Trends in Alpine Run-Of-River Generation

Section summary
  1. Long-term climate change accelerates Alpine glacier retreat and alters regional precipitation patterns.
  2. Seasonal runoff profiles are shifting from summer snowmelt toward increased winter rainfall.
  3. Alpine run-of-river generation capacity faces winter increases and summer volume reductions.
  4. Nordic hydrological regimes display contrasting trends with stable snowpacks and rising demand.

Alpine Cryosphere and Glacier Retreat

1.
Accelerated melting: Scientific studies indicate that climate change is causing unprecedented degradation of the European cryosphere. Glaciers are shrinking rapidly, which makes them a less dependable water source over the long term. Under a warmer climate, the mountain cryosphere will experience a mean annual runoff decline exceeding 10% by 2100 in one-third of large basins. This continuous melt of ice reserves will permanently alter downstream hydrology and impact hydropower.
2.
Shrinking glaciers: Hydrological monitoring shows that temperature increases are driving extreme glacier mass loss across all European mountain ranges. Alpine glaciers lost roughly 10% of their volume from 2022 to 2023 alone. This massive ice reduction has immediate repercussions on local water availability and agricultural practices. Ultimately, these rapid glacier changes constrain baseline run-of-river electricity generation during the warm summer months.
3.
Rising temperatures: Climate projections suggest that warming in mountain regions will continue to accelerate glacial retreat over the coming decades. In similar mountainous catchments, up to 90% of glacier volumes could be lost by the turn of the century. This depletion reduces the critical dry season flows that support river systems. These severe water volume reductions threaten the stability of run-of-river assets that rely on stable flows.
4.
Diminishing storage: Hydrological models indicate that shrinking glaciers are becoming a far less dependable water supply source for European rivers. This long-term decrease in frozen water reserves is closely linked with declining soil moisture and rising local temperatures. Consequently, the natural buffering effect of Alpine glaciers is projected to deteriorate significantly. This depletion will ultimately pressure summer river discharge volumes across Central Europe.

Shifting Seasonal Runoff Distribution

5.
Earlier snowmelt: Warming trends are actively shifting the timing of the seasonal melt cycle across European mountain ranges. In the Alps, snowmelt currently starts approximately 2 days earlier each decade. This earlier melt cycle is expected to double in speed between 2050 and 2100 under high-emissions scenarios. Consequently, seasonal water runoff will shift forward into early spring rather than summer.
6.
Precipitation shifts: Long-term climate models show a clear shift in the seasonal distribution of precipitation across the Danube basin. Projections through 2100 indicate winter precipitation will increase by 6.5%, while summer precipitation drops by 13.8%. This changing weather pattern reduces the average amount of snowmelt while lowering summer river flows. These seasonal water shifts will reduce summer hydroelectric power generation in the region.
7.
Runoff seasonality: Climate-driven changes across the Danube basin will cause wetter winters but drier spring and summer months. This transition leads to a shift toward liquid precipitation with more rain instead of snow. Consequently, seasonal river flow patterns will change as rivers rise earlier to align with winter rainfall. This early peak means that summer river flows will decline due to reduced snowpack.
8.
Winter flooding: Precipitation projections indicate that Alpine regions will experience a substantial decline in freezing days over the next century. This temperature increase reduces the winter snowpack and modifies the runoff seasonality of mountain catchments. As a result, more water will settle directly into the soil during winter months, increasing regional flooding risks. This altered hydrological cycle will increase winter runof-river output while curtailing summer volumes.
