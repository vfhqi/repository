Section 2 – Sell-side and market focus prior to these recent earnings

Section summary

The market expected further acceleration.

3.
Peer cycle, relative advantage: DNB Carnegie’s sector work expected Swedish compounders to deliver approximately 4% organic growth in Q3 and Q4, while it identified OEM, NCAB and Bufab as the companies with the strongest near-term momentum; OEM therefore entered the results with a higher organic-growth expectation than mature peers such as Addtech, Indutrade, Lagercrantz and Lifco OEM carried the sector’s higher bar.
4.
Cyclicality, unusually resilient: DNB Carnegie’s earlier industry analysis found that OEM had historically been less affected by the Nordic and European industrial slowdown than more cyclical peers such as NCAB and Bufab, despite a close relationship between OEM’s organic growth and Swedish and euro-area industrial production; the pre-results question was whether this resilience would persist as the cycle turned Resilience was already embedded in expectations.
5.
Industrial recovery, assumed catalyst: DNB Carnegie expected European industrial production to rebound during 2026 and 2027 after two to three difficult years, supporting approximately 6% average organic growth for OEM in 2026–27; the market was consequently focused on confirmation that the recovery had begun, rather than on a return to merely stable demand The cycle was expected to turn.

End-market mix, uneven exposure: DNB Carnegie’s peer work highlighted stronger demand in power grids, data centres and defence across the broader technology-trading universe, while sawmills, special vehicles and construction remained weak; investors therefore expected OEM’s automation, electrification and industrial niches to offset construction-related softness in Sweden and Finland Structural niches had to offset construction.

18.
Guidance, qualitative confidence: Management’s Q2 disclosure said strong order intake and a growing backlog provided a solid starting point for the second half, while its financial position supported both organic growth and targeted acquisitions; because OEM did not provide numerical sales or EBITA guidance, investors had to translate qualitative confidence into their own forecasts The guidance framework remained nonquantified.

20.

21.
Market focus, expectation risk: DNB Carnegie’s May framework described OEM as capable of 15–20% annual EBITA growth through a combination of 7–8% organic EBITA growth and 10–15% acquisition-driven growth, while Redeye’s valuation already placed OEM above the sector’s peer median; the pre-results risk was therefore that a good quarter would merely meet an elevated private expectation A good quarter risked being insufficient.
22.
Other side of the story: The bullish setup could have been wrong if the industrial recovery remained uneven, construction weakness spread, acquisition competition forced OEM to pay higher prices or the company sacrificed cash conversion to support faster growth, because peer research showed that acquisition intensity, leverage and valuation were already central risks across Swedish compounders The recovery thesis still carried cycle risk.

Product regulation, the clearest company-specific liability: OEM reports obligations under the European Registration, Evaluation, Authorisation and Restriction of Chemicals framework, waste rules and Substances of Concern in Products reporting, while acknowledging incomplete supplier data and material mapping; future requirements for traceability, labelling or restricted substances could increase compliance work, trigger warranty or recall costs and tie up cash in product remediation Traceability gaps expose future liability.
7.
Climate disclosure, execution risk: OEM is applying phase-in provisions for parts of the European Sustainability Reporting Standards and has not yet established a fully developed due-diligence process or quantitative scenario analysis; the immediate cash cost is likely manageable, but incomplete data could cause customers to reject products lacking carbon information and could weaken OEM’s ability to capture sustainability-linked demand Disclosure capability trails regulatory ambition.
8.
Cybersecurity and trusted procurement, selective opportunity: A senior procurement manager at RWE said European critical-infrastructure buyers are scrutinising high-risk vendors, component origins and software development practices more closely, particularly where Chinese suppliers are involved; this may favour OEM’s local technical relationships and European market presence, but it also raises supplier-screening, cybersecurity-documentation and audit costs across data-centre and electrical-infrastructure opportunities Trusted sourcing can support market access.
9.
Labour and human-rights rules, upstream burden: OEM’s annual report identifies potential risks involving low wages, long shifts and physically demanding work in upstream manufacturing and warehouse operations, especially in parts of Asia, where direct monitoring is limited; stronger due diligence and supplier audits could add operating expense and lengthen onboarding, but failure to comply could cause customer exclusion, reputational damage or lost sales Upstream labour controls remain incomplete.
10.
Competition and governance, no immediate change: OEM’s 2025 report says it does not accept corruption, bribery or unlawful restrictions of competition, but also states that a fully established due-diligence process is not yet in place; no competition-law investigation, sanction, procurement ban or material regulatory enforcement event was identified through 22 October 2026, so the current EPS effect is mainly controlinvestment risk rather than a reported financial loss No material enforcement event was identified.
11.
Geopolitical exposure, indirect and asymmetric: OEM does not report material direct sales exposure to Russia, the Middle East or other active conflict zones, but its European logistics, imported components and industrial customers remain vulnerable to sanctions, shipping disruption, energy inflation and delayed capital projects; the Q3 report continued to identify competition, liquidity, interest-rate and currency risks without quantifying geopolitical sensitivities, leaving downside effects on margin and cash harder to model Geopolitics reaches OEM through logistics.
12.
Other side of the story: Regulatory change could become a competitive advantage rather than a drag if OEM’s technical documentation, supplier relationships and local presence allow it to absorb compliance costs more efficiently than small distributors, while climate, cybersecurity and regional-investment rules increase demand for trusted European component channels; the risk is that the company’s decentralised structure and incomplete supplier data prevent consistent execution across 39 operating units Compliance could strengthen OEM’s moat.

Section 15 – Value chain: supply side

Section summary
  1. OEM International’s supplier network remains diversified and relationship-led, but supply economics have become less transparent just as growth is consuming more inventory and cash; the Q3 working-capital outflow therefore raises a greater EPS risk than management’s record order intake suggests.
1.
Supplier concentration, apparently low: OEM reported more than 400 suppliers and no supplier representing more than 3% of Group sales, while Redeye identified the same 3% ceiling for 2024 and described supplier relationships as long-lasting; this is more diversified than a single-source model, although subsidiary-level dependence can still be materially higher than the Group disclosure implies.
2.
Local dependence, underappreciated: OEM acknowledged that dependence on individual suppliers remains a key operational risk for individual subsidiaries, even though many suppliers serve several markets; this means the Group-level 3% concentration statistic may understate the effect of losing a specialist supplier in a particular product niche, where replacement qualification could delay sales and weaken customer relationships.
3.
Asian sourcing, limited visibility: DNB Carnegie’s 2025 review concluded that Asia was probably OEM’s most important sourcing region, followed by Europe and the United States, while many components were manufactured only in Asia and available non-Asian alternatives could be lower quality; OEM’s current disclosures do not quantify regional purchasing or dual-sourcing coverage, leaving tariff, geopolitical and lead-time exposure difficult to model.
4.
Dual sourcing, incomplete: OEM has stated that it works to identify alternative suppliers for key components, but DNB Carnegie reported that many components still had only Asian production options; compared with Bufab’s disclosed emphasis on dual sourcing, supplier audits and consignment arrangements, OEM provides less evidence that resilience measures are sufficiently advanced to protect gross margin and service levels through the next disruption. Supplier relationships, economically valuable: OEM’s 2025 business-model material describes long-term dialogue, loyalty, product-development cooperation and a policy of avoiding competing products, which can secure supplier commitment and protect access to specialised components; the trade-off is that exclusivity and relationship depth may reduce substitution flexibility if a supplier raises prices, reallocates capacity or develops a direct sales channel. Pricing protection, weaker than assumed: DNB Carnegie found that most OEM supplier and customer contracts do not contain fixed prices or inflation clauses, so OEM generally owns the inventory and must pass cost changes through to customers or renegotiate case by case; this leaves a timing risk between supplier price increases and customer repricing, which could compress gross margin and EPS even if demand remains strong.
5.
Inventory risk, rising again: OEM’s Q3 report showed a SEK 55 million inventory build and a total working- capital outflow of SEK 155 million, while operating cash flow fell to SEK 88 million from SEK 179 million despite record EBITA; this is the opposite of the pre-results expectation that strong earnings and order conversion would support cash-funded growth, and it makes inventory turns and obsolescence key indicators for 2027 EPS.
6.
Working-capital normalisation, not yet demonstrated: DNB Carnegie previously expected OEM’s workingcapital-to-sales ratio to decline from approximately 26% in 2024 toward 24% in 2027, based on the view that no structural change prevented a return toward pre-pandemic inventory economics; the Q3 inventory build and weaker cash conversion do not yet confirm that forecast, so the broker’s cash-funding assumption is more demanding than the latest delivery.
7.
Logistics, renewed cost exposure: External supply-chain indicators reported that Asia–North Europe freight rates were approximately 25% above the prior year in late summer 2026, while capacity was being constrained by port congestion, cancelled sailings and higher diesel costs; OEM’s logistics-intensive model can absorb part of this through customer pricing, but the absence of quantified freight sensitivity leaves a possible grossmargin drag outside the published EPS model.
8.
Supplier compliance, material gap: OEM’s 2025 sustainability report said its Supplier Code of Conduct had been signed by only approximately 50% of strategic suppliers, against an ambition for all strategic suppliers, while supplier assessments covered 200 suppliers representing 82% of purchase volume; this leaves a meaningful portion of the upstream chain insufficiently verified for labour, environmental and governance risks that could disrupt supply or exclude OEM from customer programmes.
9.
Traceability, still incomplete: OEM identified limited upstream transparency, insufficient material traceability and incomplete mapping of hazardous substances as areas requiring improvement, while its resilience assessment highlighted production stoppages, price fluctuations and future regulation as vulnerabilities; these risks may not affect Q3 EBITA, but remediation, redesign or supplier replacement could raise working capital and operating costs over the next three years.
10.
Supplier financial health, unquantified: OEM discloses supplier diversification and long-term relationships but does not report supplier failure rates, payment-term changes, supplier returns on capital or the financial condition of critical vendors; unlike the Group’s reported 24.7% trailing return on equity, the economics of the upstream supplier base are therefore not observable, limiting confidence that suppliers can absorb inflation and maintain investment without passing through higher prices.
11.
Peer benchmark, stronger disclosure elsewhere: Beijer Ref reports a strategy of working with major strategic suppliers while maintaining at least two suppliers in each market and price segment, whereas OEM discloses no comparable minimum dual-sourcing standard; this does not prove OEM is less resilient, but it shows that its public evidence on substitution capacity is weaker than a named distribution peer’s. / CSR reports Peer benchmark, better supply-chain operating tools: Bufab describes supplier consolidation, dual sourcing, demand forecasting, machine-learning planning and consignment stock as measures intended to shorten lead times and improve net working capital, while OEM’s disclosed Q3 response was an inventory build and a broad explanation of receivables timing; the comparison suggests OEM may be carrying more of the resilience burden on its own balance sheet.
12.
Nearshoring, two-sided EPS effect: OEM’s European supplier footprint provides some protection against Asian labour, regulatory and geopolitical risks, but the company also acknowledges that Asian upstream suppliers can create exposure to working-condition failures, production stoppages and price volatility; over the next three years, nearshoring may improve availability and reduce freight risk, but alternative European sourcing could carry higher purchase prices and reduce gross margin unless customers accept the premium.
13.
Other side of the story: The supply-side risk may be overstated if the Q3 inventory build was temporary stock positioned against the strong backlog, if receivables normalise in Q4 and if OEM’s long-standing supplier relationships secure priority allocation during tight markets; however, that benign interpretation would require inventory-to-sales, payable terms and operating cash flow to improve in the next report, because the latest quarter provided no evidence that the expected working-capital normalisation has begun.

Section 16 – Conclusion

Section summary
  1. The 15 October 2026 earnings changed OEM International’s outlook from a recovery story supported mainly by acquisitions into a stronger near-term organic-growth story, lifting documented 2026–28 earnings expectations while leaving cash conversion, succession and valuation as the decisive tests.
1.
Outlook upgrade, substantial: OEM’s Q3 sales growth of 24%, organic growth of 14% and adjusted EBITA growth of 25% exceeded DNB Carnegie’s pre-results expectations of 16% sales growth, 7% organic growth and 19% adjusted EBITA growth, while adjusted EBITA beat Bloomberg consensus by 4%; the delivery therefore raised the earnings base rather than merely confirming the recovery already priced in.The earnings base moved higher.
2.
EPS reset, durable near term: DNB Carnegie raised adjusted earnings per share to SEK 5.79 for 2026, SEK 6.39 for 2027 and SEK 6.85 for 2028 from SEK 5.49, SEK 6.03 and SEK 6.47, respectively, implying increases of approximately 5%, 6% and 6%; the revisions incorporate stronger organic growth, ABSSAC, foreign exchange, improved net financials and lower minority leakage.EPS rose across the forecast period .
3.
Expectation deviation, broader than consensus: Q3 revenue of SEK 1,582 million exceeded DNB Carnegie’s pre-results forecast of SEK 1,490 million by 6%, adjusted EBITA of SEK 264 million exceeded its SEK 254 million consensus reference by 4%, and reported EPS of SEK 1.44 exceeded consensus of SEK 1.29, although the complete market forecast distribution and private investor expectations were not observable.The beat reached every key measure.
4.
Margin qualification, important: The earnings beat did not validate the full pre-results operating-leverage thesis, because adjusted EBITA margin was 16.7% against consensus of 17.0% and reported EBITA margin was 16.5% against 16.6% a year earlier; higher earnings therefore came chiefly from volume, organic growth and mix, not a decisive structural margin step-up.Growth beat while margins lagged.
5.
Order evidence, strongest positive change: Incoming orders rose 40% to SEK 1,683 million, like-for-like orders including currency rose 33%, and the book-to-bill ratio reached 1.06 times against a three-year Q3 average of 0.94 times, exceeding the pre-results expectation of seasonal normalisation and giving DNB Carnegie grounds to model 32% adjusted EBITA growth for Q4.Orders made the beat more durable.
6.
Demand breadth, improved conviction: Sweden and Finland, the Baltics and China each delivered 11% organic growth, while Rest of Europe delivered 25%, so the result was stronger and broader than the pre-results concern that OEM might depend on a single region or acquisition; however, Rest of Europe’s 25% organic growth was accompanied by 19% acquired growth, and its order intake included a SEK 117 million Irish project.Organic momentum was geographically broad.
7.
Cash conversion, principal unresolved negative: Operating cash flow fell to SEK 88 million from SEK 179 million despite record EBITA, as working capital consumed SEK 155 million through a SEK 55 million inventory build and a SEK 126 million receivables increase; the company attributed the receivables movement to a limited number of larger units, but supplied no quantified normalisation timetable.Cash failed to follow earnings.
8.
Three-year EPS quality, conditional: The higher 2028 EPS estimate of SEK 6.85 is not yet supported by the quarter’s cash generation, because DNB Carnegie’s longer-term valuation assumes 5% annual organic growth, 12% annual acquisition-driven EBITA growth through 2033, 65% free-cash-flow-to-EBITA conversion and continued reinvestment into acquisitions.Long-term EPS still depends on M&A.
9.
Valuation consequence, modest rerating: DNB Carnegie raised its target price only to SEK 210 from SEK 205 despite the 5–6% EPS upgrades, retained BUY and continued to value OEM at approximately 22 times 2027 estimated EV/EBITA, indicating that the broker recognised better earnings while allowing the higher forecast to do much of the valuation work.Target price rose less than EPS.
10.
Premium valuation, still exposed: The SEK 210 target is based on a 50/50 blend of a SEK 174 peer valuation and a SEK 245 M&A-scenario valuation, implying 25.3 times 2027 estimated EV/EBITA; half the target therefore depends on future acquisitions that were not generated by Q3 and that management has not quantified in a formal earnings roadmap.Half the valuation depends on acquisitions.
11.
Internal change, execution burden higher: EQT’s ownership has accelerated the acquisition agenda, OEM has added group-level M&A capacity and completed multiple transactions, and ABSSAC provides a UK motioncontrol platform with only a marginal current-year earnings contribution; the Q3 beat was therefore primarily organic, but the three-year EPS case now requires the faster portfolio strategy to work without weakening the decentralised culture.The strategy now carries greater execution risk.
12.
External change, supportive but selective: European industrial demand and capital-goods activity improved enough to support OEM’s organic acceleration, but construction remained weak in Sweden and Finland and external automation evidence pointed to selective, return-focused customer projects rather than a broad spending boom; the evidence supports a higher 12-to-18-month EPS path, not extrapolation of 14% organic growth through three years.The cycle supports recovery, not extrapolation.
13.
Single most important change, organic conversion: The most important change is that OEM demonstrated 14% organic sales growth and 31% organic order growth when DNB Carnegie had expected 7% organic sales growth and a more normal book-to-bill, shifting the investment case from “EQT may eventually create growth through M&A” toward “the existing businesses are already converting demand and gaining share.” Organic execution became the central thesis.
14.
Strongest case against this conclusion, timing and quality: The strongest counter-case is that the quarter was flattered by an Irish order, unusually strong Rest-of-Europe activity, positive currency and backlog conversion, while cash flow deteriorated and margins failed to expand as expected; if Q4 organic growth falls back toward the broker’s 5–6% through-cycle assumption without cash normalisation, the Q3 beat may prove cyclical rather than structural.A temporary quarter could be misread.
15.
WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND, specific test: A change of view would require the next interim report, scheduled for 18 February 2027, to show Q4 sales and order conversion broadly consistent with DNB Carnegie’s SEK 1,696 million sales and 32% adjusted EBITA-growth assumptions, while operating cash flow improves materially from SEK 88 million and receivables and inventory stop consuming cash; failure on either demand or cash would overturn the upgraded EPS interpretation.Q4 must prove both growth and cash.
16.
Consensus-ignored reading, plausible but unproven: The non-obvious reading is that OEM may be transitioning from a cash-rich but under-deployed distributor into a higher-growth compounder before it has demonstrated the cash conversion required for that label, meaning the market could be underestimating working-capital intensity even as it focuses on organic growth and acquisition capacity.The hidden risk is funding quality.
17.
Next disclosure question, demand: The next report should answer how much of Q4 revenue came from the SEK 117 million Irish project and the existing SEK 1,386 million backlog, and whether order growth remains broad after that project is delivered; without backlog margin, cancellation and delivery information, order quantity remains an incomplete guide to future EPS.How repeatable is the order surge.
18.
Next disclosure question, cash and leadership: The next report should also disclose whether receivables and inventory normalised, whether operating cash flow recovered toward EBITA growth, and who will succeed Jörgen Zahlin as CEO, because finance discipline and cultural continuity are the two missing conditions for converting DNB Carnegie’s higher 2027–28 EPS estimates into a credible three-year investment case. Cash recovery and succession are decisive.
19.
Other side of the story: OEM’s Q3 may ultimately justify an even higher valuation if organic growth remains above the 5–6% through-cycle assumption, Q4 converts the backlog without another working-capital shock and EQT funds acquisitions at disciplined returns, but the current evidence supports a higher earnings trajectory more strongly than it supports the premium multiple required by the bullish M&A scenario.The earnings improved before the proof was complete.
