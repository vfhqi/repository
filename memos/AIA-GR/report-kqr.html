Executive Summary

Section summary
  1. The key question surrounding Athens International Airport's 17% share rally is partially resolvable through existing regulatory framework documentation and broker consensus models, though critical uncertainties remain regarding terminal value treatment as the 2046 concession expiration approaches and the mechanics of potential extension negotiations. The evidence demonstrates that Athens International Airport's share price appreciation to near the top of its tenyear multiple range was driven by a €1.3 billion (subsequently restructured to €950 million) expansion program targeting 40 million passenger capacity by 2032, operating within a dual-till regulatory regime capped at 15% cumulative return on equity. The analysis reveals a fundamental tension between viewing the asset as a compoundable infrastructure growth story versus recognizing it as potentially the most stretched valuation profile in the European airport sector given its finite concession horizon. The regulatory framework provides highly predictable dual-till return protection, with regulated Air Activities subject to a 15% maximum cumulative return cap while unregulated Non-Air commercial streams contain uncapped earning potential. However, a critical structural limitation emerges: capital expenditure incurred for Air activities expansion enters regulated charges at cost through depreciation and interest recovery but earns zero incremental return on equity under article 14.13 regulations. This mechanism fundamentally constrains the ability of the €950 million modular expansion to generate additional equity returns, distinguishing Athens International Airport from traditional regulated asset base growth models employed by utility infrastructure operators. Following the expiration of temporary 30% passenger fee discounts on April 30, 2026, aeronautical revenue yields began normalizing in the second half of 2026, with the carry-forward balance rebuilding to €16.0 million by June 2026. Strong first-half 2026 passenger traffic reaching 15.75 million passengers (+4.5% year-over-year)

prompted management to upgrade full-year traffic guidance from low-single-digit to mid-single-digit percentage growth. The revenue trajectory is increasingly dependent on unregulated commercial expansion, with Non-Air Activities generating €80.4 million revenue in H1 2026 and surpassing 50% of total company profits for the first time, maintaining an exceptional 80.5% Adjusted EBITDA margin.

The valuation landscape reveals stark divergence between bullish and bearish interpretations. Athens International Airport trades at approximately 9.0x forward EV/EBITDA, representing a discount to European peers Aena (10.4x-11.3x) and Fraport (10.3x), yet this multiple sits near the 97th percentile of its own post-listing historical range. The critical distinction lies in concession duration: Athens International Airport operates under a fixed concession expiring in June 2046, whereas peer Fraport holds perpetual Frankfurt assets alongside international concessions extending to 2057.

Broker models enforce a finite cash flow horizon ending in 2046 with zero terminal value credited post-concession, creating steep cash flow discounting pressure. The restructured modular expansion introduces execution de-risking but simultaneously pushes capacity relief further into the concession timeline. The September 2026 board decision restructured the terminal expansion from a single-step €1.30 billion plan into a €950 million modular program through 2030, improving short-term flexibility while preserving the ultimate 40 million passenger capacity target.

However, Morgan Stanley cautioned that the revised timeline pushes the terminal opening to the right, thereby diluting expected mid-term commercial revenue uplift.

Material data gaps constrain definitive resolution of the key question. Uncertainties remain regarding unconfirmed technical approvals for modular expansion phases and the untested legal mechanics of unamortized debt transfers to the Greek State at the 2046 concession expiry. The most critical unresolved variable concerns concession extension optionality: broker models currently assign zero net present value to a potential concession extension beyond 2046, treating 2046 as a hard cutoff, yet re-rating the asset as an infrastructure growth play hinges on state incentives to unlock concession extension agreements, with Greece possessing structural means through legislative amendments under Law 5080/2024 to extend the timeline.

Cross-sector transport concession precedents provide directional signals but not definitive answers. Vinci's subsidiary ANA demonstrated that proposing an €8.5 billion capital expenditure plan enabled negotiating a 30- year airport concession extension to 2092 without direct state subsidies, suggesting a potential template for Athens International Airport. However, the absence of formal negotiations or disclosed state intentions leaves this pathway entirely speculative.

The analysis ultimately reveals that resolving whether Athens International Airport represents sustainable asset growth or mispriced terminal value decay depends critically on three unobservable factors: the Greek State's willingness to grant early concession extensions in exchange for privately funded capacity expansion; the exact mechanics of terminal compensation and debt transfer at expiration; and whether current peak multiples reflect permanent re-rating or technical inflows following Greece's Developed Market reclassification.

Broker research demonstrates stark divergence, with Deutsche Bank maintaining a Buy rating based on de-risked regulatory return protection and inflation hedging, while Morgan Stanley reiterates Underweight citing peak multiples yielding under 7% equity internal rate of return and exposure to unmitigated terminal value decay as the 2046 concession end approaches. Without clarity on extension negotiations or state infrastructure strategy, investors face a binary outcome: either the concession is extended and current multiples are justified by extended cash flow duration, or the 20-year remaining term triggers accelerating multiple compression as finite-life economics dominate valuation frameworks.

Section 2 – Regulatory framework and 15% return cap mechanics

Section summary
  1. Athens International Airport's Airport Development Agreement provides highly predictable dual-till return protection capped at 15% inflation-indexed equity return, though aeronautical expansion capital expenditure expands cost recovery without generating incremental equity returns.

Airport Development Agreement structure and dual-till allocation

1.
Statutory concession framework: The Airport Development Agreement ratified under Greek Law 2338/1995 and amended by Law 5080/2024 establishes a unique ad hoc regulatory regime operating through June 2046.
2.
Dual-till operational split: The framework enforces a dual-till structure separating regulated Air Activities from unregulated Non-Air commercial streams containing uncapped commercial earning potential.
3.
Air activities cost coverage: Regulated charges cover operational expenses, depreciation, and allocated concession debt service while ensuring a capped 15% maximum cumulative return.
4.
Peer regulatory insulation: Unlike European peers subject to five-year regulatory resets, Deutsche Bank's 5 August 2026 report emphasizes that Athens International Airport operates without periodic regulatory price-cap reviews.
5.
Commercial margin flexibility: Non-Air activities generate high-margin rental and parking revenues reaching over 80% EBITDA margin without regulatory profit sharing constraints.

Return cap mechanics, inflation indexation, and capex inclusion

6.
Paid-in equity base definition: The 15% return cap applies not to traditional Regulated Asset Base figures but to 300 million euros initial equity adjusted annually by European Union Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices inflation.
7.
Expansion capex return limit: Capital expenditure incurred for Air activities expansion enters regulated charges at cost through depreciation and interest recovery but earns zero incremental return on equity under article 14.13 regulations.
8.
Carry-forward cushion recovery: Shortfalls in allowed regulatory returns accumulate in a carry-forward account adjusted for inflation, which stood at 16 million euros as of June 2026 following temporary fee cuts.
9.
Excess return clawback rule: If cumulative Air returns exceed 15% in three of four consecutive financial periods, excess profit must be refunded to the Greek State within 45 days or passed through lower passenger fees.
10.
Broker valuation divergence: Deutsche Bank's 10 September 2026 note rates the stock Buy for built-in inflation hedging, while Morgan Stanley's 9 September 2026 report rates it Underweight citing unrewarded capital deployment risks during heavy terminal expansion phases.
11.
Other side of the story: Sceptical analysis indicates that because expansion capex does not lift the allowed equity profit ceiling, large capital outlays consume cash flow while leaving equity holders exposed to diminishing return on invested capital as the 2046 concession expiration approaches.
