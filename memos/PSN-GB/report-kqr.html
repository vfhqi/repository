These limits are designed to target support towards lowerincome households and prevent higher-earners from accessing government-backed equity loans. This tighter calibration is expected to narrow the eligible buyer pool compared to the previous Help to Buy scheme which had no income restrictions. Consequently, the scheme's ability to drive incremental sales will depend heavily on how local price thresholds are set.

Government figures also specify outer London price caps at 500,000 pounds and inner London caps at 650,000 pounds. These caps are intended to align with localauthority median transaction prices rather than new-build premiums. Therefore, developers with lower average selling prices will find it much easier to qualify for government demand support.

Section 3 – Bank of England LTI Limits and Lender Adoption at 6% Rates

Section summary
  1. The Bank of England Financial Policy Committee has deferred standard threshold increases, passing proposals to future housing reviews.
  2. High mortgage rates near 6% create severe serviceability constraints that prevent widespread lender adoption of 5.0 times earnings limits.
  3. Underwriting criteria and steep stress tests remain the primary barriers to entry-level demand, keeping volume recovery slow.

Regulatory Framework and Decisions

1.
Regulatory flexibility: The Bank of England has altered its approach to loan-to-income limits to allow individual lenders more operational leeway. The Financial Policy Committee recommended in July 2025 that the Prudential Regulation Authority and the Financial Conduct Authority remove firm-specific caps while maintaining an aggregate 15% limit. This shift allows active lenders to expand high loan-to-income lending above the historical fifteen percent limit as long as the market aggregate remains compliant. This structural shift aims to support homebuilders' volumes by unlocking credit capacity.
2.
Delayed threshold review: Regulatory authorities have deferred decisions on raising the official high loan-to- income threshold from 4.5 to 5.0 times earnings. In the Policy Statement CP6/26 feedback published on 14 October 2026, regulators noted that several respondents urged an increase to 5.0 times. However, the authorities passed these suggestions to the Financial Policy Committee for its next regular housing review as they were out of scope for the immediate consultation. This delay means that housebuilders cannot expect immediate legislative relief on standard income multiples.
3.
Decision timeline: The Bank of England is expected to evaluate the threshold rules during its upcoming macroprudential reviews without a fixed deadline. The Financial Policy Committee regularly assesses systemic mortgage risks and has integrated the industry feedback into its ongoing policy cycles. While no specific decision date has been confirmed, any statutory shift to a 5.0 times limit will require prior coordination with retail regulators. Therefore, developers like Persimmon face a prolonged period of uncertainty regarding standard borrow limits which will delay volume gains.
4.
Contrary evidence: Some major mortgage providers are already bypassing standard thresholds by offering massive income multiples to qualified buyers. Nationwide Building Society has successfully utilized its Helping Hand scheme to allow first-time buyers to borrow up to six times their household income. This initiative has supported nearly 60,000 buyers since 2021, proving that selective high leverage is already functional. Consequently, the formal regulatory threshold is less of an absolute barrier than broad market consensus assumes.

Financial analysts at Rothschild and Co Redburn reported on 1 October 2026 that a standard buyer at 95% leverage faces annual mortgage costs of 39% of post-tax income. While raising limits to 5.0 times earnings increases borrowing capacity, it simultaneously forces unsustainable monthly debt service obligations on households. Therefore, lenders are highly unlikely to approve these extreme multiples while mortgage rates remain near 6%.
