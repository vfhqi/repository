Section 2 – Ocean Segment Cost Structure and Disclosed Splits

Container liners carry high fixed network costs that expose margins to severe downside when spot freight rates decline. Accounting useful-life extensions mask underlying cash burn by optically reducing depreciation and boosting reported operating earnings. High-cost charter profiles create massive negative operating leverage for select peers as global vessel capacity expands.

Cost classification and shipping cost splits

1.
Classifying fleet expenses: Expert analysis of container liner expenses reveals that operating structures carry substantial asset-backed leverage. Historical data over the past 30 years shows that approximately 50% of shipping costs per TEU are volume-driven, 35% are fleetrelated, and 15% are overhead-related. This means that while direct cargo handling costs scale with activity, fixed vessel and network expenses dominate during cyclical normalisations.
2.
Analyzing fuel volatility: Bunker fuel represents a highly volatile component of the voyage-related variable cost base. Deutsche Bank Research's report of 29 September 2026 states that bunker fuel typically accounts for around 14% of container shippers' overall cost structures. This cost line can fluctuate widely based on crude prices and rerouting networks, thereby impacting voyage-related cash outflows.
3.
Disclosing cost splits: Maersk's Q1 2026 disclosed Ocean operating cost split highlights the heavy weight of local terminal operations. The company's interim report shows terminal handling at 39%, network costs excluding bunker at 27%, bunker at 19%, and SG&A at 9% of total Ocean operating expenses. These figures demonstrate that terminal handling represents the largest variable cost component per box.

Comparative peer cost structures

4.
Comparing peer spending: Hapag-Lloyd's reported transport cost breakdown in Q2 2026 aligns closely with Maersk's high terminal spending. Out of USD 1.27bn in total transport expenses, Hapag-Lloyd reported handling and haulage of USD 638m, bunker and emissions of USD 258m, and vessel costs of USD 215m. This confirms that terminal handling represents over 50% of Hapag-Lloyd's direct transport expenses, verifying Maersk's cost split.
5.
Detailing voyage allocations: COSCO Shipping's interim financial reporting indicates a massive nominal allocation to fuel and voyage expenses. In the first half of 2026, COSCO reported bunker costs of RMB 10.55bn alongside crew expenses of RMB 1.36bn. This heavy fuel exposure means that COSCO remains highly sensitive to fuel price volatility despite its lower relative SG&A expense.
6.
Evaluating fleet models: ZIM Integrated Shipping operates on an asymmetric cost model that carries extreme operating leverage compared to peers. The company chartered-in 87.5% of its operating fleet as of December 31, 2025, which is significantly higher than the industry average of 37.6%. This charter-heavy model raises operational breakevens during downturns because charter lease commitments remain contractually fixed for multi-year periods.
7.
Highlighting sticky leases: ZIM's detailed cost disclosures highlight that chartering and bunkering absorb the vast majority of its revenues. Bunkering costs alone accounted for 25.7% of ZIM's total operating expenses and cost of services in 2025. This means that unless charter rates collapse, ZIM faces severe margin compression because slot purchases and short-term charter hire remain highly sticky.
8.
Benchmarking regional feeders: Feeder operator cost structures show similar terminal handling and fuel dependencies to global liners. Jefferies Research notes that Malaysian feeder operator MTT Shipping's cost structure was dominated by terminal handling, bunkers, and equipment costs which collectively averaged over 42% of total costs. This comparison indicates that terminal fees remain highly inflexible across both global and regional vessel networks.

Useful-life accounting changes and depreciation

9.
Altering vessel depreciation: Maersk's decision to extend its vessel depreciation timeline provides a massive optical benefit to reported operating income. Effective 1 January 2026, the company increased the estimated useful lives of its vessels from 20 to 25 years. This change reduced reported depreciation expense by USD 175m in Q2 2026 alone, which directly inflates EBIT without generating cash.
10.
Lowering annual depreciation: The extended useful life policy substantially alters Maersk's corporate profitability guidance for the full year. Deutsche Bank Research's report of 5 February 2026 states that this accounting change lowers annual depreciation by USD 700m in 2026. Consequently, Maersk's underlying operating profit looks optically stronger than peers because depreciation charges are deferred further into the future.
11.
Reviewing industry standards: The revision of vessel economic life is not unique to Maersk, as other regional operators have enacted matching policies. Uni-Asia Group reported that it revised its estimated vessel useful lives from 20 to 25 years effective 1 January 2026. This parallel adjustment indicates that liners are collectively extending asset lives to cushion their reported earnings ahead of a global shipping downturn.
12.
Contrasting Japanese timelines: Japanese shipping companies maintain far more conservative depreciation policies compared to their Western counterparts. Jefferies Research's report of 1 September 2021 highlights that Japanese lines use depreciation periods of 12 to 15 years versus 25 years for Hong Kong operators. This means that Japanese liners understate optical earnings but generate superior cash flow protections through accelerated tax shields.

Impairment risks and environmental regulations

13.
Stiffening compliance pressure: Stricter global maritime regulations threaten to shorten the actual economic viability of older containership fleets. Bernstein Research's report of 3 February 2025 estimates that approximately 45% of the current global maritime fleet is at risk of non-compliance with the IMO's Carbon Intensity Indicator. This regulatory pressure means that vessel economic lives may fall short of the newly adopted 25-year accounting assumptions.
14.
Contrary evidence: Some shipping operators argue that vessel modernization and retrofitting can economically extend useful lives even under tighter environmental rules. Bernstein Research's report of 3 February 2025 notes that retrofitting energy-saving devices can extend compliance by 5 to 8 years for a modest cost of USD 1m to USD 5m. This implies that useful-life extensions of 25 years are technologically feasible, which reduces the immediate risk of asset impairments.
15.
Escalating write-down risks: Depressed charter rates during overcapacity cycles materially escalate the risk of large-scale asset write-downs. Ardmore Shipping's report of 8 April 2026 highlights that subjective impairment assumptions are highly sensitive to declining charter rates. If the industry faces prolonged overcapacity, Maersk faces significant impairment risks because the carrying value of its 25-year assets may exceed their recoverable amounts.
16.
Other side of the story: While extending vessel useful lives to 25 years improves near-term reported profitability, it dramatically increases the risk of holding obsolete, stranded assets as decarbonization mandates accelerate. DNB Carnegie argues that stricter environmental standards will expand the earnings gap between modern dual-fuel vessels and older tonnage, putting downward pressure on residual asset values. Consequently, Maersk's accounting extension poses long-term balance sheet risks because future impairment charges will likely wipe out the optical depreciation savings recorded in 2026.

Section 3 – Margin Dynamics and Capacity Management in Historical Cycles

Section summary
  1. Container liners exhibit extreme negative operating leverage during market normalisations when spot freight rates decline faster than rigid network costs.
  2. Capacity withdrawal tools such as blank sailings and slow steaming provide short-term rate support but fail to offset massive structural vessel deliveries.

Historical performance in container downturns

1.
Margin compression history: During the 2015-2016 downturn, Maersk Line experienced severe pressure as global container demand slumped. Maersk's annual report for 2016 highlights that Maersk Line recorded an underlying loss of USD 155m in 2016 compared to a profit of USD 1.3bn in 2015. Although unit costs decreased by 11.5% to USD 2,288/FFE due to lower bunkers, average loaded freight rates fell further to USD 1,804/FFE. This shows that network costs cannot be cut fast enough to preserve earnings because ship operations carry high asset-backed leverage.
2.
Peer margin volatility: Hapag-Lloyd also demonstrated intense profitability declines during the 2015-2016 cyclical trough. Hapag-Lloyd's 2016 earnings call transcript reports that average freight rates dropped 15% to USD 1,036/TEU, resulting in a disappointing EBIT of USD 140m. This compares with an EBIT of EUR 366.4m in 2015 when average rates stood at USD 1,225/TEU. Consequently, both partners faced significant earnings compression because vessel deployment costs remained sticky.
3.
State-backed resilience: COSCO Shipping Holdings exhibited extreme losses during the 2016 downturn due to high spot rate exposure. COSCO's interim financial report for 2016 reveals a net loss of RMB 9.22bn for the first nine months of 2016. Average container freight rates slipped 12.4% to RMB 3,995 per TEU as market supply overwhelmed demand. This confirms that even state-backed liners face severe capital destruction when spot rates converge on marginal costs.

Stable baseline year: The container shipping sector achieved temporary margin stability in 2019 through strict capacity discipline and cargo selection. Maersk's annual report for 2019 states that Ocean segment EBITDA increased to USD 4.4bn with a margin of 15.3%. Similarly, Hapag-Lloyd reported an EBITDA margin of 15.8% with average freight rates at USD 1,072/TEU. Rapid normalization crash: The 2023 normalization triggered a historical collapse in freight rates as post-pandemic congestion cleared. High-leverage distress: ZIM Integrated Shipping's charterheavy model generated severe financial distress during the 2023 market normalization. Capacity management and operational levers

6.
Chronic industry overcapacity: Container carriers have historically exacerbated downturns by failing to execute collective capacity discipline. Jefferies Research's note of 13 January 2019 points out that lines often inflict self-harm through market share battles. As global demand slows, lines prioritize filling vessels over rate stability, which delays necessary capacity adjustments. This operational bias means that EBIT margins compress rapidly in downcycles unless alliances enforce coordinated vessel idling. Tactical blank sailings: Shipping lines rely heavily on blank sailings as a primary tactical lever to temporarily absorb excess capacity. A shipping expert call from 4 February 2026 highlights that blank sailings rose past 50% on key Eastbound transpacific trades. This tactical maneuver attempts to defend pricing and contract rates by restricting immediate slot availability. However, blanking sailings raises unit costs per carried container because vessel voyage costs are spread over fewer boxes. Delayed fleet adjustments: Returning chartered tonnage is a highly restricted lever due to multi-year lease obligations. DNB Carnegie's report of 4 May 2026 notes that liners must wait for contracts to lapse before returning charter-in vessels. This slow phase-out pressures freight rates further as high-cost tonnage remains operational. Consequently, carriers cannot quickly adjust fleet sizes when charter durations lock in peak rates. Slow scrapping response: Vessel demolition provides no immediate relief for oversupplied shipping markets during prolonged downturns. DNB Carnegie's report of 4 May 2026 notes that scrapping is a slow process because of carriers' strong cash reserves. Shippers are highly reluctant to scrap vessels early in a downcycle, hoping for a demand rebound. This means that excess capacity continues to depress margins until sustained cash burn forces capitulation.

Sensitivity modeling and market asymmetry

7.
High operational leverage: Maersk carries high operating sensitivity to freight rates, making modeling extremely volatile. Deutsche Bank Research's report of 3 June 2024 states that Maersk represents a highly levered play where a minor 4% freight rate change translates into a USD 1.0bn EBIT swing. This sensitivity exposes earnings per share to extreme volatility. Consequently, minor spot rate shifts dominate earnings unless contracts shield the baseline. Divergent sensitivity assumptions: Broker sensitivity estimates differ from Maersk's official guidance, raising modeling complexity. Maersk's Q2 2026 slides state that a USD 100/FFE rate change alters annual EBIT by USD 700m. However, Deutsche Bank Research on 3 June 2024 models a much higher sensitivity of USD 1.3bn per USD 100/FFE rate change. This divergence suggests that brokers assume higher spot rate exposure than Maersk's contract-heavy baseline reflects. Contrary evidence: Some analysts argue that terminal constraints act as a permanent support buffer, reducing sensitivity to oversupply. Bernstein's note of 25 September 2026 highlights that Maersk expects terminal capacity bottlenecks to support freight rates even during rapid fleet growth. If correct, this structural constraint would prevent severe margin compression, meaning sensitivities may be far lower in practice.
8.
Other side of the story: Operating sensitivities are highly asymmetric because customer contract compliance breaks down in falling spot markets. DNB Carnegie argues that while rising rates are immediately passed through via surcharges, falling spot rates force carriers to renegotiate long-term contracts early to prevent customer attrition. Consequently, Maersk's sensitivities underestimate the pace of downcycle margin collapse because surcharge revenues drop far faster than contractual rate protections can support.
