Pool operators like Hafnia, Maersk Tankers, and the DiaNor partnership do not publicly disclose the precise breakdown of how administrative overhead, commercial brokerage commissions, and technical management costs are allocated across participating vessel owners.

This opacity makes it impossible to accurately benchmark whether integrated inhouse platforms like TORM's One TORM model truly carry higher structural costs than outsourced pool alternatives after adjusting for hidden fees, profit-sharing dilution, and commercial execution quality. Section 8 – If the Answer Differs from the Common View

The consensus view among shipping analysts and market participants assumes that TORM and its peers can navigate cyclical downturns through their refined operating platforms and progressively lower cash breakeven thresholds achieved through refinancing and cost discipline. However, if this key assumption proves incorrect–specifically if fixed costs prove stickier than anticipated or freight rates collapse to historical trough levels –the implications would cascade through the entire product tanker value chain with severe consequences for equity valuations, operational viability, and industry structure.

The most immediate risk centers on the inflexibility of TORM's integrated operating platform. TORM's direct seafarer pool of 3,819 crew members represents a permanent cost baseline that is highly unfeasible to cut materially without severely compromising vessel performance. If spot rates decline sharply below USD 16,200 per day, TORM's current cash breakeven, the company would face rapid cash drain that cannot be offset through near-term cost reductions.

Historical tanker cycles show that fixed crewing, technical maintenance, and shoreside administrative expenses cannot be easily downscaled, meaning platforms with higher daily operational cost bases will experience accelerated earnings contraction and cash drain once spot rates retreat to historical mid-cycle levels. Unlike outsourced pool models that can adjust capacity or renegotiate third-party management fees, TORM's in-house structure locks in these obligations regardless of market conditions.

The knock-on effects on crew suppliers would be particularly severe in a prolonged downturn scenario. TORM maintained a pool of 3,819 seafarers, with the majority of vessel staff on short-term contracts, keeping overhead expenses highly fixed even when spot market volumes drop. If rates remain depressed for multiple consecutive quarters, TORM and peers would be forced to terminate crew contracts en masse, flooding the maritime labor market with displaced officers and ratings.

This oversupply would depress crew wages temporarily but would also create severe crew retention problems when the market eventually recovers, as skilled seafarers migrate to more stable shore-based positions or alternative maritime sectors. Manning agencies in the Philippines, India, and Eastern Europe that specialize in product tanker crew supply would face immediate revenue collapse, potentially forcing consolidation or bankruptcy among smaller providers.

Major charterers and oil refiners represent another critical stakeholder group whose behavior could deviate sharply from current expectations. The consensus assumes that product tanker demand will remain resilient due to structural ton-mile growth and supply constraints. However, if global refined product demand weakens materially due to economic recession or accelerated electrification of transportation, charterers would gain immediate pricing power.

Product tanker owners are largely successful in passing regional environmental compliance surcharges directly to charterers through contractual agreements that legally permit shipowners to pass EU ETS costs to the charterers responsible for voyage routing and fuel consumption. Yet directly passing environmental compliance costs to freight rates is highly difficult for spot market voyages, meaning owners operating on spot contracts must absorb regional carbon liabilities until structural market rates rise.

In a sustained downturn, charterers would resist environmental surcharges entirely, forcing owners to absorb escalating EU ETS and FuelEU Maritime penalties that could reach several thousand dollars per operating day for older non-eco vessels.

At carbon allowance prices of approximately EUR 70 per ton, a Panamax vessel on a US-EU voyage incurs daily costs of USD 2,100 under the 50% emissions threshold, which doubles to USD 4,200 per operating day for intra- European voyages. If carbon prices surge beyond EUR 100 per ton while spot rates collapse, the combined regulatory and operational cost burden would render older tonnage commercially unviable in European trades.

Owners would be forced to redeploy vessels to non-EU markets or accept deeply negative margins, accelerating scrapping and creating acute supply dislocations. Hafnia Limited, as both a major competitor and potential activist shareholder, possesses significant power to disrupt current industry dynamics. Hafnia operates a highly efficient pool model that recorded average daily G&A costs of just under USD 1,200 per vessel operating day, compared to the peer average of USD 1,600 per day, allowing Hafnia to maintain low corporate cash breakevens across its spot-exposed platform.

If Hafnia were to aggressively expand its pool membership during a downturn–offering distressed independent owners access to superior commercial execution at lower fixed costs–it could rapidly consolidate market share at the expense of integrated platforms like TORM. Alternatively, if Hafnia or another pool operator acquired a strategic stake in TORM during a period of depressed valuations, they could pressure management to outsource commercial operations, dismantle the in-house platform, and dramatically reduce overhead.

Such a strategic shift would fundamentally alter TORM's business model and eliminate the commercial outperformance premium that has historically justified its higher cost structure. Environmental regulators, particularly within the European Union, represent another critical disruptive force. The current consensus assumes that environmental regulations will tighten gradually, allowing operators time to adapt through fleet renewal and incremental compliance investments. However, if regulators accelerate the phase-in schedule for FuelEU Maritime intensity reductions or impose stricter near-term emissions caps, the economic penalty for operating older non-eco tonnage would spike dramatically. The FuelEU Maritime regulation requires vessels above 5,000 gross tonnes calling at European ports to reduce fuel greenhouse gas intensity by 2% starting in 2025, which gradually tightens to reach 80% by 2050, with shipowners who fail to meet these thresholds facing financial non-compliance penalties.

Accelerated enforcement or higher penalty rates would force premature scrapping of vessels approaching their fifteen-year special survey, collapsing secondhand asset values and creating immediate write-down pressure on fleet carrying values. Several early, overlooked indicators could signal that the market is deviating from consensus expectations. First, a sustained widening of the Brent-Dubai crude spread beyond historical ranges would indicate structural shifts in global refining patterns that reduce product tanker demand from key Middle Eastern export terminals.

Second, sequential increases in product tanker inventories at key storage hubs in Singapore, Fujairah, and Amsterdam- Rotterdam-Antwerp would signal demand softness ahead of visible spot rate declines. Third, accelerating crew wage inflation in the Philippines and India beyond general inflation rates would indicate tightening labor markets that prevent cost reductions even during freight rate downturns. Fourth, carbon allowance prices in the EU ETS exceeding EUR 90 per ton combined with weak pass-through rates would create immediate margin compression for operators with older, less efficient fleets.

The competitive landscape would undergo dramatic restructuring if fixed costs prove stickier than expected. Scorpio Tankers achieved an exceptionally low daily cash breakeven rate of USD 11,000 per day through massive deleveraging, transitioning from USD 2.9 bn of net debt in 2021 to a net cash position of USD 1.4 bn in late 2026, allowing the company to maintain positive cash flow during market troughs. This structural advantage would allow Scorpio to outlast higher-cost competitors and acquire distressed tonnage at depressed prices, further consolidating industry capacity.

Conversely, operators with breakeven rates above USD 15,000 per day would face immediate liquidity crises unless they maintained substantial cash reserves or secured emergency refinancing. The resulting wave of distressed asset sales and consolidation would permanently reshape competitive dynamics, favoring low-cost platforms and potentially eliminating subscale independent operators entirely. - Conference If TORM's operating leverage proves to be a structural liability rather than an asset, the company would face extreme pressure to execute a strategic pivot toward outsourced management, asset-light operations, or outright merger with a larger pool operator.

The current equity valuation premium assigned to TORM's integrated platform would evaporate, forcing a fundamental reassessment of the strategic value of in-house technical and commercial capabilities versus the defensive benefits of flexible, outsourced cost structures.
