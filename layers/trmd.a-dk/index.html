External change forces

Layer 1 · External change forces

Written last by the Investment Analyst for its group. The rating above stands for it, so it carries no number of its own.

The external environment is as favourable as it has been in TORM's history, but the earnings it produces are a rent on geopolitical scarcity rather than a structural step change, and the visibility of that rent is about one to two quarters. Four forces (chokepoint closures, LR2 clean-to-dirty migration, shadow-fleet segmentation, inventory depletion) have stacked on top of a modest structural ton-mile tailwind and a supply side rigid until 2028. Three of the four are reversible and correlated (a Middle East settlement lowers crude rates, releases shuttle and rerouted tonnage, and returns LR2s to clean trades together), so the downside arrives as a cluster, not gradually. The Consumer should model 2026 as a disruption peak, 2027 as a fade with an uncertain slope (the central variable), and 2028 as the test of the structural floor, which depends on the refinery-dislocation tailwind and on scrapping against roughly 63% of a 20% orderbook delivering from 2028. Consensus already embeds this shape (FY2028 EPS at FY2025's level), so the external analysis matters mostly for the slope of 2027 and the level of the floor, not for the direction.

C-02

To what extent, and in which direction, is competitive intensity changing?

B584th of 26

Layer 1 · External change forces

Judgement: effective competition for clean cargoes has fallen sharply in 2026 because supply has been withdrawn from the clean market, but competitive intensity is set to rise from 2027-2028 as the orderbook delivers and as the withdrawn tonnage can return; the direction over the model horizon is from very low back toward normal.

In shipping, competitive intensity is the supply of vessels chasing cargoes. In 2026 that supply has contracted on several fronts at once: about two-thirds of the LR2 fleet trades dirty (about 355 LR2s in dirty trades against 152 clean; about 70 fewer LR2s clean than at the start of 2026 despite 66 deliveries), cutting clean capacity by about 4%; about 20 LR2s and 60-plus VLCCs are tied up in inefficient Persian Gulf shuttle and ship-to-ship operations; about 21% to 24% of global tanker capacity sits in the sanctioned shadow fleet, outside the compliant market; and rerouting absorbs ships. The result is LR2 spot TCE of about USD 294,000 per day and LR1 USD 212,000 in late October 2026 (Deutsche Bank).

Every one of these is reversible: LR2s return to clean trades when crude rates fall; shuttle capacity is released when Hormuz reopens; the shadow fleet partly re-enters if sanctions on Russia ease (General License 135 on Russian diesel, October 2026, is the first sign). Separately, the product tanker orderbook has risen from about 5.5% of the fleet in March 2022 to about 20% (Deutsche Bank, November 2026), concentrated in LR2s, with about 63% delivering in 2028 and later. Among listed peers behaviour is rational (Scorpio selling 19 older ships and buying back stock; Hafnia winding down its Handy and LR2 pools), but the industry is fragmented and private owners order into peaks.

For the model: the clean-market supply cushion is mostly cyclical; assume competitive intensity normalises through 2027 and turns adverse in 2028 when LR2 deliveries meet any return of LR2s from dirty trades.

C-05

How are input costs, input availability and supplier concentration changing, and is the net effect a headwind or a tailwind?

C+526th of 26

Layer 1 · External change forces

Judgement: a mild cost headwind that is trivial against current rates but becomes material at mid-cycle rates; supplier scarcity (shipyards) is a tailwind for asset values and for supply discipline but a headwind for the cost of fleet renewal.

  • Vessel operating expenses rose to USD 8,315 per day in Q2 2026 from USD 7,853 (about +6%); seafarer costs rose to USD 80.8m in H1 2026 from USD 75.3m (+7%) with a larger crew pool (3,891 to 3,924 seafarers); administration rose 19% to USD 113.8m in 2025, partly retention and accelerated vesting. War-risk insurance and security costs are up; P&I clubs cancelled Gulf war-risk cover in early 2026.
  • Fuel is mostly a pass-through in spot voyage economics (TCE is net of bunkers), and the wide very-low-sulphur to high-sulphur spread (USD 202 a ton in August 2026) rewards TORM's scrubber-fitted ships with a premium of about USD 1,500 per day on MRs.
  • Shipyards are the concentrated suppliers (China, Korea, Japan about 85% of capacity; top yards booked to 2029; newbuild prices at historical highs), which caps supply growth (good for rates) and props up secondhand values (good for net asset value) but makes renewal expensive (bad for return on new capital).

At about USD 40,000 per day, a USD 500 per day cost rise is 1.2% of TCE; at USD 25,000 it is 2% of TCE but about 10% of EPS. For the model: grow opex per day about 4% to 6% a year and administration about 3% to 5%; the cost headwind matters only in the 2028 normalisation case.

C457th of 26

Layer 1 · External change forces

Judgement: the macro forces (an oil-price and freight-cost shock, high interest rates, slowing demand) act on TORM mainly through two channels, oil product demand destruction and ship asset values, and they bite hardest at the industry level (energy prices and demand) rather than on TORM specifically; they are currently swamped by geopolitics but become the dominant force once routes normalise.

The Portfolio Fitness for Fighting read is relevant: the Sector (downstream distribution) is Double Overweight on the tape while Fragility is Double Underweight, and its Energy condition (Brent under USD 85) would remove the reason for the Energy overweight. For the model: macro is the swing factor for the 2028 floor (demand volume and restocking), not for 2026-2027.

C-01

To what extent is end-market demand changing, structurally or cyclically, and with how much visibility? (A cyclical swing counts when it has two-plus years of visibility.)

D6016th of 26

Layer 1 · External change forces

Judgement: demand for compliant product tanker capacity is in an extreme cyclical spike created by disruption, sitting on top of a modest structural ton-mile tailwind; the spike has good visibility for one to two quarters and poor visibility beyond mid-2027, so it does not meet the two-year test for a "visible" cyclical swing.

The spike is about distance and dislocation, not volume. Barrel demand is falling (the International Energy Agency sees global oil demand down about 1 million barrels a day in 2026, the World Bank 2.6 million; China's apparent demand was down 15% year on year in September 2026), yet product tanker ton-miles have soared because:

  • the Strait of Hormuz has been effectively shut since February 2026 (re-closed after the US-Iran ceasefire collapsed in July 2026), with Middle East refined product exports down about two-thirds from the pre-conflict level and Arabian Gulf product exports down 79% year on year from March to July;
  • Red Sea and Bab el-Mandeb transits have halved, forcing Cape of Good Hope routings (seven to fourteen extra days on Asia-Europe);
  • replacement barrels come from far away (US Gulf product exports +40%; US Gulf to Ningbo 110 days against 45 from the Persian Gulf), and average product haul has risen about 21% to about 3,530 miles;
  • Russian refining has been degraded by drone strikes (Russian diesel exports suspended in July 2026), and global refined product inventories were drawn at about 2.4 million barrels a day from March to September 2026, setting up a restocking demand that management puts at 1% to 2% of seaborne oil trade, plus a refinery maintenance peak of about 6.4 million barrels a day in Q2 2027.

The structural layer is real but small: refinery capacity is relocating from the Atlantic basin to the Middle East and Asia (about 30 European refineries closed since 2009; more than 1 million barrels a day shut in the US and Europe in 2025), so ton-miles grow two to three times faster than barrels. It adds perhaps 1% to 3% a year to demand, which matters for the floor, not the peak.

Visibility: TORM's own books give one quarter (Q4 2026 53% fixed at USD 78,419 per day), and the 1-year time charter market (Saudi Aramco fixed a Scorpio vessel for five years at USD 28,350 per day; Scorpio fixed three 2015-built ships for three years at USD 23,900 to 25,000 per day) shows where charterers think the mid-cycle sits: about USD 25,000 to 30,000 per day, a third to a half of today's spot. That gap is the market's own estimate of how much of today's demand is cyclical disruption. The cone of outcomes beyond six months is unusually wide and its skew is negative: a ceasefire normalises routes within two to three months (Morgan Stanley's estimate of the unwind lag), while persistence adds earnings at a declining marginal rate.

For the model: treat 2026 as a disruption year; model 2027 as a fade whose speed is the key scenario variable, and anchor the floor on the structural layer and time-charter rates, not on spot. Visibility is under twelve months.

C-03

To what extent is a disruption force strengthening or being disproved, and in which direction does it act on the company?

D4818th of 26

Layer 1 · External change forces

Judgement: the long-term disruption force (oil demand erosion from electrification) is strengthening but sits mostly beyond the model horizon; the nearer disruption force, decarbonisation regulation, is strengthening and acts in two directions on TORM, shrinking effective supply industry-wide (positive) while penalising TORM's older-than-peer fleet (negative). Net, neutral to mildly negative within four years.

Electrification is no longer hypothetical: electric vehicles displaced about 1 million barrels a day of Chinese oil demand in 2025 (about 15% of road transport demand), electric heavy trucks took 31% of Chinese heavy-truck sales in August 2026, and European road fuel demand is shrinking (European diesel import needs forecast to fall from 34 million tons in 2026 to 19 million by 2030). Most forecasters put the global oil-demand impact after 2035, but diesel into Europe, TORM's core long-haul import lane, is the first trade to be hit, inside the model's four-year window.

Decarbonisation rules bite sooner: the EU Emissions Trading System covers 100% of intra-EU voyage emissions in 2026, FuelEU Maritime started in 2025, the UK maritime scheme began July 2026, and the IMO Carbon Intensity Indicator forces poorly rated ships to slow steam. Slower, older ships reduce effective fleet supply (a positive for rates), but European carbon costs fall 30% to 40% more heavily on non-eco tankers (Deutsche Bank), and TORM's fleet averages about 11 years against peers' 9.9. TORM's offsets are real: it hit its 40% carbon-intensity target a year early, owns ME Production (scrubbers and green technology) outright since June 2025, and runs a substantial scrubber fleet. The IMO's Net-Zero Framework was postponed, which delays the forcing of fleet renewal.

For the model: no disruption-driven volume loss before 2028; include rising compliance cost per day (mostly passed through voyage economics, but a residual opex drag) and a gradually rising relative cost for older vessels.

To what extent is political or regulatory change under way, and how does it bear on the company?

B636th of 26

Judgement: political change is the single largest earnings driver and currently a strong tailwind, but it is the most reversible force in the case, and the political incentive now runs against it because freight is a visible part of record fuel prices.
