The seven things that matter most:

  1. Renew's EPS growth over the next two years is mostly mechanical, and therefore unusually predictable. From FY23 to FY25, EPS grew only about 3% a year while EBIT grew 7.5% a year, because the tax rate rose from about 18% to 25% and interest on acquisition debt rose from £1.3m to £4.6m. Both increases are now complete, so from FY27 EPS growth tracks EBIT growth at 6% to 9% a year. FY26 EPS (about 71p) is effectively known, and FY27 EPS should land between about 70p and 80p.
  2. Renew's operations will change little. Organic growth of 4% to 5% a year is mostly inflation passed through to clients, with only 1 to 2 points of real volume; bolt-on acquisitions add 1.5 to 2 points. The margin holds at about 6.6% because Renew shares efficiency gains with clients, and it falls only when clients defer work and Renew's own crews sit idle.

#1Operating environment: tailwinds and headwinds

Judgement: Renew's funding tailwinds are strong, legislated and broad, but they arrive mostly in 2027 to 2029, and they deliver visibility rather than margin.

The tailwinds come from three regulated spending programmes:

  • Water: Asset Management Period 8 (AMP8), 2025 to 2030. The £104bn programme is roughly double the last one, and Renew now serves 10 of the 12 largest water companies, up from 3 in 2020. Most construction falls in 2027 to 2029.
  • Power: the RIIO-ED2 distribution and RIIO-T3 transmission price controls. Transmission is the fastest-growing demand, but power is only about 5% of revenue.
  • Highways: the third Road Investment Strategy (RIS3), from April 2026. The £27bn programme funds renewals about 75% above the last strategy.

The headwind is rail, Renew's largest market at about 40% of revenue.

Rail renewals are flat in real terms through Control Period 7 (CP7, April 2024 to March 2029). The rail regulator says inflation has removed about £2bn of buying power.

Water, power and telecoms work is inflation-linked and compelled by regulation. The risk is that strained water clients slow their orders: Thames Water is restructuring, Pennon needed a £550m rights issue, and South East Water is rated BB+.

Taxpayer-funded · about 55% to 60%

Rail and highways work is exposed to fiscal events. The 28 October Budget faces a fiscal gap of about £12bn, and a £428m cut to RIS3 is already proposed.

Regulated money buys a contractor visibility and a higher multiple, not a higher margin. Construction wage inflation (about 5.8% a year), client efficiency targets (Network Rail wants £3.8bn of savings over CP7) and gain-share contract terms absorb the benefit of higher volume.

Three structural supports favour incumbents with their own crews:

  • Clients are consolidating their supplier panels.
  • About 3,800 construction firms failed in the year to June 2026.
  • Accredited labour is scarce: high-voltage crews take three to five years to train.

Two dated risks fall within the window:

  • Returning competition, 2027 to 2028. Large contractors will move crews from HS2 into Renew's maintenance frameworks.
  • Two control-period transitions. Power's RIIO-ED3 starts in April 2028 and rail's CP8 in April 2029. The last rail transition swung Renew's organic growth from +16.6% in FY24 to -2.5% in FY25.

Macroeconomics acts on Renew's multiple, not on its demand. With thirty-year gilt yields near 6%, high UK discount rates penalise small, illiquid stocks such as Renew, which trades only $2m to $3m a day on AIM (the London Stock Exchange's junior market). Sterling does not matter: 99% of revenue is in the UK.

Score: 62. Legislated, multi-year demand growth in three of four end markets is better than most European companies have, but it is back-loaded, it brings no pricing power, and the largest market is being squeezed in real terms.

#2Internally driven change forces

Judgement: Renew's big internal changes are complete and were well executed. What lies ahead is steady execution, plus one option: the new chair.

What Renew changed from 2024 to 2026:

  • It became a pure-play maintenance group by selling specialist builder Walter Lilly (October 2024), removing fixed-price and latent-defect risk.
  • It built a turnkey high-voltage power offer from nothing through four acquisitions: Excalon, Emerald, PWR-X and EDES.
  • It widened its water footprint for AMP8.
  • It strengthened governance and rebalanced incentives towards EPS and cash.

Three operational drags are small and fixable:

  • France: a French wind unit bought at roughly 10x EBITDA has been under restructuring review within 18 months.
  • Sellafield: a site-wide strike hit nuclear work in H1 FY26.
  • Margin drift: the continuing margin fell from 7.0% to 6.5%.

Fixing France and nuclear is worth £2m to £3m of EBIT by FY28, half of which wage inflation absorbs.

The quality issue to watch is acquisition pricing. Including earn-outs, recent deals cost 6.5x to about 14x EBITDA, which earns roughly the cost of capital before growth. Return on capital employed (ROCE) has fallen from 25% to 22%. The deals create value only if the acquired high-voltage crews grow, which the disclosures do not yet show.

The live change force is the new chair. At Morgan Advanced Materials, Pete Raby:

pruned the portfolio and closed sites;
reported by segment;
held capital markets days in 2022 and 2024;
launched a £40m buyback, then paused it to keep leverage within target;
walked away from deals that would not beat the cost of capital by year three.
Pete Raby's record makes better disclosure and a formal capital-allocation framework more likely than not. A buyback is possible but not his reflex, and nothing is announced. Treat the chair transition as a real option dated to the 27 January 2027 AGM, not as a forecast.

Score: 52. The repositioning was sensible and is complete, but nothing internal forces an EPS step-change within 18 months, and the most valuable change, capital allocation under a new chair, is still conjecture.

#5Pricing power, operating leverage, financial leverage, cost-out and feedback loops

Judgement: none of Renew's profit amplifiers is strong, so revenue growth turns into proportional, not outsized, profit growth. That is the price of the business's low risk.

Pricing power

Defensive, not offensive

Operating leverage

Low up, moderate down

Financial leverage · cost-out

Negligible · small

Renew's pricing power protects its margin but cannot raise its real prices. More than 60% of the order book is reimbursable or target-cost work (cost plus a fee, with gains shared). The protection works: Renew held its margin through the 2022 to 2023 inflation, while Costain lost 1.8 points.

Power: scarce, but small

Excalon earns about 9.6% and Emerald about 18%, but power is too small to add more than 10 to 20 basis points to the group margin.

Rail: margin at risk

The re-procurement of rail work for CP8 is more likely to take margin from Renew than to add it.

Operating leverage is asymmetric: low on the way up, moderate on the way down.

▲ On the way up: little gain

  • Overheads inflate. Overheads (about 7.5% of revenue, mostly salaries) rise 4% to 6% a year, so the margin rises only when organic growth exceeds about 4%, and then by only 7 to 8 basis points per extra point.
  • Gains are shared. Target-cost terms pass most productivity gains to clients.

▼ On the way down: real pain

  • Deferrals strand crews. When clients defer work, about 2,700 directly employed operatives sit idle for months.
  • The evidence. The margin fell in FY24 despite +16.6% organic growth, and fell another 45 basis points when organic revenue fell 2.5% in FY25.
Asymmetric operating leverage is the main reason the range of possible earnings outcomes is skewed downward.

Financial leverage and cost-cutting add little. Falling interest lifts FY26 and FY27 EPS slightly, then stops. A group-wide shared-services programme could save £4m to £8m (35 to 70 basis points), but management rejects it as contrary to the decentralised model; it is an option for the new chair, not a forecast.

#7Scope for upgrades over the next 6 to 12 months

Judgement: the scope for upgrades is small. The operating business is already forecast correctly, so upgrades must come from acquisitions, weather or a board decision.

Sources of upgrades, from most to least reliable:

  1. Bolt-on acquisitions, which the consensus excludes. Each £25m deal lifts forecasts by 0.5% to 1.5%: likely, but small.
  2. A clean H2 FY26 on 24 November. Organic growth of 7% or more, led by water and highways, would lift confidence more than forecasts, because Deutsche Bank already expects about 7%.
  3. A second harsh winter, which would bring reactive rail repairs again but cannot be forecast.
  4. A capital-return policy, the only possible step-change, at 20% to 25% likely within 18 months.

Sources of downgrades:

  1. FY27 consensus EPS (76.6p) sits 1% to 2% above our forecast, because the brokers assume slightly faster growth, lower interest and lower tax. Tax guidance on 24 November could trim it by up to 1%.
  2. The April 2027 trading update laps the record weather-driven winter, making it the largest single revision risk in either direction.

The narrative stays "visibility confirmed, slowly". The FY26 results, the new chair, the first AMP8 construction volume (May 2027) and the National Highways SDF2 framework award each confirm the existing story. SDF2 is small: the old lots were worth 2% to 4% of revenue, and new work starts after February 2028.

Score: 38. Forecasts that are already right leave little to upgrade beyond routine bolt-ons, and the one large upgrade source, capital returns, depends on a board decision not yet made.

#9Riskiness and simplicity of the case

Judgement: Renew's fundamental risk is low and its case is simple, but disclosure is poor and sentiment is fragile. The risks to the share price exceed the risks to value, and because they are dated, they help with timing.

Why the fundamental risk is low:

  • The EPS acceleration is mostly mechanical (tax and interest) or inflation-linked.
  • Funding is legislated on four independent timetables, running to 2028 to 2031.
  • Work is short-cycle and largely reimbursable, with no fixed-price exposure.
  • The balance sheet carries net cash.

Even the CP7 shock left EPS growing.

The case is concentrated, but benignly.

The one uncertain driver is the timing of AMP8 construction and the rail transitions.

Five fragilities to underwrite, from most to least important:

  1. False friend: organic volume may be weaker than reported growth suggests. H1 FY26 was weather-assisted and the order book grew only 4.1%. The tests are the mix of H2 FY26 growth (24 November) and H1 FY27 (April and May 2027).
  2. A rail deferral hits Renew twice: it cuts revenue and, through idle crews, margin. A 25 basis point margin hit costs about 3.9% of EPS.

  • Renew reports one segment, so its accounts give no early warning of a shift in its mix of work; client and regulator data are the better monitoring tool.

#13Summary conclusion

Renew is a good business priced fairly. Its predictable EPS growth is already in the consensus and in a median multiple, so at about 2% a year it is a hold, not a bet. The case becomes a bet through one of two doors.

Door 1 · Capital allocation

Spare borrowing capacity of up to a fifth of the market value and a new chair with a record of buybacks make a capital return a real, under-discussed option, roughly one-in-four likely within 18 months.

Door 2 · Price
