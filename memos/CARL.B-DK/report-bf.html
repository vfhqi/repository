EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Carlsberg Group operates as a scale challenger in the global brewing industry, generating 51% of revenue from mature Western European markets while strategically pivoting toward a multi-beverage platform through the transformational Britvic acquisition and an expanding PepsiCo partnership covering 17 markets by 2029. The company has successfully increased its soft drinks exposure from under 20% to 31% of total volumes, providing crucial insulation from structural declines in mature European beer consumption.

This strategic rebalancing represents management's most significant portfolio pivot since 2008, fundamentally reshaping the company's defensive moat. delivered a consistent 3.6% average organic growth rate from 2010 to 2025 while expanding its historical operating margin from 15.2% toward a projected 17.3% over the next two decades. The group maintained a robust 10.8% return on invested capital in 2026, demonstrating superior capital efficiency despite the dilutive impact of the Britvic acquisition.

However, this financial profile trails Heineken's superior volume momentum driven by broader emerging market exposure and Anheuser-Busch InBev's unmatched 33.6% EBITDA margins supported by leading market positions across 75% of volumes.

The global brewing industry has consolidated heavily, with the top five players controlling more than half of all volumes, leaving Carlsberg with a defensive footprint covering only 24% of its volumes compared to AB InBev's 75% leadership exposure. This structural competitive disadvantage forces Carlsberg to rely on taking market share from established incumbents rather than defending dominant positions, exposing regional earnings to volatile market dynamics and intensifying execution requirements. Carlsberg's concentrated footprint creates significant earnings sensitivity to China, which represents approximately 30% of beer volumes, and suffered a severe 10% volume decline in the third quarter of 2026 due to extreme summer rainfall and wholesaler destocking.

Conversely, India has emerged as the primary growth driver, contributing nearly half of incremental volumes through 2030 with an 18% market share and projected 4.8% compound annual growth, vastly outpacing the global market average of 0.2%. 4 The brewing value chain remains dominated by energy-intensive packaging materials like aluminum cans and glass bottles, which represent the largest variable cost component and expose gross margins to severe geopolitical and utilities volatility.

Packaging costs consume a very high portion of variable expenses in European markets, making them the single largest factor affecting group gross margins annually. Extreme consolidation among container manufacturers like Ardagh Metal Packaging has created tight industry capacity, giving suppliers stable pricing power and reducing Carlsberg's bargaining leverage during long-term contract negotiations. Carlsberg segments its customer base across on-trade hospitality venues and off-trade retail channels, facing structural shifts toward at-home consumption that require optimization of regional pack architecture to protect long-term margins.

High retail concentration in mature markets, strengthened by European buying alliances pooling procurement volumes, limits manufacturer pricing leverage unless Carlsberg maintains highly differentiated premium brands. This downstream consolidation represents a persistent threat to off-trade pricing power and commercial flexibility.

Insourcing these volumes by end-2026 is expected to deliver a meaningful profit uplift in 2027.

Bolt-on expansion: Carlsberg signed agreements to acquire Iberia Refreshments in Georgia and JI Pepsi Cola Bottler in Armenia from Revery. These bolt-on transactions create a unified South Caucasus hub alongside its established business in Azerbaijan. This geographic cluster provides a strong platform to build regional beer market share over the next three years.

Plausible problems to monitor

Carlsberg exhibits several operational and structural vulnerabilities that position the company unfavorably relative to established peer benchmarks and sector norms across the global brewing industry. These findings represent material concerns that could constrain the group's ability to compound earnings at the rate anticipated by management's Accelerate SAIL strategy. The most striking competitive weakness lies in Carlsberg's defensibility profile, where the company holds a leading market position in only 24% of its volumes compared to 75% for Anheuser-Busch InBev and 52% for Heineken.

This translates into Carlsberg operating as a challenger in 66% of its markets, representing the highest challenger weighting in the global brewing sector. This structural disadvantage forces Carlsberg to consistently win market share from entrenched incumbents rather than defending profitable leadership positions, thereby exposing regional earnings to volatile competitive dynamics and limiting pricing power during periods of consumer weakness.

Carlsberg's geographic concentration represents a second material vulnerability, with China accounting for approximately 30% of total beer volumes, making the group's overall earnings highly sensitive to economic shocks in this single market. This concentration risk materialized severely in the third quarter of 2026, when Chinese operations suffered volume declines of approximately 10% due to poor weather and weak consumer sentiment, with extreme summer rainfall triggering extensive wholesaler destocking and subsidiary Chongqing Brewery reporting a 9.6% sales decline during the peak consumption season.

The narrow geographic footprint contrasts unfavorably with Heineken's broad emerging market diversification, which generates 64% of volumes from growth markets and insulates the business from localized economic disruptions.

Balance sheet leverage deteriorated sharply following the Britvic acquisition, with Carlsberg's net debt to EBITDA ratio escalating to approximately 3.9 times, compared to the company's historical framework of keeping leverage comfortably below 2.0 times. This elevated leverage creates significant refinancing exposure, with Deutsche Bank estimating a potential 1.1% hit to earnings per share in year three at a 6.5% interest rate, materially higher than the 0.6% impact projected for Anheuser-Busch InBev.

The debt burden temporarily suspends share buybacks and constrains capital allocation flexibility at a time when better-capitalized peers are implementing progressive shareholder distributions. Carlsberg maintains the highest structural exposure to aluminum cans among European brewers due to its developed market and soft drink footprint, with spot aluminum prices rising approximately 17% close to post- Ukraine highs in 2026, requiring a 1.3% price increase to offset worst-case aluminum inflation if high spot prices persist.

This packaging vulnerability is compounded by packaging materials and freight expenses accounting for 54% of total production costs at comparable regional operations like Tsingtao Brewery, where sensitive packaging costs like glass and aluminum alone represent 30% of total operating expenses, meaning a packaging price spike of 5% would compress gross margins by up to 6% organically. This cost structure sensitivity places Carlsberg at a meaningful competitive disadvantage during periods of elevated energy and commodity inflation. final critical weakness centers on execution risk surrounding the planned transition of Nordic and Baltic bottling contracts from Coca-Cola to PepsiCo in 2029, which represents the single largest strategic risk to business quality and carries severe operational risks if customer contracts face disruptions that could dilute expected multi-beverage synergies and impair operating margins.

This complex system migration involves renegotiating relationships across major Scandinavian grocery chains during a period when retail buying alliances are consolidating their bargaining power, creating material downside risk to the core Western European profit base that generates 51% of group revenue and 48% of operating profit.

Outliers worth noting

Three genuine financial and operational surprises emerge from Carlsberg's recent performance that sit at the extremes of sector norms or remain inadequately explained by management guidance and disclosures. The first striking outlier is the severity of Carlsberg's Chinese volume collapse in the third quarter of 2026, where volumes plunged approximately 10% due to extreme summer rainfall and wholesaler destocking, with its subsidiary Chongqing Brewery reporting a 9.6% decline in sales during the same quarter.

This contraction sits at the bottom extreme of sector performance and appears disproportionately severe relative to the reported weather disruption. While the broader Chinese brewing industry experienced a 6% production volume decline in the first nine months of 2026 and Tsingtao reported a 9% volume decline in the third quarter, the magnitude of Carlsberg's regional collapse suggests deeper structural issues beyond weather-related disruptions.

Management attributed the downturn to weak consumer sentiment and severe summer weather dragging down organic volumes by approximately 10% in China, but this explanation inadequately addresses why Carlsberg's performance materially undershot the broader industry decline. The gap between reported weather impacts and actual volume outcomes suggests either unusually concentrated geographic exposure to the affected regions or unacknowledged competitive share losses that management has not fully disclosed to investors. The second extreme outlier is Royal Unibrew's dramatic tangible return on invested capital compression, which fell from a peak of 94% in 2021 to 54.1% in 2024, reflecting the integration of lower-margin assets like Solera. This near-halving of capital efficiency over a three-year period represents one of the most severe ROIC declines observed in the European brewing sector and raises fundamental questions about the strategic rationale behind the company's aggressive acquisition programme.

Royal Unibrew signed 11 bolt-on and three large transactions between 2021 and 2025, but this aggressive deal-making now faces a risk of margin dilution if the company fails to harvest synergies from these deals. The magnitude of this capital efficiency deterioration sits far outside normal integration friction and suggests that the acquired businesses operate at structurally lower returns than the company's historical beer-focused portfolio, yet management has provided limited public disclosure on the timeline or likelihood of ROIC recovery to justify the shareholder value destruction.

The third operational surprise is Carlsberg's extreme sensitivity to debt refinancing costs following the Britvic acquisition, where the company faces a potential 1.1% hit to year-three earnings per share at a 6.5% interest rate, significantly higher than the 0.6% impact projected for Anheuser-Busch InBev, meaning persistent high interest rates could delay the resumption of share buybacks if deleveraging progress slows.

This refinancing vulnerability appears disproportionately severe relative to peers and sits at the extreme end of sector leverage risk profiles. Carlsberg's net debt to EBITDA leverage ratio escalated to approximately 3.9 times post-Britvic compared to the company's historical framework of keeping leverage comfortably below 2.0 times, suggesting that Carlsberg must prioritize rapid debt paydown before resuming share buybacks.

What remains unexplained is why Carlsberg structured the Britvic transaction with such elevated financial risk relative to its conservative historical capital allocation framework, particularly given the known execution risks associated with integrating a transformational soft drinks acquisition while simultaneously preparing for the complex 2029 PepsiCo bottling system transition across the Nordics and Baltics. Management has not adequately articulated the strategic imperative that justified accepting this level of balance sheet strain and earnings-per-share refinancing sensitivity, especially when the company's closest peer AB InBev demonstrates materially lower refinancing risk despite operating at similar absolute leverage multiples.
