According to a Jefferies report published on October 26, 2026, annual average fleet capacity growth is forecast at 7% in 2027, accelerating to 10.6% in 2028 and remaining at 9.7% in 2029. Shipyard capacity constraints and delivery slippage represent the most immediate unknown. Green fuel availability and infrastructure development remains deeply uncertain despite heavy dual-fuel vessel commitments. Without reliable green fuel networks, these expensive vessels may operate on conventional fuel, undermining their economic rationale and potentially triggering delivery deferrals. The required evidence includes: committed investment in methanol production facilities with specific start-up dates; signed long-term fuel supply agreements between carriers and energy producers; and detailed bunkering infrastructure development plans at top-twenty container ports.

This gap would resolve through public announcements from energy majors (Shell, TotalEnergies, BP) confirming commercial-scale marine fuel projects, or through IMO reporting on alternative fuel availability by region. Geopolitical stability and trade route normalization constitute the largest wildcard affecting effective capacity. Morgan Stanley noted Suez Canal transits recovered to 54% of pre-disruption levels, with full normalization potentially releasing 7% of global fleet capacity.

The necessary evidence includes: formal peace agreements with multinational security guarantees for Red Sea shipping lanes; committed government infrastructure spending on port automation and expansion projects; and insurance market pricing for war-risk premiums on major trade routes. Resolution would come from sustained six-month periods of normal Suez transit volumes exceeding 90% of historical averages, or from major terminal operators announcing capacity expansion completions at gateway ports.

However, if the market diverges sharply from this baseline view–either toward severe overcapacity or unexpectedly tight supply –the financial consequences for A.P.
