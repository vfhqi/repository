Section 1 – Brief company description and history

1.
Pure-play transformation: Nexans is a French electrical-cable manufacturer with more than 140 years of history, now organized around PWR-Transmission, PWR-Grid and PWR-Connect, and operating across 39 countries with 12,830 employees and €6.1 billion of 2025 standard sales. The strategic significance is that Nexans has moved away from a diversified cable portfolio toward higher-value electrification infrastructure, including subsea interconnectors, grid modernization, data centers, solar, battery storage and other electricity end markets.
2.
Business model: Nexans combines cable manufacturing with engineering, accessories, installation, project execution, lifecycle services and digital tools, rather than competing only as a commodity cable producer. Its stated model is to capture more value through advanced cable systems, bundled accessories, low-carbon products, asset-management tools and customer-specific solutions, with investment directed toward PWR- Transmission, PWR-Grid and PWR-Connect.
3.
Higher-value mix: PWR-Connect generated 38% of 2025 standard sales and 40% of adjusted EBITDA, while PWR-Transmission generated 27% of sales and 28% of adjusted EBITDA and PWR-Grid generated 22% of sales and 30% of adjusted EBITDA. Copper security, structurally improved: Nexans’s four global rod mills, multi-source procurement policy and long-term relationships with key suppliers give it a stronger supply position than a cable maker dependent entirely on external rod purchases, while its seven-year agreement with a Tier 1 copper miner was described by Bernstein’s Leslie as protection against severe supply-chain disruption.
2.
Copper scarcity, not resolved: Nexans warned in its 2025 Universal Registration Document that the world could face a 10–20% copper supply gap by 2030, and management said in the February 2026 earnings call that copper scarcity was already emerging as prices reached approximately $13,000 per tonne, so recycling reduces exposure but does not eliminate the risk of higher input costs, constrained production or lost volumes.
3.
Recycling, a meaningful medium-term hedge: The Lens NCCCR project is scheduled to add up to 80,000 tonnes of annual recycled-copper processing capacity from 2027, with flexibility between scrap refining and primary copper use, while Nexans aims to increase recycled content in products to 25% by 2028; this should improve supply resilience and tender differentiation, although no quantified 2027 or 2028 margin uplift was disclosed. Aluminium procurement, materially de-risked: Nexans signed a long-term Hydro agreement for approximately 85,000 tonnes of low-carbon aluminium wire rod, securing a larger portion of supply while supporting traceability and decarbonisation requirements in European grid projects; this is favourable relative to the previous reporting position because it reduces spot-market exposure, but it does not protect against every energy, labour or logistics cost. Metal pass-through, protects revenue more than margins: Nexans’s policy is to pass copper and aluminium prices through to customers and hedge the related exposure, while Bernstein reported daily pricing indexation in Connect and long-term supply contracts as mitigants; however, expert procurement evidence showed cable prices rising 5–10% year on year as copper and aluminium costs increased, so timing differences between procurement, hedging and customer repricing can still pressure cash and operating margins.
4.
Copper working capital, remains material: Nexans requires minimum “core exposure” metal inventories for routine operations, and copper-related trade payables carried payment terms of 30 to 120 days, including €449 million under negotiated supplier-payment arrangements at December 2025 versus €341 million a year earlier; higher metal prices therefore increase the financing requirement even when the metal margin is contractually passed through.
5.
Working-capital conversion, below the prior year: H1 2026 free cash flow was €165.5 million with 42.7% cash conversion, compared with €308.8 million and 83.1% in H1 2025, while working-capital inflow was €121.1 million versus €202.5 million; Nexans attributed the comparison partly to unusually large prior-year Transmission downpayments, but the October update gave no Q3 working-capital data to show that conversion had recovered.
6.
Energy procurement, partially protected: Nexans’s Energy Risk Policy requires energy procurement to be secured at least 18 months ahead, and a group energy committee monitors requirements and purchasing strategy; this reduces immediate exposure to electricity-price spikes relative to an unhedged producer, but European energy costs, plant utilisation and future contract renewals can still affect EBITDA when procurement protection rolls off.
7.
Equipment capacity, improving in medium voltage: Nexans plans to invest approximately €62 million in medium-voltage accessories capacity in Europe and the United States, increasing accessories production capacity by 39% from 2026 to 2028 and reducing lead times by up to approximately 60%; this should improve service levels, local sourcing and asset utilisation if demand persists, but it adds capital expenditure before the return on the new capacity is proven.
8.
Republic Wire, local supply economics improving: Nexans reports that Republic Wire has installed two new production lines, is operating seven days a week and is on track for a 30% capacity increase, while its more than 250 United States sales agents now distribute the Nexans portfolio; local manufacturing should reduce delivery and logistics friction, but Bernstein found the acquisition’s Q3 scope contribution of 24.7% was about 3 percentage points below consensus, indicating that commercial conversion was not yet fully matching the supply investment. High-voltage supply, still the principal bottleneck: A global utility procurement manager reported high-voltage and extra-high-voltage lead times of two to five years, with early manufacturing-slot reservations becoming standard, while a United Kingdom transmission-network engineer described lead times rising from roughly 40–48 weeks previously to 24–36 months; demand therefore continues to exceed qualified supply, supporting pricing and backlog but delaying revenue recognition and tying up working capital. Transmission capacity, selectively released: Nexans began industrial-scale production of its 525 kilovolt highvoltage direct-current subsea cable for TenneT’s LanWin2 project, moving the technology from qualification into delivery, while the Charleroi high-voltage tower is expected to enter service in mid-2027; these developments expand future capacity, but the Q3 Transmission organic decline of 4.0% and the unresolved Mass Impregnated order show that not all existing capacity was economically loaded. Halden utilisation, remains the supply-side risk: Bernstein’s Leslie, Kela and Witting warned that delayed Great Sea Interconnector volumes could leave Halden under-utilised and weaken Nexans’s bargaining power, while the October guidance still assumed that the Mass Impregnated line would be loaded at year-end despite no replacement order being announced; under-absorption could therefore offset benefits from stronger Grid and Connect demand through lower margins and weaker EPS. Labour and installation, still cost pressures: Expert procurement evidence indicated that margins were approximately 2–5 percentage points below expectations because of copper and aluminium volatility, higher installation costs and aggressive competition, while labour shortages were forcing contractors to pay wage premiums; Nexans’s new Chief Operating Officer, Stéphane Artaud, was appointed specifically to improve industrial operations and supply-chain performance, suggesting management sees execution costs as an active rather than settled issue. Supplier resilience, better monitored but not fully diversified: Nexans evaluated 100% of in-scope suppliers in its 2025 vigilance plan, assessed critical-partner financial vulnerability and maintains alternative sourcing for key metals, while more than 600 strategic suppliers participate in its climate roadmap; nevertheless, the company does not disclose supplier-level concentration, returns on capital or financial-health metrics, so the research cannot establish whether supplier bargaining power has materially improved since the previous reporting period.
9.
Peer supply comparison, Nexans remains constrained: A renewable-energy developer’s October 2026 channel check ranked Prysmian first for overall supply-chain resilience because of scale and portfolio breadth, viewed NKT as a strong European high-voltage alternative and described Nexans as competitive in transmission and subsea, but said none of the three had abundant spare capacity; Nexans therefore benefits from an oligopolistic market without enjoying a clear supply advantage over its principal peers. Supply outlook, favourable but less scarce over time: NKT’s new capacity and Prysmian’s expansion may add industry supply from 2027 onward, while Sumitomo and LS Cable are gaining traction in high-voltage and extra-high-voltage projects; this could eventually weaken scarcity pricing and returns on capital, although qualification, factory construction and customer references mean the competitive supply response is unlikely to eliminate bottlenecks immediately. Other side of the story: Nexans’s vertical integration, Hydro aluminium agreement, recycled-copper project, local accessories expansion and long-term supplier relationships support a more resilient cost base than the pre-2026 position, but the stronger sceptical reading is that the company is adding fixed capacity while Halden remains exposed to project delays, input and labour inflation, and cash conversion has not yet returned to the 2025 level.

Non-obvious reading, Grid may be structurally underestimated: The non-obvious reading supported by the evidence is that PWR-Grid's 14.8% Q3 organic growth may represent a structural shift in utility, data-center and solar procurement behavior rather than a temporary acceleration, because customers are securing capacity earlier, paying for certainty and accepting longer lead times in a supply-constrained market.
