Section 16 – Conclusion 1 finding

The single most important thing that changed during this reporting period was EXOR's decisive shift from defending its Ferrari concentration to actively mitigating it through disciplined monetization. EXOR completed a massive €3 billion accelerated bookbuild sale of a portion of its Ferrari stake in March 2025, reducing its economic interest from 22.9% to 19.5% to address elevated portfolio concentration concerns as Ferrari had risen to nearly half of EXOR's Net Asset Value.

This tactical rebalancing, combined with the non-core asset exits, built EXOR's cash available for deployment to more than €3.5 billion by early 2026, while simultaneously reducing the company's Loan-to-Value ratio to a highly conservative 6.9% from 9.6% in the prior year. Management intends to utilize this massive war chest to pursue another large-scale strategic acquisition similar in scale and ambition to its original Philips investment, while maintaining a LTV ratio well below the corporate target threshold of keeping net debt under 15%.

This liquidity-driven defensive posture represents a marked departure from EXOR's historical willingness to maintain concentrated equity positions and deploy capital rapidly into new sectors.

The strongest counter-hypothesis to the bullish investment thesis centers on severe and potentially permanent capital drag from EXOR's elevated cash position during a period of persistent inflation and market volatility. While keeping a low LTV ratio of 6.9% provides safety, investors may continue to apply a record 56% NAV discount if they grow frustrated with the slow pace of capital deployment into new healthcare investments, as holding over €3.5 billion in cash during a high-inflation period risks material capital drag if market opportunities do not materialize quickly.

The market's extreme skepticism is reflected in EXOR's historically unprecedented 56.3% share price discount to Net Asset Value, which expanded significantly from the five-year average of 45.6%. The explicit outlier call-out that contradicts conventional holding company analysis is Ferrari's deliberate downward revision of its 2030 fully electric vehicle penetration target from 40% to 20%, which paradoxically strengthens rather than weakens the luxury brand's long-term margin sustainability. At its landmark Capital Markets Day, Ferrari adjusted its long-term 2030 powertrain mix targets, reflecting slower global EV adoption, as the company's prior goal of achieving a 40% fully electric sales mix by 2030 was halved to 20%, while internal combustion engines were upgraded to a 40% target mix.

While this strategic pivot appears defensive and risks environmental criticism, it actually validates Ferrari's pricing power independence from regulatory-driven electrification mandates that are crushing mass-market automotive peers. Ferrari's brand desirability remains intact, with its order backlog fully covered until late 2027, and this extreme exclusivity allows Ferrari to avoid the dilutive promotional incentives currently required by volume OEMs.
