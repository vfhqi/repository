[Materiality of] Investment Thesis (BB 1) Rating = F

Thesis. DCC is a completed 'Major Corporate Change' that ended in a takeover rather than a re-rating. At 6,430p (2026-09-22) the shares are a five-month loan to a recommended, shareholder-approved scheme of arrangement, paying 3.6 to 8.4 percent a year, with about a one-in-ten chance of a 16 to 24 percent fall if the scheme fails. That is a merger arbitrage, not an investment case for Viewforth's capital, and the standalone business beneath it does not clear the 20 percent bar from any price available today.

  • The change inside the company was as broad and as executed as the framework's screen asks for.
    • In November 2024 DCC decided to sell Healthcare and Technology and become a pure-play energy distributor, and it then did what it said.
    • Healthcare was sold in September 2025 for £1,050m of enterprise value, about 12x 2024 operating profit, against a group multiple of about 9.5x
      • Info Tech was sold to Aurelius in November 2025 for £100m of enterprise value
      • the Netherlands solar distribution business was exited
      • Nexora, the remaining Technology business, is for sale with an agreement targeted by the end of 2026.
    • £700m was returned in FY2026: a £600m tender at £51.70 for 11.6m shares and a £100m buyback at an average £47.19, both funded from disposal proceeds
      • a final £100m is scheduled for FY2028 on receipt of deferred Healthcare consideration.
    • The finance and operating leadership changed in July 2025, four Central European markets were entered in May 2026 ahead of schedule, and the company was renamed DCC Energy plc on 16 July 2026.
    • The screen's false-positive checks do not fire: the chief executive is not a caretaker, the disposals were not crown jewels, leverage is 0.9x net debt to EBITDA, and the buybacks were not debt-funded.
  • The financial outputs the change produced were modest, and the ones investors pay for barely moved.
    • Continuing adjusted operating profit rose 3.6 percent to £634.0m in FY2026, of which organic growth was 2.3 percent, about the company's historical average of 2.5 percent.
    • Adjusted EPS rose 9.9 percent to 438.1p, and most of that was the tender's arithmetic: the share count fell 13.7 percent, from 99.0m to 85.4m.
    • Revenue fell 2.9 percent to £15,442m
      • core fuel volumes fell 3 percent
      • Mobility volumes fell 4.6 percent as management chose margin over volume.
    • The replacement engine failed in the year it was meant to prove itself: Energy Services operating profit fell 67.5 percent to £15.7m, with a £43.16m Netherlands solar impairment and a £24.42m UK Services earn-out write-down beside it.
    • Unit margins and cash were the bright outputs: Energy Products operating profit per litre rose from 3.3p to 3.8p, cash conversion reached 108 percent, but the research itself flags the cash as flattered by a £71.4m working-capital inflow from high oil prices.
    • Consensus EPS drifted down over the year, by 1.5 percent for FY2027 and 3.2 percent for FY2028 over twelve months.
      • The sell-side never chased the story upward.
  • The public market did not re-rate the equity for the simplification; private capital bid for it instead.
    • The stock's twelve-month-forward multiple had fallen from about 20x to about 10x over a decade, and two years of break-up did not lift it.
    • KKR and Energy Capital Partners approached at 5,800p in April 2026 and were refused
      • they agreed 6,525p in cash plus up to 125p contingent on the Nexora sale proceeds, a 24 percent premium to the undisturbed price, 7.5x CY2026 EV/EBITDA and 10.5x EV/EBIT, an equity value of about £5.75bn.
    • Deutsche Bank calls that exit multiple virtually no premium to the conglomerate's historical average
      • the board told the offer negotiations that reaching the 2030 Ambition as a public company carried substantial execution risks.
    • The board recommended the offer on 27 July 2026, ISS supported it on 5 September 2026, and the Court and General Meetings approved the scheme overwhelmingly on 18 September 2026.
      • Fidelity, Ninety One, Aviva and the founder Jim Flavin argued the price was too low
      • they were outvoted.
  • The change has travelled the whole 'Six Layer Inputs-outputs' chain to the share price, by a road the pattern does not describe.
    • It shows in the company's people, its decisions, its actions, its unit margins, its financial statements and, weakly, in the sell-side's estimates
    • and it reached the price not through upgrades and a public multiple but through a bid.
    • For a public holder the change is fully mature because it is fully priced.
      • For the 'Major Corporate Change' pattern it stalled at the fifth layer: the defining output, a rising EPS trajectory that the sell-side chases upward, never happened.
  • What the price assumes is completion at about nine in ten and about 50p of contingent; the multiple is the bid, not the business.
    • At 6,430p the price sits 1,049p of the 1,144p from the implied undisturbed level of about 5,381p to the 6,525p base, about 92 percent of the way, ignoring the contingent and time value
    • about 88 percent if the market expects 50p of contingent.
    • The 24-month-forward multiple is 11.68x (2026-09-18), the 87th percentile of the stock's five-year range of 8.28x to 14.21x and the 99th percentile of two years, against a five-year median of 9.85x and a two-year median of 9.61x.
      • Nothing about the business is priced
      • the multiple exists only with the bid.
  • What a buyer at 6,430p earns is the spread to the offer, and nothing else.
    • The three legs of the return, each counted once: cash return nil, because the 147.22p final dividend was paid on 23 July 2026 and the files name no further dividend under the scheme
    • earnings growth nil to the holder after completion
    • re-rating the spread, 95p (1.5 percent) to the 6,525p base or up to 220p (3.4 percent) if the full 125p contingent is paid.
    • Over about five months to completion in the first quarter of 2027 that annualises to about 3.6 percent (base) to 8.4 percent (full contingent)
      • with our expected contingent of about 50p, about 5.5 percent a year.
      • Over eighteen months and three years the total is the same, so the rate falls to about 1.0 to 2.3 percent and 0.5 to 1.1 percent a year.
    • The skew is against the holder: about +2.3 percent in the modal case against about −16 percent if the scheme fails, at roughly nine-to-one odds.
      • The expected value across the branches is about 6,458p, 0.4 percent above the price.
      • The price is fair to the odds, which is the same as saying there is nothing in it.
  • The standalone branch, which opens only if the scheme fails, does not clear the bar either, on the framework's own valuation convention.
    • A break would first take the shares to about 5,300p to 5,400p, the two- and five-year median multiples on today's 24-month-forward earnings of 549.47p, because the multiple that failed to re-rate through two years of simplification would not re-rate on a broken deal.
    • From 5,400p, at 9.85x on our modal FY2030 EPS of 570p plus about 340p of dividends, the eighteen-month return is about 6.7 percent a year
      • over three years about 6.9 percent a year.
      • From today's 6,430p the same path loses 7.4 percent over eighteen months.
    • Our modal standalone EPS path compounds at about 7 to 8 percent a year from FY2026 to FY2029, against consensus of 8.9 percent, and about 3 points of that is the share count already retired
      • the operating growth is 4 to 5 percent a year.
      • A plausibly worse drop-through from EBITDA to EPS, a normal winter, a flat tax rate and dearer debt, takes FY2029 EPS from £5.43 to about £4.70 and the eighteen-month return from a break price to −3.4 percent.
    • Even the highest anchor we would concede, the five-year 75th percentile of 10.98x, gives about 14 percent a year from a break price and under 2 percent a year from today's.
  • The pattern is 'Major Corporate Change', at C for fit.
    • The inputs fit crisply, the outputs were weak, and the payoff mechanism, upgrades pulling the public multiple up, was replaced by a cash exit at the historical multiple.
    • No other 'Target Setup' fits: there is no demand explosion, the stock is not oversold, there is no trough multiple, no estimate cuts, no rebased guidance, no capitulation, no mis-modelled line and no beat-and-raise record.
  • Where the chain holds, and where it breaks.
    • The chain holds from the company's resources and choices through its actions to its unit margins, its cash and its price: every promised step was delivered and the price reached a level the public market would not have given it.
    • It breaks at the return, because the payoff is now fixed by a contract that pays 6,525p to 6,650p in about five months for shares at 6,430p, and no strength in the inputs can lift a payoff the scheme has capped.

  • The case therefore is a cash equivalent with an adverse skew.

    • It is crisp, it is well founded, it is nearly certain, and it pays 3.6 to 8.4 percent a year for five months and then nothing.
    • There is nothing in it for a concentrated long-only investor with a 20 percent bar, and no price on offer today changes that.

  • Rating = F, because the parts bind on the return: the change forces are strong (B) and the outputs modest (D), but the 'Modal return' (F) is fixed by a recommended, approved cash offer at 3.6 to 8.4 percent a year for about five months, and the standalone branch fails the bar as well, at about 7 percent a year from the price a break would set.

    • Not D, because D means a modal return near 10 to 12 percent a year, and neither branch reaches it
    • the only way to 20 percent a year from here is to buy after a scheme break at a price the market would set only if the break were likely.

  • Most plausible competing hypothesis, and our response.

  • The strongest case against our verdict is the bull's: the public market has mispriced a structurally advantaged asset and the takeover proves it.
  • DCC owns the tanks on about 1.5 million customers' sites with 10-to-20-year customer lives
    • it holds about 30 percent share in consolidated European liquid gas markets from which the oil majors have exited
    • it earns 18.8 percent pre-tax return on capital in Energy and has converted 98 percent of profit to cash for 32 years
    • its network is a low-cost base for the transition products (solar, heat pumps, HVO) that fragmented installers cannot match
    • the 2030 Ambition doubles operating profit to £830m and Jefferies thinks the M&A leg is conservative
    • a consortium with better information is paying 7.5x EBITDA and, on Deutsche Bank's arithmetic, expects a 20 percent IRR at 4x opening leverage
    • Fidelity said £70 was the floor and Ninety One, Aviva and the founder agreed
    • Davidson Kempner built 8.13 percent into the vote
    • and the exit multiple of 10.5x EV/EBIT is, by the bidders' own broker, no premium to the conglomerate average.

  • On that view the equity is worth more than 6,525p, the scheme transfers value from public to private holders, and a shareholder who could block or outlast it would earn the difference.

  • What we concede.

    • Every fact above.
    • The buyers probably earn their 20 percent.
    • The public market never paid for the simplification and the board sold at the asset's historical multiple.
    • On a break the assets would attract interest again.
  • The bull's bounded-downside claim, shown properly.
    • The claim is that Energy alone covers the enterprise value.
    • Energy adjusted operating profit was £554.2m in FY2026, an operating-profit line, so it must be valued on an enterprise-value-to-operating-profit multiple.
    • The only such multiple in the evidence is the offer's own 10.5x CY2026 EV/EBIT
    • at 10.5x Energy is worth £5,819m against an enterprise value of £6,367m at £64.25 (market capitalisation £5,538m plus debt £1,885m less cash £1,086m plus minorities £103m and pensions £5.7m less associates £79.2m), a cover of 91 percent.
    • The comparison set gives no enterprise-value multiple
    • the lowest enterprise multiple in the research, UGI International at 6.0x EV/EBITDA, is on a different profit line for which no Energy figure exists, so a like-for-like lower bound cannot be shown, and at any multiple below 10.5x the cover falls below 91 percent.
    • Energy does not cover the enterprise value at the offer's multiple.
    • The Technology business (Jefferies about £800m at 8x EV/EBITA; Deutsche Bank's 2027 operating profit of £85.3m gives about £680m at 8x) is what closes the gap, and its sale is the contingent leg.
  • Why we hold our view.
    • The framework asks what our capital earns from 6,430p, not what the asset is worth to a levered private owner.
    • The scheme is approved by shareholders.
    • The standalone branch opens only on a break, and the first thing a break does is take the price to about 5,400p
    • from there the standalone case, on the framework's convention at the multiple the market actually paid for this business for two years, returns about 7 percent a year, and even at the conceded 10.98x about 14 percent a year from the lower price and under 2 percent a year from today's.
    • The buyers' 20 percent needs 4x leverage the public company never carried and the public market would not have allowed.
    • The competing hypothesis is right about the asset and wrong about the case.
