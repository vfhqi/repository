Section 1 – The Key Question and Why It Matters

Section summary
  1. Assessing fixed-cost leverage and cost cuttability is critical to determining Teleperformance's medium- term earnings resilience under structural AI disruption.

Defining the Key Question

1.
Key Question: "how much fixed-cost operating leverage does this business model carry, how much scope is there for it to work over the next 18 months to four years, and, where it is relevant to the company's strategy, how feasible is cost elimination or reduction, both incremental and in material large cuts?"
2.
Severe structural pressure: Decelerating revenue growth forces global customer experience providers like Teleperformance to aggressively focus on de-leveraging their capital structures and managing costs. The customer care outsourcing industry is currently experiencing severe pricing pressure from clients seeking to reduce costs, while providers face rising compute and AI token fees. Analyzing cost flexibility is essential because flat organic revenue will dilute operating profitability unless SG&A is highly flexible. Determining whether self-help cost measures can offset inflation and volume declines is critical for predicting whether earnings per share will grow or contract through 2028.
3.
Severe compression: Teleperformance shares have suffered a massive valuation collapse over the last few years as the market prices in terminal growth disruption. The company has seen its trading multiples drop precipitously from 30x earnings in 2021 to below 5x earnings as automation fears intensify. This de-rating reflects deep investor concern that generative AI will replace human interactions and compress contract pricing within Core Services. Resolving whether the operating cost base is truly variable will determine whether undemanding valuations represent a value trap or a major buying opportunity.
4.
Weak downside protection: Estimating the margin floor during organic revenue slowdowns remains a primary concern for public market investors evaluating the BPO sector. Cautious client behavior is driving a rapid transfer of activity from high-cost onshore locations to lower-cost offshore hubs like India. While offshoring typically reduces top-line contract pricing and creates headwind, it often supports margin preservation and vendor consolidation. Resolving the leverage question helps size the headwinds to future earnings per share as contracts roll off over the next four years.

Strategic Restructuring and Implications

5.
Aggressive restructuring: Teleperformance is implementing a large-scale cost efficiency and modernization program to adapt its operating model to changing market demands. The company's Future Forward initiative targets significant run-rate savings of €150 million to €170 million to fuel its ongoing digital transformation. These cost actions are designed to sustain robust net free cash flow and protect the bottom line over the next eighteen months.
6.

Client contracts are generally sticky but offer limited opportunities for BPO providers to restructure commercial pricing models outside of scheduled annual renewals. Furthermore, transitioning toward transaction-based and outcome-based models requires major operational adjustments that lag behind volume declines. Restructuring cost bases successfully requires navigating complex client interaction types, limited global footprints, and escalating regional labor costs.
7.

The risk of immediate BPO revenue deflation is overstated because historical transitions like robotic process automation similarly expanded the overall addressable market. Furthermore, BPO exports have remained steady over the last two years, indicating that fears of rapid automated disintermediation are largely exaggerated. This suggests that traditional operating leverage assumptions may hold firm.

Section 2 – Fixed and Variable Cost Splits in the Contact-Centre Model

Section summary
  1. Direct labor accounts for 65% to 71% of outsourced customer experience revenue, leaving BPOs highly exposed to direct capacity utilization and payroll inflation risks.
  2. Fixed overheads like real estate leases, technology hosting, and communication infrastructure create significant operating de-leverage when client volumes contract under traditional hourly pricing.

Direct Personnel and Cost Structure

1.
Significant labor concentration: Personnel expenses represent the overwhelming majority of costs for customer experience providers, reaching 6,157 million euros in Core Services at Teleperformance during fiscal year 2025. This actual result represents 70.57% of the segment's 8,724 million euros of revenue, confirming that personnel represents approximately 70% of Core Services revenues. This extreme cost concentration means operating margins are highly sensitive to payroll inflation and capacity utilization.
2.
Rigid expense profiles: Peer customer experience companies display a very similar reliance on high labor cost structures across their global operations. In the fiscal year ended June 30, 2026, Ibex reported that payroll and related costs accounted for 420 million dollars, or 65% of its total revenue. This metric indicates that labor concentration remains structurally high across the entire customer process outsourcing sector. Consequently, any sudden drop in customer volumes can quickly depress operating margins if personnel hours are not managed dynamically.
3.
Escalating delivery pressures: Other scale operators also demonstrate this rigid cost profile in their latest financial results. TaskUs reported that its cost of service as a percentage of revenue rose to 65.4% in the third quarter of 2026 compared to 62.1% in the prior year period. Management attributed this increase to personnel cost inflation and shifts in geographic delivery mix. These figures confirm that labor cost remains a dominant variable that is difficult to compress in the short term.

Agent Attrition and Labor Flexibility

5.
Gradual capacity adjustment: High employee turnover is often cited as a natural mechanism for BPOs to adjust capacity downward without incurring severe redundancy costs. Analysts have noted that the sector carries a high annual agent attrition rate of 66% to 68% as reported in recent industry analyses. While this attrition allows BPOs to reduce headcount naturally during volume declines, it also drives significant ongoing recruitment expenses. Therefore, the cost savings from natural attrition are partially offset by the constant need to onboard new talent.
6.
Contrary evidence: Some evidence suggests that high employee turnover fails to act as an effective buffer and instead directly damages operating margins during downturns. Ibex warned in its annual filing on October 28, 2026, that a significant increase in the employee turnover rate could materially increase recruiting costs and decrease overall operating profit margins. This shows that attrition is an expensive operating headwind rather than a pure variable cost relief. Thus, relying on natural attrition to manage labor capacity during volume slowdowns can backfire by raising unit training costs.
7.
Declining headcount elasticity: Sector analysts expect slower headcounts and tighter labor markets to complicate workforce adjustments going forward. The IT-BPO Association in the Philippines expects the local industry to add only approximately 50,000 employees annually over the next few years, lower than the increase of 80,000 in 2025. This slowing employment expansion suggests that labor capacity is becoming less flexible as recruitment pipelines tighten. As a result, BPOs may struggle to scale their labor force up or down efficiently as customer volumes fluctuate.

Fixed Overheads and Real Estate

8.
Rigid overhead structures: Beyond direct agent labor, a significant portion of BPO costs consists of structurally fixed overhead and facilities. Teleperformance spent 605 million euros on external expenses in the first half of 2026, compared to 3,466 million euros of personnel costs. These external expenses include structurally fixed elements such as property leases, technology licenses, and communication infrastructure. Because these facilities costs cannot be cut immediately during downturns, they create significant operating de-leverage when revenue contracts.
9.
Compressing margin headroom: Technology infrastructure and cloud hosting fees are becoming increasingly fixed and material cost lines for modern BPOs. Customer service providers are shifting toward advanced digital architectures that require substantial ongoing payments to software and infrastructure vendors. As compute becomes a standard cost line item, BPO gross margin headroom is expected to compress further. This transition means that hosting and compute costs are increasingly rigid, leaving less room for discretionary cost reductions.
10.
Fixed lease burdens: Traditional office space lease commitments represent another highly inflexible overhead cost for customer care providers. While work-from-home models cover a portion of the workforce, BPOs must still maintain substantial physical footprint to satisfy client security requirements. Wider adoption of work-from-home policies might lower demand for office space, but existing long-term leases prevent immediate cost elimination. Consequently, real estate commitments act as a fixed drag on operating margins when volumes decline.

Pricing Models and Margin Operating Leverage

11.
Volatile billing dynamics: The choice of billing model heavily dictates how much operating leverage a provider carries and how closely costs align with volume. Under traditional full-time equivalent or hourly pricing models, BPOs charge clients based on deployed labor capacity. This FTE-based framework links revenue directly to labor hours, meaning that any volume reduction immediately reduces revenue while labor costs remain sticky. Consequently, hourly pricing models expose BPOs to severe margin compression during sudden client volume cutbacks.
12.
Shifting operational risk: To mitigate this risk, some BPOs are transitioning to transaction-based or outcome- based pricing models where fees are linked to completed tasks. Under transaction-based agreements, revenue is generated based on the total volume of interactions processed. While this model offers clients greater cost flexibility, it also shifts the operational volume risk to the BPO provider. Therefore, BPOs must achieve high automation and asset utilization to protect their margins under transactional structures.
13.
Increasing platform scalability: Modern digital-first business models utilize automated platforms to break the linear relationship between headcount and revenue. For example, transaction-based pricing models paired with automated offerings can create significant scalability while maintaining a smaller physical footprint. Unlike traditional labor-heavy providers, highly automated operators can leverage fixed technology investments over expanding transaction volumes. However, this leverage works in reverse when transactions decline, causing rapid margin erosion if hosting fees remain flat.
14.
Pressuring margin economics: Clients are increasingly demanding non-FTE structures, forcing BPOs to balance cost flexibility with transaction risk. Analysts estimate that Genpact generates approximately 45% of its revenue from non-FTE models, while WNS reports about 25% of its revenue from non-FTE structures. This shift requires BPOs to balance automation gains with contract negotiations while taking on more operational ownership. Ultimately, this transition can pressure operating margins near-term due to the high upfront investments required.
15.
Other side of the story: While personnel expenses are highly concentrated, BPOs can maintain margin stability through rapid offshoring and agent utilization improvements rather than structural cost cuts. Industry analyses show that BPOs generate much higher profit margins from agents located in offshore and nearshore regions compared to onshore locations. By aggressively shifting delivery to lower-cost hubs like the Philippines or India, BPOs can offset direct volume de-leverage and maintain stable group margins. This operational flexibility suggests that the cost base is more dynamic than a simple fixed-versus-variable split would imply.

1.
Stable baseline: To evaluate historical margin elasticity, research analyzed 9 BPO peer companies across 18 distinct industry sources and 3 historical cycles. Teleperformance demonstrated resilient top-line growth during the 2009 financial crisis but faced immediate margin pressure. The company reported a consolidated revenue increase of 3.5% to €1,847.7 million in fiscal year 2009 compared to €1,784.7 million in 2008. However, its recurring EBITA margin fell to 8.5% in 2009 from 10.2% in 2008, showing that sticky personnel and reorganization costs created immediate operational de-leverage during the downturn.
2.
Consistent performance: Genpact managed to maintain stable margins during the 2009 recession through disciplined expense management and GE contract stability. The provider reported full-year 2009 revenues of $1,120.1 million, representing a 7.6% increase compared to 2008. Its adjusted operating income margin expanded by 63 basis points to 17.8% in 2009 from 17.1% in 2008, driven by improved productivity and disciplined cost controls which offset pricing pressure. This shows that some transaction-based pricing models can successfully insulate margins.
3.
Severe compression: TTEC Holdings experienced substantial top-line contraction and margin erosion during the 2009 macroeconomic downturn. The company's annual revenue decreased by 16.6% to $1,168 million in 2009 due to lower client volumes and offshore migration. Although TTEC proactively managed underperforming assets, its operating income declined by 7.5% to $100.7 million, proving that fixed overhead costs remained rigid despite aggressive restructuring. This case underlines the severe de-leverage risks when volume declines outpace workforce attrition.

Moderate resilience: Genpact's quarterly results in early 2009 highlighted that pricing concessions and client volume declines posed persistent challenges. During the first quarter of 2009, Genpact's revenue fell 6% sequentially as clients demanded pricing discounts and deferred projects.
