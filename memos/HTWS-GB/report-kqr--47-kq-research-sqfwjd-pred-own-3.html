Section 2 – Components of the Group Tax Charge (FY2023–H1 2026)

Section summary
  1. The group tax charge for Helios Towers has escalated significantly between FY2024 and FY2025 due to deferred tax unwinding and the non-availability of group relief.
  2. Unrecognized tax losses across holding companies in Mauritius, the United Kingdom, and the Netherlands continue to inflate the overall effective tax rate.
  3. Multiple outstanding tax claims and environmental audits in Tanzania and the Democratic Republic of Congo present substantial contingent liabilities.
  4. Helios Towers lacks the structural tax advantages of REIT-status peers, exposing its emerging market operations to statutory tax and withholding tax leakages.

This development represents a major drag on consolidated net profit, thereby reducing the group's overall earnings per share.

Shifting deferred tax: The group deferred tax charge experienced a significant swing from a credit of US$25.7 million in FY2024 to a charge of US$38.3 million in FY2025. This shift was caused by originating temporary differences on capital assets and losses amounting to US$43.3 million in FY2025 compared to a credit of US$28.7 million in FY2024. The rapid unwinding of these deferred tax balances in the Democratic Republic of Congo negatively impacts projected EPS by raising book tax expense.

Unrecognized Holding Company Losses and Leakage

7.
Large unrecognized losses: Helios Towers accumulated US$281.1 million in unrecognized tax losses by the end of FY2025 because these intermediate entities are not expected to generate sufficient future taxable profits. A substantial portion of these unutilized losses resides in Mauritius holding companies totaling US$196.3 million with an associated tax effect of US$29.4 million. Because these losses cannot be group relieved, they create a permanent upward pressure on the consolidated effective tax rate and drag down EPS.
8.
Diverse geographical losses: The group's remaining unrecognized tax losses at the end of FY2025 are split between the United Kingdom holding company at US$51.6 million and the Netherlands entity at US$7.6 million. Additionally, the South African business holds unrecognised deferred tax assets of US$6.9 million on losses of US$25.6 million. Since minimum tax rules do not apply to these holding structures, these losses remain unutilized and continue to depress overall group earnings.
9.
Rigid expiry timelines: Out of the total US$281.1 million in unrecognized tax losses at the end of FY2025, a significant portion of US$196.3 million is subject to statutory expiry within five years. The remaining US$84.8 million of losses is not expected to expire under current local tax regulations. Unless these assets can be utilized against taxable profits, their eventual expiration represents a lost tax shield that restricts long-term EPS recovery.
10.
Restrictive group relief: Helios Towers is unable to group relieve losses recorded in certain holding companies in the United Kingdom, Mauritius, and the Netherlands against taxable profits in operating company jurisdictions. This lack of relief results in a consolidated tax charge that is above the local statutory range of 15% to 30% because profitable operating segments cannot offset holding company overheads. Consequently, this structural tax drag lowers net income and reduces consolidated earnings per share.
11.
Shifting withholding taxes: During the fiscal year ended 31 December 2025, Helios Towers suffered withholding taxes of US$1.7 million, up from zero in the prior year. These charges represent taxation on dividends and interest upstreamed from operating companies to the group's holding companies. This tax leakage directly reduces the cash available at the parent level, which threatens the sustainability of prospective dividend increases and future EPS growth.

Section 4 – Statutory and Withholding Tax Rates by Country

Section summary
  1. Statutory corporate income tax rates across the nine operating markets of Helios Towers range from a low of 15% in Oman to a high of 35% in the Democratic Republic of Congo.
  2. Intermediate holding company structures in Mauritius and the Netherlands help mitigate withholding taxes but suffer from the nonavailability of loss relief at the consolidated group level.

Southern African Tax Regimes

1.
High statutory taxes: The Democratic Republic of Congo imposes the highest statutory corporate income tax rate of 35% among the operating jurisdictions of Helios Towers. Additionally, the local tax authorities levy a minimum income tax of 1% of revenue even when the operating company reports tax losses. Because these heavy fiscal demands apply regardless of profitability, they directly constrain the cash flows needed to support parent-level earnings per share.
2.
Declining corporate taxes: South Africa maintains a standard corporate income tax rate of 27% which was recently lowered from the previous rate of 28%. The country also levies a steep withholding tax on dividend distributions made to foreign shareholders, which can limit net cash repatriation. Consequently, this tax drag reduces the available cash for parent-level distributions, unless specific double taxation agreements are utilized to reduce the withholding rate.
3.
Progressive corporate levies: Malawi applies a standard corporate tax rate of 30% on taxable profits, but a recent amendment imposes a higher rate of 40% on taxable income exceeding 5 billion Malawi Kwacha. This two-tier tax structure increases the fiscal burden on large-scale operators like telecommunications companies. Unless the Malawian subsidiary stays below this high income threshold, the extra corporate tax will directly weigh on consolidated earnings per share.
4.
Competitive tax advantages: International tower peer IHS Holding and global operators like Vodacom Group utilize South African double taxation treaties to optimize their dividend and interest repatriation pathways. Under these treaties, South Africa's standard 20% withholding tax on dividends can be reduced to 5% or 10% for corporate entities holding substantial equity. These structured tax benefits allow peers to minimize cash leakage, enhancing their consolidated earnings per share relative to un-optimized holding structures.

East and West African Regimes

5.
Standardized corporate tax: Tanzania applies a statutory corporate tax rate of 30% alongside a minimum tax levy of 0.3% of revenue for loss-making entities. Local tax laws allow companies to carry forward tax losses indefinitely to offset future taxable income. However, persistent tax disputes over ambiguous regulations can lead to additional assessments that reduce the net profitability and cash flow of the Tanzanian subsidiary.
6.
Stable corporate levies: Senegal enforces a standard statutory corporate tax rate of 30% on telecom operators within its jurisdiction. Despite growing domestic revenues, local authorities frequently introduce specific sector taxes such as levies on international incoming minutes. These additional fiscal measures increase the overall operating costs in West Africa, directly restricting the subsidiary's contribution to consolidated earnings.
7.
Lower headline taxes: Congo Brazzaville enacted a reduction in its corporate income tax rate to 28% effective from 1 January 2026, down from the historical level of 30%. The jurisdiction also imposes a minimum income tax of 1% of revenue on loss-making entities. While this rate reduction lowers the tax expense on profitable operations, the minimum tax rules ensure a continuous cash outflow that limits EPS growth.

Middle East and Island Markets

8.
Elevated royalty costs: Oman imposes a relatively low statutory corporate income tax rate of 15% on corporate profits. However, the Omani government previously increased the standard royalty fee on telecom operators from 7% to 12% of revenue to support the national budget. This heavy royalty burden acts as a top-line tax that reduces the operating margins of local entities, directly impacting consolidated net income.
9.
Favorable income taxes: Madagascar applies a flat statutory corporate tax rate of 20% on corporate profits, which is among the lowest in the African footprint of Helios Towers. However, local operating subsidiaries must also pay ARTEC regulatory fees based on a percentage of turnover. These regulatory fees and turnover taxes erode the benefit of the low statutory tax rate, creating a persistent drag on consolidated EPS.
10.
Divergent sector levies: In the Omani market, major operators such as Oman Telecommunications Company must balance the 15% corporate tax rate with domestic minimum top-up taxes under OECD Pillar Two guidelines. These minimum tax rules ensure an effective tax rate of at least 15% in each operating jurisdiction, which eliminates previous tax holiday advantages. Consequently, this global tax harmonization prevents multinational telecom groups from utilizing low-tax shelters, putting downward pressure on future EPS.
11.
Heavy sustainability levies: Ghana levies a corporate income tax of 25% but also imposes a Growth and Sustainability Levy of 1% that was recently increased to 3% for certain entities. The country also imposes an 8% withholding tax on dividend distributions made to foreign entities. These cumulative levies elevate the tax burden on telecom infrastructure providers, directly restricting the cash available to be upstreamed to parent companies.

Holding Company Treaty Protections

12.
Optimized tax structures: Helios Towers utilizes intermediate holding companies in Mauritius and the Netherlands to benefit from double taxation treaties that lower withholding tax rates. Under these tax treaties, withholding taxes on interest and dividends upstreamed from African operating subsidiaries can be significantly reduced. However, the group cannot offset the unrecognized tax losses of these holding entities against profitable operating segments.
13.
Contrary evidence: In comparable emerging market tower operations, historical changes in tax structures have occasionally increased the tax burden despite existing treaty protections. For instance, the Democratic Republic of Congo levied an additional tax of $180 million annually on its mobile operators. This precedent shows that local governments can bypass double tax agreements by introducing new sector-specific levies, directly impacting the projected cash flow and earnings per share of infrastructure companies.
14.
Other side of the story: Although high statutory rates and withholding taxes represent a material drag on cash flows, some jurisdictions are planning corporate tax rate cuts that could significantly improve consolidated profitability. For example, the Portuguese government approved a gradual corporate tax reduction of 1% per year from 2026 to 2028. If similar tax-cutting policies are adopted across the core African markets of Helios Towers, the reduction in statutory rates will directly boost group earnings per share.
