Section 3 – Industry Structure + Concentration + Value Chain Economics

Section summary
  1. highly fragmented European scrap market is driven by decarbonisation mandates, with regulatory retention keeping local scrap within Continental borders
  2. volatile Turkish import scrap indices dictate domestic pricing, while processing complexity determines the margin split between ferrous and non- ferrous
  3. low consolidation at the collection level leaves the top players exposed to aggressive volume-over-ROCE competition from local operators
  4. elevated Chinese steel exports and volatile European energy costs squeeze electric arc furnace margins, dampening immediate scrap demand

Global Industry Scale and Regional Trajectories

1.
Massive European market: the metal recycling sector comprises approximately 5,500 operating firms across the European Union.
2.
Highly fragmented base: most of these firms are small, undercapitalized, and geographically dispersed.
3.
Decarbonisation growth driver: steelmaking transition from blast furnaces to scrap-intensive electric arc furnaces underpins long-term demand.
4.
Regional supply dynamics: the United States remains a major net exporter of ferrous scrap globally.
5.
Turkey export destination: Turkey and India represent the two largest offshore destinations for U.S. scrap exports.
6.
Freight-sensitive supply chain: logistics and handling represent the most volatile cost segments for exporters.
7.
Regulatory onshoring push: the European Union Critical Raw Materials Act supports onshoring recycling capabilities.
8.
Scrap export friction: new Waste Shipment Regulations starting in 2027 restrict non-hazardous scrap exports to non-OECD countries.
9.
Strategic raw material: scrap is increasingly treated as a strategic resource rather than simple waste feedstock.

Value Chain Economics and Pricing Leverage Factors

10.
Pyramid flow structure: metal flows upward from small local peddlers to regional processors.
11.
Small yard aggregator: smaller dealers aggregate local truckloads to secure better pricing from major mills.
12.
Large trader role: global traders operate at the absolute peak of the pyramid to supply end smelters.
13.
Single-digit operating margins: pure recycling activities generally yield tight single-digit EBIT margins due to intense buying competition.
14.
Value-added premium: advanced processing of non-ferrous fractions commands much wider margins than bulk ferrous volumes.
15.
Specialised waste comparison: hazardous waste recyclers like Befesa achieve superior margins of around 20% EBITDA.
16.
Turkish pricing benchmark: global scrap markets track the heavy melting scrap import CFR price in Turkey.
17.
Turkish domestic link: Turkish rebar FOB prices serve as a primary leading indicator for domestic U.S. monthly pricing.
18.
Index-linked pricing: large-scale Turkish mill buyers purchase ferrous scrap 100% on index-linked formulas.
19.
Contract duration mismatch: spot export shipping pricing contrasts with domestic monthly contracts.

Concentration Metrics and Competitor Dynamics

20.
Low industry concentration: the scrap recycling midstream remains highly fragmented and asset-light on the trading side.
21.
Low top-5 share: no single operator dominates the collection market, despite consolidation efforts by global players.
22.
Regional density focus: consolidation success depends on local collection density rather than global scale.
23.
Direct-to-mill arrangements: large manufacturers establish bilateral scrap return agreements with steel mills.
24.
Formula-based pricing: steelmakers like Steel Dynamics secure prime scrap directly at formula-based prices.
25.
Midstream disintermediation: bilateral contracts bypass independent scrap yards for high-volume clean industrial scrap.
26.
Competitor pricing posture: independent private operators like SA Recycling demonstrate agile, highly competitive buying behaviors.
27.
Volume over ROCE: some players prioritize plant utilization, resulting in irrational margin bidding during scrap shortages.
28.
High-cost mill idling: elevated scrap prices combined with low demand force high-cost Turkish mills to idle.
