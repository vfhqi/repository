Section 2 – Net Financing Costs and Refinancing Pressures

Section summary
  1. Vodafone faces significant headwinds from diminished financing income as spent disposal proceeds and redeemed preference shares no longer generate yield.
  2. Higher interest rates will drive up refinancing costs as cheap legacy bonds mature, although the company maintains a long average debt maturity profile. Net

read: Peer comparisons show that while Vodafone faces notable interest and lease expenses, its refinancing pressure is less severe than Telefónica's extreme free cash flow drag.

Spent Proceeds and Income Drags

1.
Diminished returns: Vodafone Group experienced a drop in interest-bearing assets because cash proceeds from its major Southern European divestments have been fully deployed. In January 2026, Zegona Communications completed the settlement of the €900m preference shares by paying €975m of total cash to Vodafone. These spent funds can no longer support the group's financing income lines. This reduction in non-operating assets implies that Vodafone's net financing costs will rise over the next four years unless offset by operational interest received.
2.
Redeemed assets: Zegona Communications utilized €975m of its FiberCo transaction proceeds to pay accrued preferential dividends and redeem the preference shares in full in early 2026. This transaction completed the full repayment of the Vodafone Group financing structure related to the Spanish asset sale. However, the redemption means that Vodafone has lost a high-yield instrument that paid a 5% dividend annually. This loss of recurring cash inflows will drag down bottom-up earnings per share.
3.
Altered models: Deutsche Bank Research highlighted that while the Zegona redemption boosted short-term liquidity, it removed a major non-operating support. Analysts now expect that Vodafone's financial metrics must rely solely on organic cash flows from continuing European and African segments. This structural shift complicates the bottom-up EPS progression for fiscal year 2027. Consequently, brokers are adjusting their interest income models downward to reflect the absence of these temporary investment yields.

Debt Maturities and Refinancing Yields

4.
Maturity schedule: Vodafone Group manages its refinancing risk by maintaining a varied bond maturity profile spanning up to 60 years. The company has senior refinancing needs averaging €1.0 bn per annum from fiscal year 2027 to fiscal year 2031. This well-spread maturity schedule protects the group from sudden liquidity shocks in volatile capital markets. The stable maturity distribution means interest expense increases will emerge gradually as older tranches mature.
5.
Rising yields: Deutsche Bank Research reported that Vodafone's tender offer to redeem €600m of its 1.5% 2028 senior notes with a new bond issue will escalate interest expenses. This refinancing swap is estimated to generate incremental interest charges of c. €40m. While the pricing was at the lower end of initial guidance, the coupon step-up illustrates the higher cost environment. These rising interest costs will directly pressure adjusted net income margins.
6.
Contrary evidence: Some analysts argue that Vodafone's c. 3% average fixed cost of debt remains highly insulated from near-term rate fluctuations. The group's financial reports confirm that after hedging, substantially all bonds are maintained on a fixed interest basis. This high hedging ratio limits any immediate P&L sensitivity to a mere €9m pre-tax profit decline per 100 basis point interest rate increase. This high rate insulation suggests that near-term refinancing pressures are more manageable than initially feared.
7.
Expensive funding: Vodacom Group consolidated Safaricom's debt footprint after lifting its stake to 55% on 30 June 2026. The consolidated African debt structure carries an average funding cost of 11% for Kenyan Shilling denominated borrowings. This high-cost local currency funding is recognized directly in consolidated net finance costs. Consequently, the Safaricom consolidation introduces a high-yield interest headwind that offsets lower-cost European debt.

Hybrid Bonds and Lease Obligations

8.
Rating upgrades: Moody's Ratings upgraded three of Vodafone's hybrid instruments worth approximately $4 bn to Baa3 from Ba1. This rating action aligns all hybrids at Baa3, positioned one notch below the senior unsecured rating of Baa2. The upgrade reflects that these hybrid bonds are no longer considered preferred equivalents under modified terms. This positive rating shift improves Vodafone's secondary market liquidity and lowers future hybrid issuance spreads.
9.
Lease liabilities: Vodafone Group reported that lease interest obligations rose to €663m in the first half of fiscal year 2027. This is up from €615m in the comparative prior period, driven by network lease additions. Total lease liabilities reached €12,777m, representing a significant off-balance sheet funding obligation. These cash outflows for leases act as a constant drag on adjusted free cash flows.
10.
Comparative pressure: Orange SA maintains a stronger credit rating of BBB+ stable compared to Vodafone's BBB rating. Orange has actively refinanced legacy debt, paying €774m in lease repayments in the first half of 2026. In contrast to Vodafone's high hybrid burden, Orange benefits from lower overall hybrid spreads due to its superior credit profile. This rating differential means Orange faces less financing headwind on its adjusted net income.
11.
Deleveraging focus: BT Group successfully restructured its capital requirements, reducing its triennial pension deficit to £0.3 bn through the issuance of zero-coupon bonds. BT maintains high leverage of 2.4x net debt to EBITDA, while targeting a stronger credit profile. To achieve a BBB+ rating, BT requires a 0.5x net leverage reduction before enhancing shareholder returns. This capital discipline mirrors Vodafone's own deleveraging targets.

Refinancing the entire fixed-coupon debt stack would result in a massive drag of over 30%. This heavy interest expense pressure highlights Vodafone's relatively stable financing cost trajectory in comparison.

Other side of the story: While rising financing expenses create headwinds, Vodafone's aggressive liability management and bond buybacks could neutralize this P&L drag. Section 3 – Non-Controlling Interests and African Segments

Section summary
  1. Vodacom successfully consolidated Safaricom after completing a thirty-five billion rand transaction on June 30, 2026, which lifted its effective shareholding to fifty-five percent.
  2. Ongoing litigation in the Kenyan Court of Appeal introduces near-term legal uncertainty, though the status quo stay allows Vodacom to maintain operational control and financial consolidation.
  3. Significant dividend leakage to non-controlling interests and narrow losses in the early-stage Ethiopian expansion continue to weigh on the group's consolidated net cash flows.

Safaricom Consolidation and Legal Risks

1.
Successful consolidation: Vodacom Group expanded its East African footprint by completing the acquisition of an additional 20% effective stake in Safaricom on June 30, 2026. This landmark transaction was valued at approximately $2.1 bn or R35 bn, and it raised Vodacom's total effective ownership to 55%. Under the agreements, Vodacom bought a 15% stake from the Government of Kenya and an effective 5% from Vodafone Group. This majority ownership allows Vodafone's African subsidiary to transition Safaricom from equity accounting to full financial consolidation, boosting revenue scale.
2.
Severe regulatory: Safaricom's consolidation remains clouded by active litigation after the Kenyan High Court nullified the government's stake sale on September 15, 2026. The High Court of Kenya ruled that the state's divestment of its 15% shareholding was unlawful. However, the Court of Appeal in Nairobi granted a stay of execution on October 13, 2026, ordering that the prevailing status quo be maintained pending the full appeal hearing. This stay allows Vodacom to preserve its registered shareholding and continue full consolidation while the legal process is fast-tracked.
3.
Diluted control: Non-controlling interests in Safaricom represent a substantial portion of the consolidated capital structure following the ownership shifts. Specifically, Safaricom's ownership is now divided between Vodacom with 55%, the Kenyan government with 20%, and public investors with 25%. This structural split means that 45% of Safaricom's net income is allocated directly to noncontrolling interests. This significant minority share reduces the ultimate earnings accretion that flows back to Vodafone's European parent company.
4.
Contrary evidence: Some analysts argue that the Kenyan legal dispute represents a negligible headwind that will not impact Vodafone's medium-term cash flow profile. Deutsche Bank Research noted on September 30, 2026, that the Safaricom acquisition has a negligible impact on Vodafone's FY27 adjusted free cash flow, though the transaction remains highly positive for below-the-line accretion from fiscal year 2028 onward. This suggests that even a negative legal outcome would not destabilize near-term consolidated cash generation.
5.
Complex financial: Vodacom secured future cash distributions by entering into a dividend purchase agreement with the Government of Kenya. Under this agreement, Vodacom paid R5.1 bn upfront to acquire rights to a portion of future state dividend distributions. While this mechanism lowers the earnings attribution to non-controlling interests, the upfront funding was financed with Kenyan shilling debt carrying an interest rate of 11%. Consequently, high financing costs will offset some of the dividend benefit in the near term.

Ethiopian Expansion and Minority Losses

6.
Strategic investments: Safaricom continues to fund and expand its greenfield mobile network in Ethiopia through the Global Partnership for Ethiopia B.V. This Netherlands-incorporated vehicle is fully consolidated by Safaricom, which holds an effective interest exceeding 50% in the entity. Consortium partners, including Sumitomo Corporation with 23.56% and British International Investment with 9.52%, hold the remaining minority stakes. These partners maintain contractual catch-up rights that complicate the allocation of capital contributions and losses.
7.
Narrowing losses: Safaricom Ethiopia is approaching a critical financial inflection point as its mobile operations scale rapidly. During the first half of fiscal year 2027, the Ethiopian segment grew its customer base by 38.4% to reach 15.43 million active users. Consequently, EBITDA losses narrowed significantly by 96.5% to KShs 0.4 bn, bringing the business close to EBITDA break-even. However, high capital expenditure and a net income loss of KShs 8.8 bn continue to drag down overall group profitability.
8.
Aggressive modeling: Morgan Stanley adjusted its financial models to reflect the full consolidation of Safaricom's financial results. The broker raised its pro-forma headline earnings per share forecasts for Vodacom to R12.29 for fiscal year 2027 and R14.17 for fiscal year 2028. However, these projections assume that the Safaricom transaction successfully closes and is consolidated without further legal disruption. If the transaction fails, brokers will have to downwardly revise these estimates to account for Safaricom as an associate once again.
9.
Severe leakage: Vodacom experienced a temporary mismatch in cash flows because Safaricom's consolidation occurred midway through the financial period. Net dividends paid to non-controlling interests reached R5.4 bn in the first half of fiscal year 2027. This cash outflow included R2.6 bn paid to Safaricom's 45% non-controlling shareholders for their fiscal year 2026 final dividends. Because Safaricom was only consolidated from the second quarter, its organic free cash flow did not fully offset this major dividend payout.

Global Peer Comparisons and Minority Structures

Complex structures: Deutsche Telekom manages a similar consolidation structure through its majority control of T-Mobile US. maintains active minority shareholdings and executes massive share buybacks valued at $14.6 bn in fiscal year 2026. However, Deutsche Telekom's CFO indicated that with T-Mobile US shares trading at around $150, the parent has limited appetite to participate by selling into the buyback. This conservative approach contrasts with Vodacom's active buyout strategy to secure outright operational control.

Active operations: Telefónica manages significant non-controlling interests through its publicly listed subsidiary, Telefónica Brasil. Over the past two years, Telefónica Brasil distributed only 70% of its equity free cash flow through dividends and capital reductions. Within this structure, Telefónica's consolidated free cash flow only reflects the minority dividend contribution rather than the full capital returns. This arrangement overstates the cash flow that actually accrues to the parent company, highlighting a similar tracking challenge faced by Vodafone.
12.
Significant exposure: Telecom Italia holds a controlling 67% stake in its highly profitable Brazilian subsidiary, TIM Brasil. TIM Brasil has demonstrated strong operational metrics, delivering an EBITDA margin of 50.4% in the third quarter of 2024., Deutsche Bank Research reported that persistent currency depreciation of the Brazilian Real historically weighs on Telecom Italia's Euro-based growth. This highlights the macro and foreign exchange risks that both Telecom Italia and Vodacom face when relying on volatile emerging market operations.
13.
Unsuccessful divestment: Telecom Italia's historical attempt to exit the Brazilian market demonstrates the financial trade-offs of divesting high-performing emerging market assets. In January 2016, Telecom Italia evaluated a potential sale of its 67% stake in TIM Participacoes to de-lever its balance sheet. However, analysts determined that the cash proceeds would be largely offset by the loss of consolidated EBITDA. This precedent suggests that Vodacom's consolidation of Safaricom is strategically vital because divesting African assets would severely damage the group's consolidated growth trajectory.
14.
Other side of the story: While consolidated minority interests and legal disputes introduce short-term volatility, the financial services and fintech scale unlocked by Safaricom will secure long-term earnings growth. Shameel Joosub, CEO of Vodacom Group, stated that combining the platforms creates one of Africa's largest fintech systems with R43.1 bn in pro-forma financial services revenue. This massive transactional scale suggests that operational and financial benefits will eventually outweigh the legal and minority leakages.

Section 4 – Taxation, Deferred Assets, and Depreciation

Section summary
  1. Vodafone Group faces significant accounting volatility and non-cash tax headwinds from massive deferred tax asset derecognitions in the United Kingdom and Germany.
  2. The recovery timeline for corporate tax losses spans several decades, while German trade tax assets remain short-term cash tax shields.
  3. Consolidated depreciation and amortisation charges will rise following the integration of Safaricom and the peak capital expenditure for the British network build.

Tax Rates and Deferred Assets

1.
Volatile reporting: Vodafone Group reported a negative effective tax rate of 864.3% in the first half of fiscal year 2027 compared to positive 50.2% in the prior year period. This extreme fluctuation reflects a large tax charge on a reported pre-tax loss, driven primarily by asset write-downs. Consequently, reported statutory earnings will continue to diverge sharply from operational performance.
2.
Stable adjustment: Vodafone Group recorded an adjusted effective tax rate of 23.3% for the first half of fiscal year 2027, down from 27.4% in the prior year. This non-GAAP measure excludes the massive non-cash deferred tax asset write-downs in the United Kingdom and Germany. Management expects the underlying adjusted rate to remain in the high twenties percentage range over the medium term.
3.
Luxembourg benefits: Vodafone Group continues to utilise its massive pre-2017 tax losses in Luxembourg, recording a deferred tax charge of €165m in the first half of fiscal year 2027. The recovery period for these recognised assets is estimated at forty-six to fifty years from the balance sheet date. However, planned rate reductions by the Luxembourg government could write down these asset values by c. €630m.

Regional Losses and Recovery Timelines

4.
German write-downs: Vodafone Group reassessed its German operations and wrote down its corporate income tax deferred tax assets by €169m in the first half of fiscal year 2027. The remaining €821m of German corporate tax losses is forecast to be utilised over fifty years due to updated profitability forecasts. This slow recovery timeline highlights persistent operational pressures within Vodafone's largest European segment.
5.
Trade tax cushions: Vodafone Group expects to utilise its fully recognised German trade tax loss deferred tax assets of €408m forecast to be used within eight years as of November 2026. This timeline has lengthened compared to the four to five years projected in the annual report published in May 2026. This elongation reflects the staggered corporate income tax rate reductions enacted in July 2025, which require periodic asset remeasurements.
6.
UK derecognition: Vodafone Group executed a total deferred tax asset derecognition of €2,390m in the United Kingdom following the formation of a single UK tax group. The integration of Three UK means head office interest and treasury costs will offset taxable profits in the combined entity. This derecognition has no current cash tax impact but will create ongoing statutory P&L tax rate volatility.
7.
Budget impacts: Vodafone Group experienced an increase in the recovery period for its UK tax assets due to legislative changes in the November 2025 UK Budget. The budget reduced the rate of deductible capital allowances for plant and machinery from eighteen to fourteen percent per annum. This policy change directly slows down the rate at which capital investments can offset the company's taxable income.

Consolidated Depreciation Profiles

8.
Rising charges: Vodafone Group reported total depreciation and amortisation on owned and leased assets of €6,545m in the first half of fiscal year 2027. This is higher than the €5,960m reported in the prior year period, driven by network lease additions. This rising non-cash expense curve will act as a persistent drag on statutory earnings per share.
9.
Safaricom integration: Vodacom Group finalised its purchase price allocation for Safaricom, estimating an annualised post-tax depreciation and amortisation charge of approximately R1.8bn. This is down slightly from the initial R2.0bn estimate, but it still represents a major non-cash charge. Consequently, the full consolidation of Safaricom's asset base will substantially inflate the group's D&A curve over the next four years.
10.
UK network builds: VodafoneThree plans to execute an £11bn network investment plan over ten years to build the best UK mobile network. Deutsche Bank Research reported that peak capital expenditure is expected in fiscal year 2027 at approximately £1.4bn. This heavy front-loaded capital spending will drive up owned depreciation charges as new 5G standalone cell sites are activated.
11.
Contrary evidence: Some analysts argue that Vodafone's capital expenditure efficiencies will quickly offset the rising depreciation burden of its new network assets. Morgan Stanley reported that the UK business is targeting operating free cash flow to more than triple by fiscal year 2032 due to massive capex efficiencies. This efficiency improvement suggests that the cash flow benefits of the network build will outpace the non-cash depreciation drag on bottom-up earnings.

Peer Dynamics and Synergies

12.
MasOrange consolidation: Orange SA successfully manages the integration of MasOrange in Spain, which was formed from a merger in 2024. ING Wholesale Banking reported that Orange expects the joint venture to accelerate its deleveraging plan and maintain stable medium-term margins. This operational precedent suggests that large-scale network consolidation can deliver substantial cash flow synergies that mitigate rising asset depreciation.
13.
BT pension constraints: BT Group faces significant cash flow constraints from massive network upgrade commitments and recurring, sizeable pension payments. BT Group successfully reduced its triennial pension deficit, but it must still manage a highly geared balance sheet. In comparison, Vodafone's lack of heavy UK defined benefit pension liabilities provides superior financial flexibility to absorb the tax asset write-downs.
14.
Other side of the story: While massive deferred tax asset derecognitions and rising depreciation expenses will depress statutory earnings, these non-cash charges have no impact on Vodafone's cash tax outflows or underlying free cash flows. Morgan Stanley adjusted its model to show that better operational EBITDAaL and lower cash taxes will drive bottom-up EPS upward by eighteen percent over the next three years. This cash-focused perspective implies that long-term equity valuation will remain anchored to cash flow generation rather than statutory accounting adjustments.

Section 6 – FX Volatility, Hyperinflation, and Joint-Ventures

Hyperinflation and Translation Dynamics

1.
Volatile translation: Turkey represents a significant hyperinflationary risk where consumer price indexes rose by 13.0% during the six months ended 30 September 2026. Applying IAS 29 led to an operating profit reduction of €146 million and a financial period loss of €197 million for Turkish operations. These non-cash adjustments will continue to depress reported European segment earnings as long as the Turkish Lira remains highly volatile.
2.
Robust pricing: Vodafone Turkey successfully mitigates currency devaluations by implementing inflation-linked price hikes to protect service revenue. Service revenue grew by 13.8% on a reported basis in the first half of fiscal year 2027, which includes a 5.5 percentage point benefit from hyperinflation accounting. Excluding these accounting adjustments, underlying organic growth reached 8.3% due to strong digital service demand and commercial base management.
3.
Encouraging recovery: Morgan Stanley reported that Vodafone's Turkish service revenue recovered to positive territory in early fiscal year 2027 after dropping late last year. This recovery is supported by price floors that adjust automatically to offset persistent inflation and currency depreciation. Consequently, Turkish EBITDAaL grew by an impressive 9.0% in euro terms, indicating that price actions can outpace currency weakness over the medium term.
4.
Sustained depreciation: Deutsche Bank Research assumes persistent, ongoing depreciation across all of the emerging market currencies to which Vodafone is exposed. Despite local price adjustments, long-term currency devaluations in Turkey, Egypt, and South Africa will act as a constant headwind. These currency headwinds will weigh on consolidated euro earnings translation over the next four years.
