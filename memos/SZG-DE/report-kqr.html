Section 1 – The Key Question and Why It Matters

Section summary
  1. Tighter trade barriers and robust automotive contract price resets are expected to structurally re- base the earnings per share of European steel and stainless producers.
  2. Potential import leakage routes and high domestic decarbonization costs remain critical risks that could offset these policy benefits if regional demand fails to recover.

Section 4 – Import Leakage Routes and CBAM Circumvention

Section summary
  1. Persistent import leakage routes and regulatory circumvention continue to undermine the effectiveness of European trade protections.
  2. Upstream slab arbitrage and downstream finished goods imports allow foreign producers to bypass safeguards ahead of the 2028 carbon border charges.
  3. Regional steelmakers face intense pressure from resource shuffling as low-carbon production is selectively routed to the European Union.

Upstream Slab Quota Arbitrage

1.
Surging slab imports: This analysis covers a total of 15 relevant sources, identifying 3 key leakage routes and 1 comparable past case. Importers are increasingly utilizing slab imports because they are completely exempt from European steel safeguard import quotas. This structural loophole allows European re-rollers to import raw slabs and process them domestically, which means foreign steel enters regional value chains unchecked. Consequently, this pathway undermines the 47% reduction in finished steel quotas implemented on 1 July 2026.
2.
Lucrative default cost arbitrage: Brazilian slab exports to the European Union have surged due to a massive carbon cost discrepancy. Under the definitive Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism phase, imported Brazilian slab faces a default carbon cost of approximately €30 per tonne. In contrast, ramping up primary European blast furnace steelmaking incurs a staggering Emissions Trading System cost of €150 per tonne. This cost difference means that importers enjoy a €120 carbon arbitrage over domestic primary steel producers.
3.
Declining Russian slab supplies: The seaborne slab leakage route faces an impending regulatory cliff as Russian imports are phased out. Historically, the Russian Federation represented approximately 55% of total European slab imports, which are scheduled to reduce to zero by October 2028. This progressive restriction has already forced the annual Russian slab quota down from 3.75 million tonnes to 3.0 million tonnes. Therefore, domestic flat steel producers must scramble to secure alternative regional feedstock before the final ban.
4.
Strategic consolidations: Salzgitter has positioned itself to exploit the tightening merchant slab market by acquiring full control of Hüttenwerke Krupp Mannesmann. The target facility has 1 million tonnes of excess marketable slab capacity, which is highly strategic because 3.5 million tonnes of Russian slab imports will leave the market by 2028. Salzgitter plans to downsize the plant's total capacity to 2 million tonnes to optimize long-term efficiency. This integration ensures that Salzgitter secures a stable slab supply while peers remain exposed to seaborne supply shocks.
5.
Stagnating secondary market supply: Expert interviews indicate that the global merchant slab market is highly constrained and cannot easily replace lost imports. An expert call with a former business development head at Liberty Steel confirms that European mills face severe feedstock insecurity. The global merchant slab market totals only 25 million tonnes annually, meaning that spare global slab capacity is extremely limited. Unless domestic blast furnace restarts accelerate, European re-rollers will struggle to maintain their current rolling utilization rates.

Downstream Value Chain Leakage

6.
Pervasive value chain shuffling: Exporters are successfully dodging upstream carbon tariffs by shifting their manufacturing focus to downstream finished products. This structural evasion involves performing energyintensive processing, such as rolling or forming, outside the European Union. By importing completed components rather than raw hot-rolled coils, foreign firms completely bypass the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism border charges. Consequently, this leakage route severely erodes the competitive shield promised to domestic steelmakers.

This policy divergence is expected to distort regional manufacturing supply chains.

Resource Shuffling and Rerouting

10.
Ineffective environmental shielding: Foreign steelmakers are engaging in extensive resource shuffling to access the European market without paying carbon penalties. This practice involves selectively routing a producer's limited low-carbon steel volumes to Europe while redirecting carbon-intensive output to unregulated domestic markets. This commercial reallocation means that the global atmospheric carbon footprint remains unchanged despite strict European regulations. As a result, the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism acts as a trade barrier rather than a genuine global climate tool.
11.
Stricter origin enforcement: Regulatory bodies are implementing mandatory origin tracking to combat resource shuffling and transshipment. Starting on 1 October 2026, the European Commission requires detailed "melt and pour" documentation for all steel imports.
