EXECUTIVE SUMMARY (BLUF) 28 findings

Cham Swiss Properties AG emerged from the April 2025 merger of Ina Invest and Cham Group with an ambitious long-term strategy to double its portfolio to CHF 3 billion and quadruple rental income to over CHF 100 million by 2032, targeting execution without capital increases. The company delivered a consolidated profit of CHF 143.9 million in FY 2025, though this figure remains overwhelmingly driven by CHF 132 million in non-cash revaluation gains rather than recurring operating cash flows.

Operating earnings are heavily concentrated in lumpy promotional property sales, with the CHF 37.4 million profit from 61 Hochhaus I condominium units representing over 86% of operating profit before revaluation. Reported net income remains highly sensitive to non-cash revaluation adjustments driven by macroeconomic interest rate shifts, creating substantial earnings volatility that masks underlying operational performance.

The capital structure remains defensively positioned with a 59.2% equity ratio and 31.4% loan-to-value ratio, providing substantial balance sheet headroom to execute the development pipeline. However, the transition to bond market financing increased the cost of debt, with the inaugural CHF 150 million bond carrying a 1.35% coupon and subsequent issuances priced at 1.55%, raising refinancing costs relative to ultra-low bank debt rates maintained by commercial peers.

Plausible problems to monitor

Cham Swiss Properties AG faces notable governance pressure from inflated executive retention awards that triggered dilutive compensation increases of CHF 2.6 million following the merger, raising concerns about longterm operational governance quality despite the smooth executive transition. While listed Swiss real estate peers PSP Swiss Property and Mobimo command NAV premiums of 19.79% and 25.33% respectively, Cham trades at a 12.54% discount to NAV, suggesting the market remains sceptical about the company's execution credibility or undervalues its development pipeline relative to established commercial landlords. The transition to capital markets financing reveals an increasing borrowing cost trajectory, with the March 2026 bond carrying a 1.55% coupon compared to the inaugural 1.35% rate, and both rates exceed the 1.03% average cost of debt maintained by peer PSP Swiss Property, creating a structural competitive disadvantage in operating margin. Although Cham maintains a defensive 31.4% loan-to-value ratio below PSP's 33-34% range, the upward drift from 29.2% in June 2025 signals gradual leverage expansion as the development pipeline accelerates. [Cross-ref: Section 4]

The flagship Winterthur Rocket and Tigerli project targeting CHF 9.8 million in annual rental income faces a delayed construction start pushed to 2027 with final handover not expected until 2030, exposing near-term cash flow projections to municipal planning risks and extending the timeline before this critical driver contributes to earnings.

Material redesign decisions such as switching the Rocket tower from timber-hybrid to concrete and steel to improve material availability highlight underlying construction cost and supply chain vulnerabilities, while lengthy municipal approval delays represent persistent execution risks that can postpone larger development completions across the entire pipeline. [Cross-ref: Section 3, Section 9, Section 10]

Outliers worth noting

Cham Swiss Properties achieved an exceptional 4.0% pro-forma organic rental growth in FY25, substantially outpacing the 1.3% reported by PSP Swiss Property and the 2.0% delivered by both Swiss Prime Site and Mobimo, positioning the company as a clear statistical outlier within the Swiss commercial real estate peer group. This unusually strong rental expansion occurs despite the company operating a development-heavy portfolio with elevated vacancy rates, suggesting either superior pricing power in selective micro-markets or measurement distortions from phased project completions rather than true like-for-like organic growth.

The acceleration appears incongruent with the company's transitional post-merger operational profile and raises questions about the sustainability of this momentum once major promotional handovers normalize. [Cross-ref:

The consolidated operating profit before revaluation in FY25 demonstrated extreme concentration risk, with the single Hochhaus I condominium handover representing over 86% of total operating profit at CHF 37.4 million, an unprecedented earnings concentration ratio far exceeding typical Swiss real estate developer norms where recurring rental income provides stable baseline profitability. This lumpy promotional dependency contrasts sharply with peers like Intershop Holding AG and PSP Swiss Property, whose operating models generate consistent recurring cash flows from diversified rental portfolios rather than episodic development completions.

The structural imbalance raises fundamental questions about earnings quality and dividend sustainability during periods between major project handovers. [Cross-ref: Section 5, Section 6] The company's inaugural bond market issuances carry coupons of 1.35% and 1.55% respectively, creating a significant financing cost disadvantage against established peers. PSP Swiss Property maintains an ultra-low 1.03% average cost of debt, while Swiss Prime Site achieved an even lower 0.90% average cost through sophisticated hedging strategies, leaving Cham exposed to a 45 to 65 basis point interest expense premium that will structurally erode operating margins as the bond portfolio scales toward the targeted twothirds debt mix.

This financing disadvantage appears difficult to reconcile with management's stated ability to self-fund the ambitious CHF 3 billion portfolio target without equity raises, particularly if subsequent bond refinancings occur at higher prevailing market rates. [Cross-ref: Section 4] Executive compensation witnessed an inflated CHF 2.6 million increase attributed to retention awards following the merger, representing an outsized governance cost relative to the scale of the combined entity and peer benchmarks for similar corporate integrations within the Swiss real estate sector.

This compensation surge occurs despite the company maintaining a comfortable 59.2% equity ratio that substantially exceeds the strategic minimum threshold of 45%, raising questions about the necessity and proportionality of retention packages when balance sheet strength provides ample flexibility. The magnitude of these awards appears inconsistent with typical Swiss corporate governance norms and suggests potential principal-agent tensions warranting closer shareholder scrutiny.
