Section 2 – Earnings Repeatability, Trading Contribution, and Inventory Effects

Section summary
  1. Sell-side analysts do not expect Shell's record 2026 trading and refining earnings to repeat in 2027 as refining margins normalize.
  2. Shell and BP experience much higher earnings volatility than US peers due to the size and opacity of their trading businesses.

This forecast is driven by refining margins returning to historical averages. Analysts can replicate Shell's refining margin using forward curves of underlying components. Therefore, the exceptional 2026 performance is treated as largely unrepeatable in 2027 forecasts.

Section summary
  1. Shell experiences intense short-term cash flow volatility driven by commodity price swings and regional environmental tax payments.
  2. The lag in cash tax settlements across Europe distorts comparisons of operational cash generation between Shell and its peers.

2.
Rapid reversals: Shell successfully recovered a significant portion of its built-up working capital in subsequent quarters. Management recorded a working capital inflow of $3.4 billion in the second quarter of 2026. The reversal of these price effects restored liquidity to the balance sheet as price spikes normalized.
3.
Contrary evidence: Shell's historical trends suggest that working capital movements are not purely random but follow distinct seasonal patterns. Analysts highlight that Shell's working capital increased during the first quarter in nine out of the last ten years. This predictable seasonality undermines the assumption that quarterly working capital outflows are entirely unexpected macro events.
4.
Unresolved deficits: Shell still recorded a cumulative working capital outflow of $2.16 billion for the first nine months of 2026. This lingering drain was driven by inventory builds and receivables that did not fully unwind. These persistent outflows dampened the overall cash conversion despite exceptionally high asset utilization rates.

Section 5 – Long-Term Structural Headwinds: Carbon Compliance and Reserve Life

Section summary
  1. Shell faces substantial long-term headwinds as annual carbon compliance costs are projected to quadruple to four billion dollars by 2035.
  2. We maintain medium confidence in Shell's ability to manage long-term headwinds based on documented carbon price projections and peer reserve comparisons.

Rising Carbon Compliance Liabilities

1.
Escalating carbon liabilities: Shell plc projected in its Annual Report 2025 that yearly carbon compliance costs will rise from approximately 1 billion dollars in 2026 to 4 billion dollars by 2035 under its mid-price scenario. This rising expense reflects the company's equity share of emissions from both operated and non-operated assets. Consequently, these cash-cost increases are expected to act as a significant headwind, reducing future earnings per share if carbon pricing matches management's baseline assumptions.
2.
Opaque country-level forecasts: Shell plc estimates country-by-country regulatory compliance costs using regional carbon price forecasts, expecting European Union prices of 100 to 140 dollars per tonne through 2035. The company expects Norway to impose the highest carbon cost of 183 to 226 dollars per tonne. These regional variations mean Shell's European refining and production assets will bear a disproportionate share of transition expenses relative to less regulated North American operations.
3.
Aggressive abatement investments: Equinor ASA manages its transitional risk by allocating approximately 3 billion dollars annually toward low-carbon and renewables segments to halve operated Scope 1 and 2 emissions by 2030. This capital strategy is designed to reduce operated greenhouse gas emissions, which dropped 5% year-over-year to 11.0 million tonnes in 2025. Consequently, these proactive investments are expected to insulate Equinor from escalating regulatory taxes on the Norwegian Continental Shelf.
4.
Steeper emission targets: BP plc focuses on a capital-light approach by limiting its low-carbon and renewable energy investments to less than 0.8 billion dollars per year in 2025 to 2027. This target represents a clear strategic choice to prioritize baseline shareholder returns over rapid transition capital spending. Therefore, BP's strategy is designed to protect near-term earnings per share at the expense of potentially higher longterm carbon penalty exposure.
5.
Severe valuation write-downs: TotalEnergies SE allocates half of its 13 to 15 billion dollars of annual investments to growth capex, with about 3 billion dollars dedicated to power and renewables. This balanced investment structure is designed to support a 40% reduction in net operated emissions by 2030. Consequently, TotalEnergies aims to secure long-term transition resilience while maintaining profitable hydrocarbon volumes through the next cycle.

Reserve Life and Production Deficits

6.
Declining asset longevity: Shell plc reported a 9.5-year reserve life in 2025, which is broadly in line with its European peer average. Rothschild & Co Redburn notes that the company's management remains highly confident in its ability to sustain production levels to 2035. This longevity helps to allay near-term investor concerns regarding reserve depletion and supports baseline dividend safety.
7.
Widening production deficits: DNB Carnegie warns that several international oil companies currently possess reserve lives of only 7 years, alongside an unsustainable average rolling reserve replacement ratio of 40%. This industry-wide decline suggests that conventional operators must increasingly rely on corporate acquisitions to sustain their production over the next decade. Consequently, this structural constraint will drive higher sector consolidation and capital requirements.
8.
Persistent resource gaps: Shell plc anticipates a liquids production decline of approximately 350 thousand barrels of oil equivalent per day between 2030 and 2035 based on its existing portfolio. Rothschild & Co Redburn calculates that even after completing the ARC Resources acquisition, Shell still has a 200 thousand barrels per day production gap to fill. Consequently, Shell will likely face diminishing upstream cash flow volumes post-2030 unless it accelerates its exploration campaigns or pursues further M&A.
9.
Misleading reserve comparisons: US majors ExxonMobil and Chevron report lower reserve lives than European conventional operators because US shale assets require stricter SEC booking rules. DNB Carnegie notes that this technical difference makes conventional operators appear to have superior asset longevity. Consequently, comparing Shell's reserve life directly to US peers exaggerates the European majors' advantage unless accounting differences are normalized.
10.
Contrary evidence: Shell's sensitivity testing reveals that applying the IEA Net Zero Emissions by 2050 carbon- price scenario would reduce the carrying value of its Integrated Gas and Chemicals assets by up to 6 billion dollars. EY audited these disclosures and confirmed that under this extreme scenario, Shell's upstream assets would also experience a 1 billion dollar impairment. This suggests that while sell-side analysts consider Shell's current carbon assumptions reasonable, stricter global climate policies would trigger massive non-cash write-downs.

This conservative terminal assumption incorporates long-term risks such as carbon pricing and slowing global demand.
