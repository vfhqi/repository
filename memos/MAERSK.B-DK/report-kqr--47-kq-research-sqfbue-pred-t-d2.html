This extensive precedent dataset reveals that freight rates explained most misses during prior transition periods.

Consequently, this structural imbalance resulted in industry-wide earnings misses against previous optimistic expectations.
3.

This delayed response highlights how sluggish supply adjustments degraded margins and led to severe forecast deviations.

Industry records show that global containership scrapping surged from 197,000 TEU in 2015 to a record 657,000 TEU in 2016 as freight rates collapsed below operating costs. This massive supply adjustment was heavily concentrated in older Panamax vessels that became economically unviable after the new Panama Canal opened. This historical precedent indicates that deep industry losses are required to trigger massive vessel demolition programs during severe downturns.

This rigid fleet structure forces liners to absorb high charter expenses during deep market downturns, accelerating their operational cash burn.

Section 6 – The Frequency and Impact of Recurring Geopolitical Disruptions

Section summary
  1. Repeated geopolitical disruptions continuously derail container shipping normalization forecasts within short twelve-month horizons.
  2. Physical supply shocks absorb substantial vessel capacity and create a highly skewed earnings outlook for Maersk in fiscal year 2027.

Chronology of recurring bottlenecks

1.
Frequent disruption cycles: Global maritime networks have faced consecutive physical supply shocks that repeatedly arrived within twelve months of projected market normalizations. The shipping industry had barely begun transitioning to normal operations before the late-2023 Red Sea crisis and the early-2026 Strait of Hormuz blockade shattered all sell-side stability models. These consecutive crises demonstrate that geopolitical volatility has become a persistent feature rather than a temporary anomaly for container lines. Therefore, Maersk's financial planning must now incorporate frequent and layered supply-chain disruptions as a central baseline.
2.
Sudden Suez blockages: The six-day Suez Canal blockage of March 2021 serves as a stark historical precedent of sudden capacity destruction. That brief disruption immediately backed up hundreds of merchant ships and created an artificial vessel shortage. This historical case suggests that even highly localized chokepoint closures can trigger massive global network delays and surge freight rates.
3.
Prolonged Red Sea diversions: Container shipping networks experienced massive structural shifts when Houthi attacks disrupted the Red Sea in late 2023. The resulting Cape of Good Hope rerouting absorbed approximately 7% of global capacity, or roughly 2.3 million TEUs. This persistent diversion kept the supply-demand balance artificially tight despite a heavy schedule of new vessel deliveries.
4.
Contrary evidence: Mainline container transits through the Suez Canal recovered to 47% of peak levels in late 2026 as some carriers resumed shorter routings. 5.

Carriers that had cautiously returned to the Suez Canal were forced to revert back to Africa detours as security risks escalated. This dual bottleneck effectively removed transit shortcuts, added 10 to 14 days to voyages, and severely dislocated vessel positions. Consequently, the stacking of these disruptions turned operational costs into major capacity constraints.

Rerouting around chokepoints in the Middle East lengthens shipping distances significantly, boosting dry bulk and container tonne-mile demand. Because more ships are required to transport the same volume of cargo, these route adjustments effectively tighten global fleet supply.
7.

Global port congestion reached a multi-year high in late 2026, with over 4.3 million TEUs waiting to berth worldwide. This massive bottleneck tied up roughly 12.6% of the global containership fleet and severely reduced available capacity. These operational delays mean that Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd must deploy extra vessels simply to maintain weekly service schedules.
8.
Elevated cost headwinds: Shifting vessels away from primary trade arteries rapidly inflates operating expenditures. Bunker fuel costs spiked as Middle Eastern hostilities drove Brent crude above 100 dollars per barrel in early 2026. Additionally, war-risk insurance premiums rose to several percentage points of hull value, creating an immediate barrier to transit. This high-cost environment directly pressures Maersk's ocean segment margins unless elevated surcharges are successfully passed to shippers.

Asian liners including HMM, Evergreen, and Yang Ming avoided the Red Sea in September 2026 due to safety considerations. Conversely, state-backed COSCO Shipping continued to operate through regional chokepoints by leveraging resilient maritime security guarantees and fleet scale. This strategic divergence illustrates that private alliances face greater capacity pressure than statesupported operators during geopolitical crises.
