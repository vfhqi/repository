Section 7 – Tax Rate Normalization and Financial Structure Plausibility

Section summary
  1. The plausibility of Medartis's guided 18% to 19% long-term tax rate is low given its historical tax volatility and decentralized manufacturing footprint.
  2. Global minimum tax regulations under Pillar Two establish a structural tax floor that challenges low tax projections for Basel-headquartered medtech multinationals.

Medartis Historical Tax Disconnect

1.
Tax volatility: IFRS tax metrics for Medartis have historically exhibited substantial volatility due to changing regional profit mixes and deferred tax adjustments. The company reported an IFRS effective tax rate of 65% in fiscal 2023, which then shifted to a negative tax credit rate of -41% in 2024. This extreme oscillation suggests that the company's historical financial structure struggled with tax predictability before its recent commercial scaling, making long-term projections difficult to assess. Any persistence of high effective tax rates will directly dilute consolidated earnings per share.
2.
Core charges: Medartis recorded a core tax charge of CHF 5.2 million in fiscal year 2025 against a core net profit of CHF 6.1 million, which demonstrates a clear disconnect from long-term guidance. This significant tax expense relative to core earnings reflects high tax liabilities in foreign affiliates and purchase price allocation adjustments from acquisitions. This tax burden highlights that core net profit remains heavily diluted during geographic expansion, directly challenging earnings per share development.
3.
Interim trends: Medartis faced persistent tax expenses in the first half of 2026 despite improving its underlying operating profitability. The group recorded an IFRS income tax expense of CHF 5.5 million in H1 2026 compared to CHF 1.9 million in H1 2025. These ongoing expenses suggest that reaching the guided longterm tax rate will require substantial operational normalization across foreign subsidiaries. Unless profitability improves in higher-tax regions, net income growth will remain constrained.
4.
Contrary evidence: Medartis demonstrated a major reduction in tax volatility when looking at core tax expenses relative to normalizing operating results in H1 2026. The group's core tax expense of CHF 5.7 million in H1 2026 was largely driven by predictable tax expenses from foreign affiliates. This indicates that operational scaling may stabilize taxes faster than historical rates suggest, helping support earnings.

Management Tax Rate Guidance Plausibility

5.
Management targets: Medartis management guides for a long-term core tax rate of 18% to 19% as the business scales internationally. This target assumes that a global transfer pricing model will successfully allocate profit to value chains at arm's length. Achieving this target is critical because it directly impacts the plausibility of earnings per share expansion over the next three years. A failure to lower the tax rate will offset the operating leverage gained from sales growth.
6.
Transfer pricing: Medartis operates a complex global transfer pricing framework that remains subject to scrutiny and challenges by national tax authorities. The company has not entered into any Advanced Pricing Agreements on a worldwide level as of late 2025. Different interpretations of tax rules by local authorities could jeopardize the long-term tax rate targets if transfer prices are challenged. This transfer pricing risk is heightened by the lack of unilateral advanced pricing agreements.
7.
Geographic risks: Medartis performs production and procurement across multiple international jurisdictions, including Switzerland, France, Brazil, and Belgium. While Basel-Stadt serves as the headquarters, the company relies on retail entities globally to distribute its premium implants. This global footprint means that local tax adjustments will heavily impact the consolidated effective rate. Consequently, changes in foreign tax regulations can easily disrupt management's long-term projections.

Peer Benchmarking and Swiss Tax Structures

8.
Straumann comparison: Straumann Holding AG represents a valuable peer benchmark, reporting an effective tax rate of 21.3% in fiscal 2025 compared to 17.9% in 2024. This peer metric is higher than Medartis's long-term guidance and was driven by foreign tax rate effects on its global footprint. The comparison shows that maintaining a sub-twenty percent tax rate is challenging for Swiss medtech multinational entities. Unless Medartis manages foreign tax credits efficiently, it will likely see a higher rate.
9.
Centralized structure: Medacta Group coordinates its global orthopaedic business from its Ticino-based headquarters and relies on Swiss-only implant manufacturing. This centralized model concentrates taxable profits in Switzerland, which historically offered lower corporate tax rates. This structural design shows that centralizing production can simplify transfer pricing compared to Medartis's multi-plant strategy. It suggests that Medartis's decentralized manufacturing introduces structural inefficiencies.
10.
Manufacturing shifts: Straumann continues to expand its manufacturing site in Villeret, Switzerland, while simultaneously scaling its local production in China. This dual strategy helps manage local tariffs but increases the complexity of global transfer pricing. This operational evolution demonstrates that diversified production footprints elevate compliance risks across foreign jurisdictions, affecting tax planning. Like Straumann, Medartis faces transfer pricing challenges from its US production expansion.

Global Minimum Tax and Pillar Two Impacts

11.
Pillar rules: Switzerland implemented qualified domestic minimum top-up tax rules effective January 1, 2024, to comply with the OECD Pillar Two minimum tax of 15%. The Swiss authorities also introduced the Income Inclusion Rule on January 1, 2025, to tax under-taxed foreign subsidiaries. These structural changes mean that Swiss medtechs face a floor on their minimum corporate tax rates. This framework restricts the ability of Basel-based companies to shift profits.
12.
Pillar liability: Straumann Holding AG recognized a significant Pillar Two income tax liability of CHF 6.1 million in fiscal 2025. Due to its low statutory rate, Straumann's Swiss entities recorded an effective tax rate of 11.9%, triggering top-up tax requirements. This real-world precedent proves that Pillar Two rules will elevate taxes for Basel-headquartered multinationals. It represents a structural headwind that Medartis will have to navigate as it scales.
13.
Safe harbor: Medartis aligns its tax strategy with the OECD inclusive framework on Base Erosion and Profit Shifting. Although its smaller revenue scale may shield it from immediate global top-up taxes, scaling past the threshold will expose its profits to minimum tax rules. Consequently, global minimum tax rules could cap the potential tax savings from low-tax cantons. This structural shift makes a sub-20% rate harder to sustain in the long run.

Strategic Financial Plausibility

14.
Uncertain normalization: Medartis's target of an 18% to 19% long-term tax rate appears highly ambitious and remains unproven by its financial history. The company's recent core tax charges and multi-jurisdictional plant expansions create continuous transfer pricing risks and administrative tax friction. Therefore, we conclude that achieving tax normalization rests on strengths yet to be shown rather than proven capabilities. Any delay in tax rate reduction will restrict EPS growth.

Other side of the story: Medartis could successfully achieve its guided 18% to 19% long-term tax rate if its global transfer pricing model scales alongside profit normalization. As foreign subsidiaries become consistently profitable, accumulated tax loss carry-forwards may be utilized to offset regional tax expenses. This operational development would allow the consolidated effective tax rate to align with management's long-term expectations, bolstering earnings per share.

Section summary
  1. Medartis's earnings are highly sensitive to unhedged exchange rate fluctuations and trade barriers that could permanently depress its core EBITDA margin.

Macroeconomic and Exchange Vulnerabilities

1.
Negative translation: Unhedged Swiss medical exporters face severe reported revenue dilution if the Swiss franc continues its persistent appreciation against major global currencies. For instance, Roche Diagnostics reported that its nine-month sales in 2026 grew by 6% at constant exchange rates but moderated to just 1% in reported Swiss francs due to currency headwinds. Similarly, Sensirion Holding reported a 3% decline in reported Swiss franc revenue during the first half of 2026 despite achieving 6.9% growth in local currencies. If these currency pressures persist, Medartis will likely experience severe reported revenue dilution that offsets its rapid operational growth.
2.
Diluted margins: Medartis has demonstrated high vulnerability to Swiss franc strength, which historically eroded its gross margins and core EBITDA. In the first half of 2025, unhedged currency headwinds reduced the company's gross margin by 0.5 percentage points. Furthermore, its core EBITDA margin for the first half of 2026 would have been 0.9 percentage points higher at constant exchange rates compared to the reported 0.2 percentage points increase. This demonstrates that persistent Swiss currency appreciation could erode projected core EBITDA margins and delay low-twenties targets.
3.
Stabilizing currencies: A potential stabilization of exchange rates in late 2026 could reverse these translation headwinds and support unhedged Swiss exporters. Roche Diagnostics noted that exchange rates began to stabilize in the third quarter of 2026, leading to a strong 8% sales growth in reported Swiss francs. If the Swiss franc depreciates or stabilizes against the US dollar, Medartis's unhedged US revenues would convert at more favorable rates. Consequently, a more stable currency environment would accelerate core profit growth beyond current conservative expectations.
4.
Elevated costs: Unanticipated trade tariffs represent a major headwind that could permanently depress Medartis's gross margins if local production fails to scale. The US government enacted reciprocal tariffs on Swiss-made devices, with rates escalating to 39% in late 2025. These customs tariffs reduced Medartis's gross margin by 0.9 percentage points in fiscal 2025 and 0.6 percentage points in the first half of 2026. If trade relations deteriorate further, unhedged Swiss exporters face permanently depressed gross margins on premium US imports.

Manufacturing Shifts and Procurement Hurdles

Accelerated relocation: Medartis launched Project Flash to accelerate the relocation of its implant manufacturing from Basel to Warsaw, Indiana, as a direct response to US tariffs. The company moved milling machines and lathes to local US facilities with the target of producing 70% of US volumes locally by late 2026. Although this local-for-local shift mitigates tariff exposure, it requires significant capital expenditure and introduces operational execution risks. Any delay in this manufacturing transition would expose the company to continued tariff penalties and margin pressure.
6.
Constrained supply: Geopolitical trade disputes and rapid production ramps expose Medartis to severe supply chain vulnerabilities. For instance, UK corporate filings show that Medartis must actively monitor supply chain disruption as KeriMedical ramps up manufacturing for its major US launch. Furthermore, the company has had to resolve supplier disputes that elevated its general and administrative expenses during the first half of 2026. 7.
Lengthy approvals: Stricter procurement processes at US hospitals present a significant barrier to the rapid adoption of premium extremities implants. Hospital Value Analysis Committees enforce detailed reviews that create approval cycles of up to four months per facility. To counter this, competitors like Acumed provide extensive Value Analysis Committee Resource Guides to streamline hospital procurement. If hospital administrators prioritize cost containment, Medartis will face protracted sales conversion cycles for its TOUCH prosthesis.

Value-based mandates: US healthcare systems are shifting from fee-for-service models toward value-based reimbursement, which heavily impacts premium implant pricing. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services proposed the mandatory CJR-X model for lower-extremity joint replacements starting in October 2027. This initiative forces hospitals to evaluate medical devices based on their total cost across the entire episode of care. Consequently, this regulatory push will squeeze premium device pricing if clinical benefits do not justify the upfront acquisition costs.
