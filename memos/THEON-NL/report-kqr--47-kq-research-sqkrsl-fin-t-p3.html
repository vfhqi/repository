Section 2 – Growth, Working Capital and Leverage Dynamics

Section summary
  1. Theon's aggressive growth trajectory requires substantial working capital absorption that peaks seasonally in the third quarter before unwinding.
  2. The company's transition from net cash to elevated debt reflects strategic acquisitions funded by a syndicated loan facility.
  3. Peer models demonstrate that customer prepayments and disciplined working capital management are crucial for deleveraging.

The Working Capital Cycle

1.
Seasonal buildup: Theon's net working capital absorption remained elevated at 42.7% of trailing revenue in September 2026. This high absorption is driven by seasonal inventory accumulation necessary to support heavy deliveries scheduled for the fourth quarter. Consequently, cash flows are typically back-end loaded as payments from government buyers materialize. This seasonal build-up means that working capital peaks in Q3 before releasing substantial cash in the final months.
2.
Slower expansion: The operational mechanics of Theon's business model create a natural balancing loop during periods of deceleration. When revenue growth moderates, the cash outflows required for inventory procurement and production capacity expansion decrease. This slower expansion reverses the cash-absorption loop by allowing outstanding receivables to convert into liquid assets without being immediately reinvested. Thus, slower growth releases cash and organically deleverages the balance sheet.
3.
Capex flexibility: Theon adjusted its near-term capital expenditure guidance to approximately €25 million for fiscal year 2026. This represents a reduction from the previous target of €30 million due to the strategic phasing of capacity investments. This capex flexibility allows the group to preserve liquidity while maintaining its longterm target of spending about 4% of revenue on expansion. Such flexibility proves that management can defer investments to support cash generation when growth targets face execution bottlenecks.
4.
Contrary evidence: Deutsche Bank Research projected that Theon's aggressive expansion plans will make the reduction of working capital to its long-term target of 35% to 40% of sales much slower than previously anticipated. This slower trajectory is driven by the intensive inventory requirements of newer platform-based products. As a result, the broker lowered its fiscal year 2027 free cash flow conversion forecast to 39% from 49%. This indicates that working capital remains a major drag on cash conversion during rapid scaling.

Acquisition Financing and Debt

5.
Shifted balance: Theon experienced a dramatic shift in its net financial position from end-2025 to late 2026. The group reported a net cash position of €126.9 million at the end of December 2025. However, strategic investments in Exosens and Kappa Optronics shifted the balance sheet to a net debt position of €251.1 million by September 2026. This rapid deployment of capital shows how M&A drives elevated leverage before synergies contribute to profitability.
6.
Debt funding: Theon secured a new syndicated term loan facility of up to €325 million in September 2026 to finance its acquisition of HGH Systems. This facility was coordinated by Alpha Bank to provide long-term financing for the €300 million enterprise value transaction without requiring an equity capital increase. While this debt funding preserves earnings per share from equity dilution, it will push pro forma leverage temporarily to approximately 3.0x EBITDA. This leverage spike is expected to raise interest expenses significantly and dilutes near-term net income.
7.
Elevated leverage: Theon's interest expenses increased by 157.88% during the first half of 2026. This sharp increase was driven by the consolidation of debt from acquired subsidiaries and the servicing of new revolving credit facilities. Deutsche Bank reported that higher leverage and borrowing costs will revise down adjusted earnings per share despite strong operating performance. Consequently, the group must prioritize rapid deleveraging to prevent interest expenses from eroding operating margin gains.
