Section 3 – Legislative Progress and the Fate of the Employer Rebate Ban

Section summary
  1. The French National Assembly successfully passed the meal-voucher reform bill on first reading on 12 October 2026.
  2. The bill's crucial ban on corporate client rebates survived the vote and will rebalance the industry's commission economics.
  3. Issuers are legally mandated to shift toward full dematerialization by January 2028 while delivering transparent fee disclosures.

Legislative Progression and Timeline

1.
Passing first reading: The French meal-voucher reform reached a critical milestone when the National Assembly adopted the draft bill on 12 October 2026. This vote progresses a long-awaited regulatory overhaul that has kept investors anxious since the initial proposals began. The next major step is scheduled for 19 November 2026, when the French Senate is set to examine the legislative text. Analysts expect this timeline to clarify the final rules, resolving a key regulatory overhang that has depressed multiples.

Upcoming Senate review: The legislative text will undergo rigorous scrutiny when the French Senate reviews the proposed framework on 19 November 2026. This review represents a major catalyst because senators will debate the operational details of the voucher system. Dematerialization target: The French legislative text mandates a full transition to digital-only formats by 1 January 2028. This rule permanently phases out traditional paper vouchers, which simplifies voucher acceptance and significantly lowers transaction handling fees. Both major issuers are well positioned because they have proactively migrated most of their user bases to digital cards. Thus, the legally mandated dematerialization represents a major operational tailwind that should drive structural margin expansion.

Mechanics of the Employer Rebate Ban

5.
Surviving assembly vote: The crucial clause banning issuer rebates and discounts to large corporate employers successfully survived the National Assembly vote. Bernstein Research reports that prohibiting these corporate discounts will fundamentally restructure how the French meal-voucher system operates. Historically, issuers used elevated merchant fees to subsidize high volume discounts for their largest corporate clients. Banning this cross-subsidization allows issuers to focus on protecting restaurants and small merchants.
6.
Corporate rebate ban: The legislative framework relies heavily on forbidding corporate discounts to rebalance the voucher market's economics. Morgan Stanley's management roadshow note on 29 July 2026 confirms that the ban on employer rebates remains a core pillar of the reform. Since large employers can no longer demand cash-back incentives, the commercial bargaining power shifts back to issuers. This structural change means that issuers can defend their bottom line without relying on aggressive merchant fees.
7.
Mandatory fee disclosures: To protect small businesses, the bill requires issuers to send affiliated merchants a comprehensive annual statement. This annual disclosure must detail the total commissions and transaction fees paid, expressed both in euros and as a percentage. Providing this level of transparency is designed to expose hidden charges and pacify merchant lobbies that demand hard commission caps. Consequently, this transparency requirement helps issuers maintain corporate goodwill while averting severe statutory fee caps.
8.
Client side rebalancing: Banning corporate rebates is expected to drive a major rebalancing of the industry's commission structure. Bernstein Research points out that France has been one of the last European markets to maintain negative commissions on the client side. Prohibiting discounts will force corporate employers to start paying positive fees to issuers for administering voucher programs. This shift to client-side fees will successfully offset merchant cap pressures.
9.
Transferring fee pressures: Rebalancing commission structures by charging corporate clients represents a logical commercial step under the new rules. Deutsche Bank Research argues that French corporates will progressively stop benefiting from discounts and begin paying positive commissions. This commercial adjustment mirrors similar fee-shifting transitions observed in other regulated European and Latin American jurisdictions. As a result, the transition will allow issuers to stabilize long-term group EBITDA.

Jurisdictional Comparisons

10.
Brazilian rebate ban: The elimination of corporate discounts in France closely mirrors regulatory frameworks implemented in Latin American markets. In Brazil, the government successfully implemented a complete ban on employer rebates and discounts in May 2023. Bernstein Research notes that ending these discounts was designed to rebalance the commercial model in favor of merchants. However, the actual commercial rebalancing in Brazil was only partially successful in reality.
11.
Brazilian operational shift: The prohibition of employer rebates in Brazil has structurally altered how digital challengers and incumbents compete. A Brazilian employee benefits expert explained on 22 December 2025 that ending rebates removed the primary economic barrier for large accounts. Once cash-back incentives are removed, corporate clients select providers based on product quality and digital experience rather than rebates. This dynamic suggests that a French rebate ban could accelerate Swile's competitive market expansion.
12.
Contrary evidence: Prohibiting employer rebates may fail to protect margins because it eliminates the crucial cross-subsidy that supported historical commission levels. Jefferies Research notes on 11 June 2026 that the combination of rebate bans and fee transparency is highly likely to drive gradual but structural compression in take rates. Without high merchant commissions, the total revenue pool shrinks, making it difficult for issuers to maintain historical profitability. This implies that the rebate ban may dilute rather than protect margins.
13.
Italian public contracts: The Italian regulatory model highlights the dangers of capping merchant fees without a corresponding private-sector rebate ban. Morgan Stanley's report on 5 September 2024 notes that CONSIP public tenders cap merchant fees at 5% while employer discounts remain widespread. This dual pressure forced Italian issuers to operate with highly squeezed margins, which ultimately triggered an antitrust investigation into offsetting fees. Consequently, France's proactive rebate ban makes its regulatory transition far more commercially manageable.
14.
Other side of the story: While banning employer rebates is intended to protect traditional voucher economics, it could actually benefit agile digital challengers. An expert from a leading benefits provider noted on 29 January 2026 that when rebates are legally banned, corporate employers stop choosing providers based on cashback economics. Instead, purchasing managers prioritize user experience, mobile app quality, and flexible wallets, where digital-first platforms typically hold a significant advantage. Therefore, the French rebate ban may inadvertently accelerate market share losses for both Edenred and Pluxee.

Swile Expansion Competitive threat: The French employee benefits landscape has consolidated into a tight duopoly between traditional leaders and aggressive digital-first challengers. Balanced market: The French voucher system is fundamentally more stable and balanced than peer European markets. 11.

Client renegotiation: Incumbents in Brazil have responded aggressively to the regulatory caps by restructuring their client contracts.
