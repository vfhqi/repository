Executive Summary

Section summary
  1. The evidence demonstrates that Hilton Food Group's earnings per share trajectory from 2026 to 2029 will be shaped by a complex interplay of structurally elevated tax rates, peak leverage during international expansion, seafood division headwinds, and currency translation volatility, with the key question answerable through detailed analysis of below-the-line financial inputs rather than operating margin dynamics. The investigation into Hilton Food Group plc's earnings drivers reveals a company operating under a distinctive cost-plus secondary packing model where thin operating margins and pass-through pricing mechanisms mean that below-the-line financial inputs–tax rates, interest costs, capital allocation timing, and foreign exchange translation–transmit directly and disproportionately to net income. Because operating margins are historically thin and stable, minor fluctuations in below-the-line items such as tax rates, interest costs, and currency translation translate directly into disproportionate impacts on net income, as evidenced by a sudden rise in the effective tax rate to 29.8% in FY2025 that instantly degraded adjusted basic EPS by 7.4% despite stable underlying operations. The company's adjusted effective tax rate represents a persistent structural headwind that distinguishes Hilton from UK-focused peers. Hilton Food Group reported an elevated adjusted effective tax rate of 29.8% in its FY2025 annual report, a substantial increase compared to the 24.9% rate reported in FY2024, attributed by management to a one-off tax true-up of historic capital allowances that directly reduced the company's adjusted basic earnings per share by 7.4% during that reporting period. The interim results for H1 2026 reported a slightly lower adjusted effective tax rate of 27.7% for the first half of the year, with management guiding that the full year 2026 rate will settle around 28.0% based on current market trends. This structurally higher rate creates a persistent earnings disadvantage relative to domestic competitors like Greencore, which guides to 23.0% to 25.0%.

Net finance costs emerged as a critical pressure point during the 2026 capital expenditure peak. Hilton Food Group's interim results for H1 2026 detailed a net finance cost of £16.8m for the 26-week period, comprising

£8.2m of bank interest, £3.8m of lease interest, and £4.2m of customer-provided supply-chain finance. Management guided the average borrowing rate at roughly 5.5% for 2026, while net bank debt rose sharply to £194.4m in H1 2026, up from £126.7m at year-end FY2025, driven by heavy capital expenditure on the Canadian and Saudi Arabian expansion projects, with leverage reaching 1.4 times adjusted EBITDA as of June 2026, compared to 0.9 times in December 2025. The company's sensitivity to interest rate movements–a 50 basis-point rate rise would increase net finance costs by £2.1m –creates significant earnings volatility during periods of elevated central bank policy rates.

Capital expenditure intensity will remain structurally elevated through 2028 as Hilton simultaneously executes multiple international expansion projects. Management stated in the H1 2026 interim results that capital expenditure is expected to reach £100m in 2026, with £26.3m spent on the Canadian processing plant in H1 2026 and bacon line investments planned for 2027. The capacity expansion in Poland will drive prepared food growth, with scoping scheduled to complete by late 2026, and demand suggesting a larger project than the guided £30m, with capital spending on this facility set to begin in 2027.

This aggressive investment program will temporarily suppress free cash flow generation and constrain near-term dividend cover rebuilding efforts.

The strategic disposal program successfully removed earnings volatility from non-core assets. Hilton's announcement of 9 October 2026 confirmed completion of the sale of its non-core Dutch vegetarian and vegan business, Dalco Food, which was sold to Livekindly Production for £5.4m, consistent with focusing investment on core red meat. Deutsche Bank reported that Dalco generated an adjusted operating loss of £2.0m during the first half of 2026.

The group also completed the disposal of foodservice supplier Fairfax Meadow in September 2025, with the sale to Sysco GB generating cash proceeds of £54.4m and a gain on disposal of £31.0m. These exits simplify the portfolio and allow management to concentrate capital on high-return retail packer partnerships.

International expansion through the Walmart Canada facility and NADEC joint venture represents the primary long-term growth driver but introduces near-term execution risk. Shore Capital models a maiden contribution of £1.5m of profit on £65m of sales in 2027. Management guides that the facility will deliver a small, low-singledigit profit contribution in 2027, with full commercial benefits realized by 2029. Deutsche Bank estimates that the NADEC joint venture will contribute £1.3m of net income by year three.

The seafood division represents the most significant near-term drag on consolidated profitability, with regulatory and operational headwinds expected to persist well into 2027. Seafood adjusted operating profit collapsed from a £2.8m profit in H1 2025 to a £2.2m adjusted operating loss in H1 2026, largely reflecting margin compression in Foppen from unfavourable raw materials and currency movements. Hilton recognized £28.1m of adjusting/exceptional items in FY2025 following the detection of Listeria monocytogenes in certain Foppen products, which led to severe US shipment suspensions and restricted Greek production, forcing the group to transition large parts of its smoked salmon production to the Netherlands. Management is working closely with the US FDA but expects export restrictions to remain in place for at least the first half of FY2026.

Critical information gaps remain regarding the exact US FDA re-approval timeline for Foppen's Greek facility, the final capital requirement for the Polish expansion beyond the initial £30m estimate, the precise pricing of customer-provided supply-chain finance arrangements, and the magnitude of working capital outflow from the tactical H2 2026 frozen-inventory purchase. These unresolved uncertainties could drive material forecast revisions once clarified through upcoming interim reports or investor presentations.

The analysis reveals that downside scenarios centered on prolonged seafood regulatory restrictions, Canadian commissioning delays, or sustained higher interest rates would disproportionately impact EPS given the company's operational gearing and thin margin structure. Structurally higher tax rates near 29.8% due to capital allowance adjustments will permanently reduce adjusted basic earnings per share and limit cash headroom.

While Shore Capital modeled a prompt £1.5m profit contribution on £65m sales in 2027 from the Canadian facility, execution delays could stall this ramp-up and trigger immediate operating deleverage on the massive capital investment made. Business Model Dynamics and Risk Transmission

Understanding the secondary packaging profit transmission: Hilton Food Group operates on an open-book, costplus or fee-per-kilogram partnership model with major global food retailers. This operational framework insulates the company’s operating margins from direct raw material volatility, as raw material price increases are passthrough mechanisms to the retail partners. Consequently, because operating margins are historically thin and stable, minor fluctuations in below-the-line items such as tax rates, interest costs, and currency translation translate directly into disproportionate impacts on net income.

For example, a sudden rise in the effective tax rate to 29.8% in FY2025, driven by capital allowance true-ups, instantly degraded adjusted basic EPS by 7.4% despite stable underlying operations.

Analyzing capital intensity and geographic expansion risk: The business model requires heavy upfront capital commitment to build out highly automated, dedicated packaging facilities for anchor retail customers. When Hilton Food Group enters new territories, such as its recent multi-protein facility expansion for Walmart in Canada or its joint venture with NADEC in Saudi Arabia, it experiences a localized capital expenditure peak. This capital-intensive growth phase increases net bank debt and elevates finance costs, which creates a multi-year lag before the facilities ramp up production and generate offsetting operating profits. This dynamic makes the near-term EPS trend highly sensitive to the exact timing of commissioning and commercial execution.

Comparative Peer Risk Structures

2.
Comparing business model risk transmission across peers: Hilton Food Group's fee-based secondary packaging structure contrasts sharply with the operational setups of traditional food processors. Peers like Cranswick operate a vertically integrated protein processing model where raw meat represents roughly 70% of cost of goods sold, introducing direct exposure to agricultural cycles. While Cranswick utilizes quarterly and annual cost-ofproduction pricing models to manage pig price volatility, it retains far greater residual inventory and processing risk than Hilton. Meanwhile, Greencore operates as a contract maker of fresh prepared foods where only 60% of sales are on raw material pass-through, leaving them exposed to direct labor and energy cost inflation. These structural differences mean Hilton’s operational volatility is lower, but its sensitivity to capital allocation, leverage, and financing efficiency is significantly higher

This analysis synthesizes evidence from a total of 15 unique sources, which spans company financial filings, interim results transcripts, and dedicated equity broker research from institutions including Deutsche Bank and Shore Capital

Other Side of the Story

4.

During periods of falling raw material prices, traditional integrated processors like Cranswick can expand gross margins rapidly, whereas Hilton's fee-per-kilogram structure maintains a flat operating margin profile. Furthermore, if retail partners experience volume declines due to consumer trading down, Hilton's high fixed-cost automation facilities can suffer from operating deleverage that cost-plus clauses cannot fully offset
