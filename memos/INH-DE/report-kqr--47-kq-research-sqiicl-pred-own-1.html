Sourcing and Lag Mechanisms

1.
Diversified procurement structures: Specialized toolmakers manage tungsten volatility by utilizing varied sourcing methods and structured contracts. Sandvik's powder business sources ten to fifteen percent of its material from its own mine, fifty-five percent from recycled scrap, and the remaining portion from external Western virgin tungsten. This varied sourcing profile helps insulate the company from supply shocks, but it exposes the cost base to varying contract terms. Consequently, diversified sourcing strategies stabilize long-term margins but cannot entirely prevent short-term inventory valuation adjustments when market prices shift.
2.
Powder price adjustments: Powder division pricing responds rapidly to international raw material benchmarks because contracts are directly linked to market indexes. Morgan Stanley's report of 19 October 2026 notes that Sandvik applies a one-month price lag and a three-month cost lag to its external powder sales. This tight pricing formula allows the powder unit to pass on price drops almost immediately, which protects volumes but compresses revenues when paratungstate falls. This dynamic means that powder earnings normalise very quickly during raw material downturns.
3.
Cutting tool cost lags: Finished cutting tools exhibit a much longer cost lag than raw powder due to extended manufacturing and distribution cycles. Sandvik's Q3 2026 presentation shows that the average inventory cycle for tools is approximately six months on top of three months in the powder business. This results in a nine-month cutting tool cost lag before raw material price changes flow fully into cost of goods sold. This delayed flow-through means that earnings per share remain highly vulnerable to margin compression if sales prices drop before high-cost inventory is fully consumed.
4.
Contrary evidence: Some premium toolmakers are expected to retain their elevated sales prices even if raw material costs collapse rapidly. Morgan Stanley's report of 19 October 2026 argues that Sandvik is highly unlikely to simply give pricing back to metal cutting customers unless paratungstate prices deteriorate suddenly and severely. For a typical machine shop, a five percent tool price cut equates to only a tiny margin benefit, which means that customers rarely demand price reductions for premium finished tools. This pricing stickiness could protect toolmakers' earnings per share during a market downturn.
