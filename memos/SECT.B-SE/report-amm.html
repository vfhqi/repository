APM Mini-Memo: Sectra

The one-line view. Sectra is the share leader in hospital imaging software, converting a signed backlog worth 3 billion kronor a year into revenue and margin, and the case depends on how fast hospitals let it deploy.

What the company does

Sectra is a Swedish software group selling imaging systems to hospitals, with net sales of 3,541.7 million kronor in the year to April 2026, up 9.3 percent, and operating profit of 710.6 million kronor. Imaging IT Solutions makes 3,057.4 million kronor of those sales: the system radiologists, cardiologists and pathologists use to view and report on scans, now sold as the Sectra One Cloud subscription. Secure Communications, 453 million kronor and up 11 percent, sells encrypted communications and cyber monitoring to governments and defence. A small Business Innovation unit incubates genomics, medical education and orthopaedics software.

Leader. Sectra holds 15 percent of the global market for imaging systems, ahead of Philips at 11 percent and Pro Medicus at 6 percent, ranks top in clinical satisfaction surveys, and loses only 0.5 percent of customers a year. The founders still run the company: Torbjörn Kronander has been chief executive since 2012, chairman Jan-Olof Brüer ran it before him, and each controls 17 percent of the votes.

A plausible investment thesis

Proven transition. Sectra has moved from upfront licences to cloud subscriptions, and the accounts now show it: recurring revenue is 69.2 percent of sales, and cloud recurring revenue grew 54.9 percent to 915.8 million kronor in the year to April 2026, then 75.3 percent in the latest figures on file, dated 4 September. Behind that sits signed contracts worth an estimated 3 billion kronor a year of recurring revenue, led by the 3.1 billion kronor Québec contract covering 150 hospitals over twelve years, Emory Healthcare in the United States and a 15.6 million pound London hospital consolidation. Between 30 and 40 percent of guaranteed bookings become revenue within twelve months.

Margin follows. Each deployment costs money up front for cloud hosting before the subscription scales, and Sectra runs three hosting platforms in parallel while it migrates customers. As contracts go live and old platforms retire, DNB Carnegie sees Imaging IT margins rising by more than ten percentage points from the group's 20.1 percent, at medium plausibility. Mid-teens revenue growth and a rising margin together give the consensus earnings growth of about 30 percent a year, which is the engine of the case.

The drivers to track are three: quarterly growth in cloud recurring revenue, the Imaging IT margin against its 20 percent base, and the recovery of Secure Communications from product delays that management expects to last through this financial year.

The Target Setup

There is no Investment Analyst memo, so there are no Bottom-up/Stock-driven ratings and the setup read is my own. The Target Setup is 'Demand-driven EPSU', with 'Big Corporate Change-driven EPSU' alongside it: the demand is signed and contracted, and the change is the move to subscriptions that is now releasing margin. There is no cause for concern to clear; the shares sit 5 percent below their 52-week high.

Case Riskiness and simplicity

Simple but single-threaded. The case rests on one variable, deployment speed, which hospital IT departments control rather than Sectra. Migrations take twelve to eighteen months and can stretch to twenty-four, and European hospitals hesitate over public cloud, so slow onboarding is the single point of failure for near-term earnings: hosting costs arrive before the revenue does. What needs to go right is that Québec, Emory and the other large contracts go live on plan through 2027, with cloud recurring revenue growing 50 percent or more and the margin holding at 20 percent or better.

Three smaller things sit beneath. Management reinvests everything above a 15 percent margin into growth, so the margin ceiling is a policy choice. Over 70 percent of sales are unhedged, with 8.9 million kronor of operating profit moving for each 1 percent move in the krona. Capitalised development rose to 99.0 million kronor from 74.1 million, with 276.7 million on the balance sheet, which flatters reported profit if replacement orders slow. In plain words, a clear case with one real risk, not a simple one.

Business quality

High. Healthcare imaging is non-cyclical spend with a demographic floor: over-65 populations grow about 3 percent a year in the United States and 2 percent in Europe, and the US radiologist shortage is expected to reach 10 percent by 2034. Once a hospital puts radiology, cardiology and pathology on one platform, switching is painful, which is what the 0.5 percent churn shows. The balance sheet is net cash at 1.5 times earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, and management beat its five-year goal of 50 percent growth in operating profit per share with 103 percent.

The gap. The flaw is economics: Pro Medicus, with a third of Sectra's market share, earns a 74 percent operating margin against Sectra's 20.1 percent, because it charges per study on one product while Sectra sells enterprise licences with integrated third-party applications and parallel hosting. Sectra buys its consolidation wins and low churn with lower revenue per exam, and the margin question is the one the thesis has to answer.

Plausible returns and valuation

The shares cost 327.6 kronor on 6 November, a market value of 4,737 million euros and 50.8 times current earnings, which is the 26th percentile of Sectra's own ten-year history. Forward earnings are not in our files. The weekly routine's plausible return of 29 October is 64.9 percent a year, grade A, but it assumes the multiple rises to 86.8 times, which I would not lean on. On earnings alone the bar is met: consensus earnings growth of about 30 percent a year on a flat multiple, plus a 2.30 kronor dividend, clears 25 percent, while the bear case of 13 percent growth, an 18 percent margin and 40 times returns little. The weekly TSR/Valuation rating of 6 November is B, a valuation reading only; Sell-side Momentum of 5 November is B; Technical Momentum of 6 November is A, with the shares in a healthy pullback and a relative strength composite of 91.

Value chain and thematics: the pros and the cons

For. The Sector Digest's first rule is to sort software by how the vendor charges: Sectra prices on enterprise usage, not on seats, so artificial intelligence that reads scans faster adds billable volume rather than cutting the bill. Hospital software fell 24.6 percent over the twelve months to 12 October while the Sector turned up 18.4 percent in three, Nordic software rose 9 percent in the month to 27 October as disruption fears faded, and Sectra is the one name in its cohort whose transition is proven in the accounts. The EU's NIS 2 cyber rules, with fines of up to 10 million euros or 2 percent of turnover, convert compliance into procurement for Secure Communications, and my own reading is that European sovereignty preferences favour a Swedish vendor with private-cloud options.

Against. Secure Communications depends on government procurement and its delivery delays persist into this financial year. Bookings fell 46.5 percent against the Québec comparison, so the backlog is being drawn down faster than refilled for now. The weekly Thematic Fit/Momentum rating of 6 November is B, an environment reading with no stock layer.

In short

  • Interesting because the growth is already signed: a 3 billion kronor annual recurring backlog converting at 30 to 40 percent within a year, with cloud recurring revenue up 54.9 percent
  • The story is margin: Imaging IT margins have more than ten points to gain as parallel platforms retire, and consensus earnings growth of about 30 percent a year clears the bar on a flat multiple
  • The risk is the clock: hospital IT departments set deployment pace and hosting costs arrive before revenue, so track cloud recurring growth and the margin each quarter
  • Quality is high and the price is fair for Sectra: net cash, 0.5 percent churn and owner-managers who under-promise, at 50.8 times, the 26th percentile of its own history
