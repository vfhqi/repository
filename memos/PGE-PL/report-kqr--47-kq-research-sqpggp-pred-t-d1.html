  2. Polish peers face similar regulatory pressures with tariff cuts offsetting asset base expansion in the near term.

PGE grid growth and tariff resets

Bernstein expects these capital outlays to drive a 7.5% RAB CAGR to FY30 for the core distribution network, reflecting a strong transition towards lower-risk activities. This growth is designed to help PGE capture rising power demand from data centers and industrial zones despite conventional generation volatility. Ultimately, this strategy positions the network as the cornerstone of PGE's medium-term earnings stability.

Regulatory compression: The allowed regulatory return for PGE is set to normalize downwards from peak historical levels as market conditions stabilize. Bernstein assumes that the weighted average cost of capital will decline from 12.96% in FY25 to 8.0% by FY30. Keeping returns at the FY25 level would have lifted EBITDA above PLN 7bn.

The Energy Regulatory Office sets distribution tariffs with a capital return rate linked to 10-year government bond yields. These high yields could eventually push the allowed return back up during future regulatory resets.

Section 3 – Offshore Wind and Major Project Execution

Section summary
  1. PGE's Baltica 2 offshore wind project is advancing steadily under a joint venture model but remains exposed to macroeconomic and supply chain pressures.
  2. Historical conventional generation delays across Polish peers underscore the execution challenges facing large-scale utility transitions.
  3. Ørsted's recent global project completions demonstrate strong technical capabilities, although severe interest rate impairments highlight persistent financial risks.

Baltica project status and financing

1.
Rapid construction of Baltica 2 is progressing on schedule under the joint execution of PGE and Ørsted in the Baltic Sea. The project partners completed the installation of all 111 monopile foundations during the third quarter of 2026. Ørsted reported in its November 2026 interim filing that the 1.5 GW asset reached a 45% degree of completion as planned. This successful foundation campaign de-risks the construction timeline and supports the target of full commercial operations by late 2027, stabilizing long-term EPS.
2.
Heavy capital requirements for Baltica 2 are being met through a diversified financing structure to protect PGE 's balance sheet. PGE secured project finance of approximately PLN 11.1 billion through a consortium of 25 financial institutions in January 2025. PGE 's June 2026 interim report showed that the utility also utilized a PLN 3.9 billion loan from the National Recovery and Resilience Plan. This non-recourse project finance structure shields the parent company's cash flow, although rising debt service costs may still pinch net earnings.
3.
Inflationary cost pressures continue to challenge the long-term economics of Baltic offshore wind projects despite secured public subsidies. PGE and Ørsted estimated the total capital budget for the 1.5 GW Baltica 2 project at PLN 30 billion in late 2025. PGE noted in its June 2026 interim report that the project holds an inflation-indexed contract for difference to mitigate macroeconomic volatility. While this indexation provides essential revenue protection, persistent supply chain inflation could still compress the project's net interest-bearing margins.

Section 4 – Lignite Coal Run-Down and Decommissioning Risks

Section summary
  1. PGE faces significant operational and financial headwinds as its inflexible lignite coal operations are increasingly crowded out by low-carbon energy sources.
  2. The expiration of capacity market revenues after 2028 and fixed mining cost structures threaten to drive substantial conventional segment losses.
  3. Decommissioning liabilities and legal disputes surrounding core mines represent long-term capital risks that contrast with more protected peer restructuring models.

Fixed costs and operational crowd-out

1.
Severe operational pressure: PGE's thermal fleet is experiencing declining utilization as low-carbon sources expand under structural market changes. Bernstein reported on 8 May 2025 that conventional assets are being crowded out by rising renewable and gas penetration. This structural shift is changing the role of coal plants from baseload supply to peak-reserve backup. Consequently, the group faces a sharp reduction in generation volumes.
2.
Inflexible cost structures: Lignite generation is uniquely vulnerable to lower volumes because mining costs are largely fixed. PGE's profitability is heavily burdened by the fixed nature of its mining cost base as highlighted by Fitch on 13 January 2025. Unlike hard coal plants which can reduce fuel purchases when demand drops, lignite operations require continuous mining infrastructure spending. This cost rigidity means any drop in generation volume triggers severe margin compression across the segment.
3.
Heavy contract liabilities: PGE's fuel procurement is further constrained by a major long-term hard coal supply agreement. PGE reported in its 2025 annual filing that it has a take-or-pay contract with Polska Grupa Gornicza running through 2028. The estimated total value of these hard coal supplies for the 2023 to 2028 period reaches PLN 13.5 billion. This contract locks in high fuel volumes even as renewables displace thermal generation.
4.
Expiring capacity revenues: The financial safety net of PGE's coal operations is set to disappear after 2028. Bernstein reported on 8 May 2025 that PGE outsmarted peers by putting its old coal plants on five-year contracts that secure capacity payments until end-2028. These capacity market contracts generated PLN 2.25 billion in FY24, providing critical cash flow to support loss-making units. Once this support ends under the EU derogation, the profitability of the conventional fleet is projected to decline rapidly.

Restructuring precedents and peer comparisons

5.
Aggressive private exit: The private Polish operator ZE PAK offers a comparable case of rapid lignite phase-out. ZE PAK announced a complete coal phase-out within the next few years, transitioning entirely to green energy. The company plans to extract its final lignite reserves and decommission its last coal unit by mid-2026. This rapid transition demonstrates that coal operations can be wound down quickly.
6.
State compensation models: German peer RWE has secured substantial federal support to offset its accelerated lignite phase-out. RWE confirmed an agreement with the German government for EUR 2.6 billion in compensation paid in 15 annual installments. This funding is explicitly designed to back RWE's early closure of lignite operations by 2030. This model highlights how state-backed financial compensation is critical to cushion the decommissioning liabilities.
7.
140 reports Accelerated asset depreciation: CEZ has adopted aggressive accounting adjustments to prepare its balance sheet for the coal exit. CEZ implemented a major change in its accounting estimates in October 2024 to depreciate coal assets more rapidly. Using the sum-of-the-years-digits method, CEZ concentrated 48% of its remaining coal depreciation in 2025 and 2026 alone. This strategy fast-tracks asset write-downs before the planned 2030 thermal generation phase-out.
8.
Rapid southern phase-out: Public Power Corporation has successfully executed an aggressive lignite exit to reduce emission intensity. PPC reported that its lignite generation declined to 0.9 TWh in the first quarter of 2026 compared to 1.1 TWh in the prior year. PPC has historically decommissioned older units like Megalopolis I and II as part of its environmental compliance strategy. This exit has dramatically reduced PPC's carbon exposure.

Strategic targets and decommissioning liabilities

9.
Deep strategic losses: PGE management expects its coal operations to become highly unprofitable by the end of the decade. PGE's Strategy 2035 update on 20 October 2026 projects that Coal EBITDA will decline to minus PLN 3.3 billion in 2030. This planned deficit reflects the loss of capacity payments and rising carbon costs. To manage these losses, management is implementing an Efficiency Improvement Programme.
10.
Massive reclamation provisions: The future retirement of PGE's massive mining sites carries substantial long- term decommissioning liabilities. PGE reported in its June 2026 interim statement that its provisions for mine rehabilitation reached PLN 6.56 billion. These long-term liabilities represent the estimated future costs of reclaiming the Bełchatów and Turów open-pit mines. Because rehabilitation works will extend out to 2085, these cash outflows will remain a multi-decade drag.
11.
Contrary evidence: Financial support mechanisms might be extended by the Polish government to prevent the premature closure of essential baseload coal plants. Bernstein suggested on 8 May 2025 that the government may provide additional regulated revenues or support to maintain grid stability. This potential support arises because decommissioning these major plants rapidly would create a severe domestic power generation gap. If implemented, such policy support would prevent PGE from operating these coal assets on a highly negative EBITDA basis.
12.
Legal operational risks: Ongoing environmental litigation continues to threaten the regulatory approvals of PGE's core mining assets. PGE reported that environmental organizations have filed cassation appeals regarding the immediate enforceability of Turów's environmental mining permit. Although the Voivodeship Administrative Court dismissed the initial complaints in June 2025, the legal battle remains unresolved. Any adverse court ruling could disrupt lignite extraction and force premature closures.
13.
Other side of the story: While conventional generation remains an earnings drag, PGE's complete write-down of these assets limits future balance sheet exposure. Bernstein noted on 8 May 2026 that coal assets are already largely impaired and discounted by the market. Since conventional capital expenditure is being restricted to a minimum of under PLN 1.0 billion, the segment's negative cash flow impact is capped. This low exposure means any successful state carve-out of coal assets would act as a major positive catalyst.

Section 5 – Capex Funding, Leverage, and Dividend Sustainability

Section summary
  1. PGE's PLN 117 billion capex program and dividend yield target of up to 5% are achievable but face headwinds from tax hikes and rising yields.
  2. While PGE maintains low leverage relative to peers, rising macroeconomic risks require tight capital discipline to preserve its investment-grade credit rating.

Capex targets and leverage boundaries

1.
Ambitious expansion: PGE has outlined a comprehensive strategy to invest a massive sum in low-emission generation and modern networks. The company's updated Strategy 2035 from October 2026 targets cumulative capital expenditures of PLN 117 billion by 2030 and PLN 210 billion by 2035. This represents a selective decrease from the previous strategy's PLN 120 billion target for 2030, showing a greater focus on capital discipline. This targeted spending will primarily fund regulated networks and renewable energy infrastructure to stabilize long-term earnings per share.
2.
Managed leverage: PGE plans to maintain its financial leverage well within the safety parameters requested by its creditors during this investment wave. Management targets keeping the corporate Net Debt to EBITDA ratio below 3.5x throughout the transition period. PGE projects that this leverage ratio will average 1.6x from 2026 to 2029 before peaking at 2.8x in 2030. Bernstein notes that this disciplined leverage profile should help PGE maintain its investment-grade credit ratings from rating agencies.
3.
Resumed dividends: PGE intends to restore shareholder distributions starting from its fiscal year 2026 earnings. The utility's Strategy 2035 update on 20 October 2026 targets a dividend yield of up to 5% from FY26 profits. Bernstein highlights that this dividend reinstatement represents a positive surprise for the market because consensus had assumed no payouts. However, actual payments will remain highly conditional on net income generation, leverage levels, and coal segment developments.

Macroeconomic headwinds and tax hikes

4.
Adverse macroeconomics: Rising Polish government bond yields and severe public deficits threaten to raise debt service costs for transitioning utilities. The national fiscal deficit is approaching 7% of GDP, which exerts upward pressure on domestic sovereign debt yields. BGZ BNP Paribas reported that Polish 10-year bond yields remained elevated near 6.15% in late 2026, which widens corporate credit spreads. This high-interestrate environment will raise PGE's refinancing costs, potentially squeezing the net income required to fund both capex and dividends.
5.
Tax headwinds: PGE's net earnings will face additional pressure from a major corporate tax reform passed by Polish lawmakers. The Polish Senate passed a bill on 6 November 2026 raising the Corporate Income Tax from 19% to 22% starting 1 January 2027. This tax hike will directly reduce PGE's net profit, limiting the cash available for equity distributions. Consequently, the group's ability to sustain a 5% dividend yield from FY2026 earnings will depend heavily on achieving its ambitious efficiency gains.

Peer capital allocation and leverage profiles

6.
Flexible funding: Peer utility Enea has taken proactive steps to secure alternative funding structures to manage its own leverage. Enea signed a program agreement on 30 October 2026 for a PLN 5 billion hybrid bond program with major Polish banks. This program enables Enea to issue subordinated debt to secure longterm capital while maintaining its balance sheet health. PGE could look to similar hybrid structures if its capital expenditure outlays exceed operating cash flows.
7.
Rising leverage: TAURON has experienced much greater balance sheet strain than PGE due to its aggressive grid investments and heavy conventional generation liabilities. TAURON's high debt has placed its dividend policy at risk, forcing the utility to suspend payments. TAURON has had to bear substantial grid capex alongside significant generation capex to modernize its aging power fleet. This leverage divergence highlights that PGE's low initial gearing of 0.13x net debt to EBITDA in Q2 2026 provides a superior safety buffer.
8.
Generous payouts: Czech utility CEZ has maintained a much more established state-backed dividend policy supported by robust free cash flows. The Czech Ministry of Finance has historically pushed for CEZ to payout 100% of profits as dividends to support public budgets. CEZ's dividend payout range is typically set at 50% to 60%, yielding an attractive return for shareholders. This high-payout benchmark shows that statecontrolled utilities can maintain high yields, but only when supported by stable, regulated grid cash flows.

Debt adjustments and dividend viability

9.
Contrary evidence: Some analysts argue that PGE cannot successfully cover both its massive capex program and a 5% dividend yield without exceeding leverage targets. Morgan Stanley reported that PGE was expected to generate negative free cash flow over the near term due to substantial investment commitments. These heavy capital requirements mean PGE must rely on new debt, increasing balance sheet risks in a weak power market. This cash drain suggests that any decline in power prices will render the 5% dividend target unsustainable.
10.
Underlying debt: While PGE's reported net debt appears exceptionally low, its economic net debt reveals substantial hidden liabilities. PGE's reported net debt was only PLN 1.8 billion at the end of Q2 2026. However, PGE's economic net debt stood at PLN 14.2 billion when adjusted for forward carbon allowance payments and lease liabilities. This larger economic debt burden means PGE has less balance sheet headroom to absorb unexpected capital cost overruns than headline metrics suggest.
11.
Squeezed returns: PGE's capital program is plausible but leaves very narrow margins for error regarding its dividend target. Funding PLN 117 billion of capex while paying up to a 5% dividend yield is dependent on stable operating cash flows from regulated networks. Any delay in commissioning profitable projects like Baltica 2 will force management to prioritize capital spending over shareholder payouts. Therefore, the 22% corporate tax rate starting in 2027 will directly drag on net earnings and reduce dividend coverage unless efficiency gains offset the hit.
12.
Other side of the story: PGE's strategic transition will significantly improve its credit profile, allowing it to support higher leverage than peer utilities. Bernstein points out that PGE's growing share of regulated grid and contracted renewable revenues will structurally reduce earnings volatility. This improved earnings predictability will support higher debt capacity, keeping leverage comfortably below the 3.5x covenant. Therefore, even with macro headwinds, PGE is well-positioned to fund its transition while resuming attractive payouts.

Section 6 – District Heating and Efficiency Programs

Section summary
  1. PGE's upgraded district heating EBITDA targets are supported by massive investments but face skepticism over low marginal returns.
  2. Regulatory tariff lag under the Energy Regulatory Office represents a persistent risk to PGE's short-term operating margins.
  3. The Efficiency Improvement Programme targets significant conventional cost reductions that compare well with historical peer precedents.

District heating expansion and targets

1.
Ambitious targets: PGE has substantially upgraded its financial ambitions for its district heating operations in its latest long-term plans. The group raised its fiscal year 2030 heating segment EBITDA target to PLN 3.4bn from PLN 2.7bn outlined in its previous strategy. This aggressive upgrade reflects a strategic pivot toward heat network integration, Power-to-Heat installations, and accelerated decarbonization across municipal systems. This strategic focus is expected to structurally improve long-term cash flow predictability and support consolidated earnings.

206 209 gy p PGE Group's strategic EBITDA targets for the district heating segment through 2035

2.
Heavy capital: PGE plans to deploy massive capital outlays to transition its local heating networks away from traditional fossil fuels. The company intends to spend approximately PLN 18bn on heating between 2025 and 2035, with PLN 15bn reserved for new generation facilities and PLN 3bn for network acquisitions. This extensive funding is designed to achieve a 60% reduction in carbon emissions compared to 2021 levels. These massive capital requirements will likely constrain near-term free cash flow until new low-emission assets are fully commissioned.
3.
Skeptical market: Broker analysts remain highly cautious about the long-term profitability and return profile of PGE's district heating expansion. Bernstein projects a more conservative FY30 EBITDA of PLN 2.5bn, which sits well below management's target. Furthermore, Bernstein notes that PGE's planned investment of almost PLN 9bn in the 2031 to 2035 period is expected to generate an EBITDA increase of only PLN 0.1bn. This extremely low marginal return suggests that a large portion of this capital expenditure is defensive and mandatory.
4.
Peer heating: Polish peer utilities are also executing capital-intensive heating transitions to comply with tightening environmental regulations. TAURON reported an 11% increase in heat sales revenue to PLN 1.44bn in 2025, driven by higher distribution rates and winter volumes. However, TAURON faces similar market risks from declining heat demand due to building thermal renovations. Consequently, both PGE and its domestic peers face high capital burdens that will continue to pressure return on capital.
5.
Market share: PGE occupies a dominant but highly localized position in the fragmented Polish district heating market. The group is the undisputed domestic leader with a market share of approximately 20%, whereas the top four producers combined control less than 40% of nationwide production. Main competitors like Orlen Termika and Veolia focus their operations on other major urban areas, limiting direct competition. This localized natural monopoly structure provides stable demand but remains highly exposed to local municipal planning decisions.

Regulatory tariff structures and lags

6.
Regulatory price: The profitability of Polish heating businesses is tightly constrained by administrative price controls overseen by the national regulator. District heating prices in Poland are determined through administrative tariff procedures approved by URE. These approved tariffs are intended to cover justified operating, fuel, and depreciation costs while allowing for a reasonable return on capital. However, many operators continue to struggle with cost recovery because of the rigid regulatory formulas.
7.
Tariff lag: PGE's district heating segment is highly vulnerable to financial distress caused by structural delays in the regulatory tariff adjustment process. Regional utilities face significant risks of delays in tariff approvals, forcing them to apply outdated, low-return tariffs. This tariff lag typically spans twelve to twenty-four months before regulator approvals catch up with actual market conditions. This structural lag means that sudden fuel price spikes can severely compress PGE's short-term operating margins.
8.
Contrary evidence: The Polish government has established legislative mechanisms that protect essential heating providers from sustaining catastrophic losses during extreme market shocks. Under Poland's Energy Law, the regulator can force a licensed utility to continue loss-making heating activities in the public interest for up to two years. However, the statute explicitly entitles the forced operator to full loss coverage from the State Treasury. This statutory safety net prevents PGE from experiencing structural cash insolvency even under highly adverse regulatory tariff delays.
9.
Environmental compliance: Tightening European Union climate policies are accelerating the obsolescence of PGE's legacy coal-fired heating installations. The recast Energy Efficiency Directive has introduced highly restrictive criteria for efficient systems. PGE must completely decommission or modernize coal-fired units across 85% of its heating assets by 2030 to maintain its regulatory standing. Failure to meet these efficiency benchmarks would jeopardize PGE's ability to recover future transition capital through regulated tariffs.

Operational self-help and efficiency programs

10.
Efficiency program: PGE is executing a comprehensive corporate self-help initiative to mitigate the severe cash drain from its legacy thermal generation division. The updated Strategy 2035 introduces the Efficiency Improvement Programme, which targets a 35% reduction in PGE GiEK's forecast 2030 negative EBITDA to minus PLN 3.3bn. This program aims to generate an additional PLN 4bn in cumulative group EBITDA by 2030 through rigorous cost-control measures. This operational improvement is vital for maintaining the group's investment-grade credit profile.
11.
Main cost: The corporate self-help program focuses on reducing unprofitable conventional operations and aligning support costs with declining asset use. Key operational initiatives include reducing power plants' minimum output levels, planning extended outages, and adjusting conventional mining capacity to match lower lignite demand. PGE also plans to align its refurbishment and maintenance outlays with the anticipated shorter lifespans of its conventional assets. These targeted cost reductions are expected to partially shield the parent's cash flows.
12.
Historical cost: PGE has a mixed track record of delivering on ambitious cost-reduction programs during prior restructuring cycles. The group launched an efficiency program in 2016 targeting a PLN 500m reduction in controllable costs by 2020. However, Deutsche Bank reported in 2016 that conventional generation margins remained under severe pressure from rising carbon costs and volume declines. This historical precedent indicates that internal cost-cutting measures are often fully offset by external regulatory and market headwinds.
13.
Peer efficiency: Comparable utility operators across Central Europe have deployed similar self-help initiatives to defend margins against falling commodity prices. Enea implemented a restructuring plan in 2014 to cut PLN 0.5bn of controllable costs by 2016 through third-party services and group streamlining. CEZ also executed cost reductions of 16% to 27% in 2014 to offset lower power prices and preserve dividend capacity. These peer precedents demonstrate that utility operators can successfully capture substantial operational savings when conventional generation cash flows deteriorate.
14.
Other side of the story: Investors may underestimate the positive cash flow impact of PGE's upgraded heating and efficiency strategies. Bernstein argues that PGE's shift toward regulated networks and contracted heat will structurally improve cash flow quality and reduce merchant market exposure. While the Efficiency Improvement Programme does not replace the need for long-term state capacity payments, it significantly narrows the conventional funding gap. These combined initiatives will therefore protect PGE's earnings per share far better than the market's current conventional valuation implies.

Critical regulatory and policy gaps

1.
Unstable regulatory returns: PGE's future grid profitability remains highly uncertain because the final tariff parameters for the next regulatory period are still pending approval from the national regulator. Although the regulatory asset base is projected to grow, the precise allowed return remains dependent on future weighted average cost of capital resets. To resolve this critical evidence gap, analysts must monitor the Polish Energy Regulatory Office's official tariff publications and announcements expected in late 2026. Evaluating these regulatory filings will clarify whether future distribution margins can successfully offset rising corporate tax rates and preserve PGE's net income.
2.
Unresolved conventional funding: PGE's strategy assumes that its system-critical coal fleet will receive state- funded capacity support after existing agreements expire in 2028. However, the European Union's strict emission rules currently prevent coal units from participating in traditional capacity auctions without special derogations. To close this gap, investors must await the outcomes of ongoing bilateral negotiations and official legislative sessions in Warsaw regarding post-2028 funding. Only a legally binding state mechanism will guarantee that PGE does not sustain massive operating deficits across its legacy thermal generation division.
3.
Adverse tax burdens: PGE's ability to fund its PLN 117 billion capital program while reinstating dividends is constrained by rising domestic interest rates. The Polish Senate recently passed a tax bill increasing the corporate income tax rate from 19% to 22% starting in 2027. To close the evidence gap on funding sustainability, investors must monitor PGE's upcoming separate financial statements and bond issuance programs. These reports will reveal if cheap transition loans can sufficiently offset the dual impact of tax hikes and high yields.

Analysts can close this gap by conducting targeted expert calls with former Polish utility operations directors to benchmark similar cost-reduction campaigns.

Macroeconomic and customer impacts

9.
Squeezed competitiveness: Elevated electricity prices in Poland are already threatening the operational survival and competitiveness of the country's manufacturing base. Industrial power costs in late 2024 were among the highest in the European Union, matching German levels and dampening economic growth. If PGE fails to deploy cheaper low-carbon generation rapidly, domestic business tariffs will remain uncompetitively high. This persistent cost pressure could severely damage long-term industrial competitiveness unless regional interconnectors are quickly expanded.
10.
Fiscal strain: Regulated household tariffs represent a complex balancing act between consumer affordability and the massive funding needed for utility transition investments. Polish authorities historically froze retail power prices to shield low-income households from global commodity shocks. A delayed renewable rollout would force the government to prolong expensive support schemes. These prolonged price freezes would burden the national state budget through multi-billion payment funds.

Supplier and competitor exposure

11.
Supplier gridlock: PGE holds massive take-or-pay fuel commitments with domestic coal miners that restrict its fuel procurement flexibility. The long-term contract with supplier Polska Grupa Gornicza requires purchasing fuel valued at billions through 2028. If conventional generation is run down slower than planned, coal demand will remain artificially elevated. This operational delay would lock in uncompetitive domestic fuel costs compared to alternative renewable options.
12.
Sector contagion: Domestic competitors such as Enea and TAURON face parallel leverage and regulatory constraints under the current transition framework. TAURON has already seen its dividend policy placed at risk due to high network capital requirements. If PGE's strategic restructuring stalls, neighboring utilities will face increased pressure to maintain system stability. This dynamic would depress sector-wide equity valuations as conventional liabilities persist on balance sheets.

Key stakeholder powers

13.
Political control: The Polish State Treasury maintains absolute strategic control over PGE through its dominant majority stake. The state owns over 60% of shares and holds special veto rights regarding material capital expenditure plans. This dominant ownership structure creates high political intervention risk for minority shareholders. Consequently, the energy ministry can easily force PGE to prioritize political stability over commercial profitability.
14.
Labor barriers: Powerful labor unions in Poland possess substantial leverage to delay the closure of conventional assets. Nearly 100% of PGE employees are currently represented by trade unions or active works councils. These organizations actively negotiate transition agreements to preserve conventional power jobs. Their political influence could prevent rapid lignite mine closures despite persistently negative earnings.
15.
Regulatory limits: The European Commission continues to enforce strict decarbonization and state-aid rules that limit national transition subsidies. EU rules scheduled to phase out the Just Transition Fund after 2027 will increase regional funding pressure. If the EU refuses to grant post-2028 capacity market derogations, PGE's coal fleet will face immediate financial distress. These strict policies will restrict Poland's fiscal flexibility during the transition.

Early signs of deviation

16.
Capital bottlenecks: Delays in obtaining grid connection approvals represent an early warning sign of a slowing power sector transition. Speculative zombie energy projects currently clog the national grid pipeline and distort capacity planning. These grid bottlenecks have already forced operators to delay offshore wind timelines elsewhere. This operational gridlock could defer PGE's expected renewables cash flows beyond 2030.
17.
Contrary evidence: PGE is successfully progressing with its near-term construction milestones to de-risk its strategic transition. The group completed the installation of all foundations for its flagship offshore asset during 2026. 306 These concrete construction achievements show that PGE's near-term green transition is progressing on schedule.

Capital and gas exposure

18.
Gas transition risks: Expanding gas-fired capacity as a bridge technology exposes PGE to high import dependencies and fuel price volatility. Poland currently imports approximately 80% of its natural gas consumption, primarily from Norway and the United States. If international gas prices spike, PGE's gas-fired assets will face significant margin compression. This exposure means that over-reliance on gas could create new earnings volatility risks for the utility.
19.
Interest rate headwinds: Rising domestic yields present a major hurdle for funding the massive capital expenditure program. High fiscal deficits have kept 10-year Polish bond yields elevated near 6.15% in late 2026. If financing costs remain high, PGE's debt service burden will swell, making dividend distributions unsustainable. These high rates could squeeze net profit margins significantly unless cheaper preferential funding is secured.
20.
Other side of the story: Investors may overstate transition delay risks and ignore the stabilizing effect of government safety nets. Polish energy laws protect critical utility operators from sustaining catastrophic transition losses. If market forces create severe grid deficits, the state will step in with temporary financial support. This regulatory backing will insulate PGE's cash flows from the worst-case transition scenarios.
