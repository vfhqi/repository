Implications — Ryanair Holdings (RYA-IE) — H1 FY27 results, 2 November 2026

These results weaken the existing case, and materially: the quarter itself landed in line, but the FY28 fuel exposure the case named as its break point has arrived larger and sooner than the case allowed, and the two sell-side readings the case leaned on, best-in-set earnings momentum and a still-mis-modelled upgrader, have both gone.

What changed

The quarter was the quarter the case's own test said would hurt it. Second-quarter profit after tax was EUR 1,610m, matching consensus exactly and down 6.3% on the EUR 1,719m of a year earlier. Revenue of EUR 5,698m (+4%) missed consensus of EUR 5,718m by 0.4%. The miss came from price: the average fare fell 2.1% to EUR 63.40 against a consensus fall of 1.7%, and ancillary yield of EUR 24.90 per passenger rose 0.8% against 1.1% expected. Half-year profit after tax fell 15% to EUR 2.15bn on revenue up 3% to EUR 10.08bn, traffic up 6% to 125.8m passengers and a half-year average fare down 4% to EUR 55.70. Load factor held at 96%.

Two things beat. Ex-fuel unit cost fell 0.4% to EUR 32.90 per passenger against consensus growth of 1.7%. And the board declared an EUR 800m buyback from February 2027 against the EUR 500m the sell-side expected, on top of the EUR 750m programme completed in September 2026, with an interim dividend of EUR 0.273 per share. Net cash stood at EUR 2bn at September 2026, the final EUR 1.2bn bond having been repaid in May 2026.

The number that matters is forward. The unhedged 20% of FY27 fuel doubled to about $130 per barrel in the half. For FY28 the company is 25% hedged at $954 per metric tonne, against 80% at $67 per barrel this year, and management puts the FY28 fuel bill increase at about EUR 2bn. The FY27 passenger target was cut from 216m to 214m in September to drop unprofitable winter flying; FY28 is now "over 220m", against the 223m the case carried. No FY27 profit guidance was given. Against that, management said third-quarter pricing has turned positive after two quarters of decline, forward bookings run modestly ahead of last year, and about 50% of December- and March-quarter seats were sold by early November. Management's claim is that profit can be held next year if industry fares rise 10% to 15%, roughly EUR 10 per passenger. The 737 MAX-10 won FAA certification in October 2026, with EASA expected by end-December and 16 deliveries between January and April 2027. The company has told unions in Ireland, the UK, Spain and Portugal to expect pay freezes from April 2027 without new agreements; the Irish pilots' union has rejected the idea and Unite says its contract stands.

The sell-side response was split by horizon. Morgan Stanley raised FY27 profit after tax 6% to EUR 1.80bn on winter pricing, cut FY28 by 21% to EUR 1.70bn, and still lifted its target to EUR 28.30 from EUR 25.70. Bernstein held Outperform at EUR 30.00 and models adjusted EPS falling from EUR 1.62 in FY27 to EUR 1.56 in FY28. Deutsche Bank held Buy at EUR 28.00, said FY28 consensus faces downward pressure, and said fares are unlikely to offset the full fuel increase. Rothschild Redburn held Buy at EUR 29.00 and said its FY28 estimate sits 7% below a consensus that models only EUR 1bn of fuel increase. Morgan Stanley's bear case has operating margin at 9% by FY29.

On the dashboard, the price is EUR 23.9 (3 November), 3.2% below its 200-day average, relative-strength percentile 25; the P/E is 11.4x at the 70th percentile of its own ten-year range; six-month EPS revisions are -37%, six-month target revisions -11%, and the buy share is 65%. Technical momentum and sell-side earnings momentum are both pinned F, valuation D.

The challenge

The case was written in August with the FY28 unhedged position as "the identified break point" and the near-term exposure "genuinely contained" by FY27 hedges. Both halves were right, and the second expires in March 2027. Three readings of these results sit outside what the case holds.

Reading one: the company cannot price up, and the winter inflection is bought, not earned. In its strongest form: Ryanair could not hold price in peak summer 2026 with 80% of fuel hedged at $67 per barrel, missing even a consensus that already assumed decline, while holding load at 96%. That is the case's own Failure #9 pattern (load defended with price) printed again, and the review has the softness still more pronounced at Ryanair than at legacy carriers. The third-quarter turn arrived only after 2m of its own winter seats were cut and after Wizz Air (2.4m winter seats), Lufthansa (2m) and easyJet (700,000) cut theirs; it is a supply event. The macro leg has Eurozone inflation heading to 4.0% in the fourth quarter of 2026 and UK air-travel spend flat year-on-year in September. Wizz Air is still adding 21% capacity in the fourth quarter with about 35% of its network on Ryanair routes, up to 78% more seats in Spain and 64% in Italy on Morgan Stanley's count. For this reading to be right, FY28 fare rises would need to stall well short of the 10% to 15% management requires, and Ryanair would need to discount to hold load, which is what it did this summer. Two of the four brokers already say the full offset is unlikely. If right, this is the case's break point reached without a hedge, and the case's own top-ranked pre-mortem risk, unhedged fuel plus pricing competition, is the thing that happens.

Reading two: this is a clearing event in a quality compounder, and the case's taxonomy has no row for it. The case rejected the clearing and trough setups (E8 RA5, RA6) because estimates had not been cut deeply, the multiple was not bottom-quartile and nobody had capitulated, and it said only a demand shock would open them. What has arrived is a cost shock, and it is doing part of the work: six-month EPS revisions on the dashboard are -37%, Morgan Stanley has cut FY28 by 21%, Redburn and Deutsche Bank say consensus has further to fall. The same shock is removing competitors: Air Baltic in Chapter 11, easyJet going private on a lower growth path, Lufthansa bidding for TAP, Wizz Air with 27 aircraft grounded in June and cutting winter seats. Ryanair goes into it debt-free with EUR 2bn net cash and the first certified high-capacity narrowbodies arriving in spring 2027. For this reading to be right, the fuel increase would need to be a one-year event (Deutsche Bank's own numbers already show FY29 EPS of EUR 2.26 after EUR 1.74 in FY28), competitor capacity would need to stay out, and the multiple would need to come down to meet the estimates. It has not: the P/E has risen from the case's 10.0x to 11.4x on the dashboard, and the ten-year percentile from the case's 42 to 70, because consensus EPS fell faster than the price. This reading says the stock is halfway through a clearing event while the market still prices the compounder. The case cannot hold that, because the setup it chose as primary fit, the mis-modelled upgrader, needs the sell-side to be behind the quality, and the sell-side is now ahead of the fuel.

Reading three, the weakest but not dismissable: the sandbagger has started to talk up. The one clean [A] in the case's simplicity work rests on conservative investor relations. Management now says profit can be maintained next year through a EUR 2bn fuel increase and that fares could rise 10% to 20%, while declining to guide FY27 and telling unions the business must freeze pay. The counter is that fare-rise warnings serve the labour negotiation and condition the consumer, and that the withheld guidance is itself the conservative act; that counter is probably right. But if the maintain-profit claim becomes the reference point and FY28 lands where the brokers have it, EUR 1.56 to EUR 1.74 of EPS against FY26's EUR 2.12, the "sandbagger archetype" has produced its first miss against its own words.

Readings one and two are not alternatives to each other so much as the same facts with different endings, and the evidence does not yet choose between them. What the results do rule out is the reading the case was built on: a steady-state quality compounder whose numbers the sell-side keeps underestimating. This quarter matched, next year is being cut, and the multiple has gone up.

What would settle it

  • The second review, four working days on. It should carry the post-print broker revisions: whether consensus FY28 moves from EUR 1bn of modelled fuel increase toward the company's EUR 2bn, where target prices settle after Morgan Stanley's rise and the others' holds, and whether the buy share holds at 65%. It cannot carry a realised FY28 fare, new hedging, EASA certification, or a union agreement. It can settle whether MR2 and E8 RA1 move; it cannot settle readings one or two.
  • The third-quarter report carries realised winter fares against management's "turned positive" and Morgan Stanley's +5% third-quarter and +10% fourth-quarter fare assumptions. A winter fare rise that holds with load near 95% weakens reading one; a rise bought with falling load strengthens it.
  • EASA certification by end-December 2026 and 16 MAX-10 deliveries between January and April 2027, observable in the fourth-quarter report. A slip strengthens the cost-shock-without-offset leg of reading one.
  • FY28 hedging additions. The 25% at $954 per tonne is described as preliminary. The company has not committed to adding cover; if it does, the break point moves. If it does not, the case is unhedged into the summer that decides it.
  • Union agreements or strikes before April 2027. Not committed; two unions have already said no.
  • Summer 2027 realised fares, the thing that actually answers readings one and two, arrive with the first-quarter FY28 report, roughly nine months out. Nothing before then carries them. That is the open question with no near answer, and any re-rating happens knowing it.
