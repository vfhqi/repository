Attractive hubs: The strategic value of low-cost slot portfolios is concentrated in primary European vacation and business gateways. EasyJet Structural Workarounds

1.
Stringent requirements: EU airline licensing rules demand strict national control to preserve regional operating rights. Under Regulation (EC) No. 1008/2008, any carrier operating within the bloc must remain majority owned and effectively controlled by EU nationals. For easyJet, its Austrian operating license and vital intra- European routes depend entirely on maintaining this strict regulatory compliance. Consequently, non- European acquisitions must deploy complex legal frameworks to protect operating certificates and avoid severe disruptions to future earnings.
2.
Capped ownership: Apollo Global Management structured its take-private offer for easyJet to keep ordinary equity stakes below regulatory limits. The firm capped its direct ownership of the purchasing vehicle at 49.9%, leaving the remaining voting rights with European stakeholders. To achieve this, rollover shareholders including the Haji-Ioannou family will hold between 45.1% and 49.9% of the equity, while an EU management trust holds up to 5%. This structure allows the private equity firm to acquire substantial economic value without triggering a breach of European nationality rules.
3.
Complex governance: Morgan Stanley analysts examined the governance safeguards established in the easyJet scheme document on October 15, 2026. Under the terms, a majority of the holding company's board directors must be EU nationals, and Apollo-appointed directors cannot constitute a board majority. Additional safeguards include continuous nationality monitoring, share-transfer restrictions, and mandatory buybacks to preserve compliance. However, Apollo retains significant consent rights over equity issuance and unbudgeted capital commitments, which could lead regulators to question whether effective control remains with qualifying European shareholders, creating execution risks that could depress future EPS.

Independent Carrier Voting Protections

4.
Protective voting: European low-cost carriers currently utilize voting restrictions to maintain compliance with ownership rules while listed on public exchanges. For instance, easyJet reported a mere 36% of EU ownership during its February 2026 AGM, forcing it to disenfranchise non-EU shareholders. By suspending the voting rights attached to shares held by UK and other non-EU investors, the airline successfully keeps its majority voting power within the bloc. This ongoing practice highlights how independent carriers remain vulnerable to sudden regulatory interventions if proxy voting mechanisms are challenged.

This would force independent budget airlines to rely solely on organic growth, capping their long-term valuation multiples and suppressing EPS growth.

Sudden slot: Strategic airport slots could lose significant value if secondary airports undergo massive infrastructure expansions or deregulation. A former commercial manager at easyJet highlights that the airline's premium model depends heavily on operating from slot-constrained airports like London Gatwick and Lisbon. Contrary evidence: Deutsche Bank Research analysts argue that the European Commission's ongoing ownership and control review may not disrupt transactions in the next two years. They highlight that the EU has been seeking stakeholder input on these rules since December 2025, and Ryanair management expects no immediate changes. This suggests that the regulatory threat to private equity takeovers might be significantly slower to materialize than critics fear.

Aggressive capacity: Unchecked narrowbody capacity growth represents a severe downside risk for low-cost carriers if demand soft-lands. Morgan Stanley analysts project narrowbody supply to grow at a 6.6% CAGR through 2030, led by Wizz Air's rapid expansion. This rapid fleet expansion threatens to trigger intense fare dilution across overlapping markets, placing near-term low-cost carrier earnings under severe pressure.

Airbus disclosures confirm that weak economic conditions can lead to customer requests to postpone or cancel existing orders. Such deferrals would swell manufacturer inventories and severely disrupt scheduled delivery cash flows.

Accelerated asset: Severe airline insolvencies would trigger a sudden oversupply of aircraft in the secondary leasing market. BOC Aviation filings indicate that historical airline reorganizations and bankruptcies force rapid fare discounting to maintain passenger cash flows. This distress behavior depresses aircraft market values and lease rates across competing lessor portfolios, harming industry-wide asset returns.

Deutsche Bank Research notes that sustained high fuel costs force airlines to trim marginal routes and fly fewer hours. This operational retrenchment delays lucrative engine shop visits, directly impacting aftermarket parts consumption and reducing long-term service revenues.
