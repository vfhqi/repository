Executive Summary

Section summary
  1. The 21 October 2026 earnings strengthened HMS Networks' medium-term demand case but weakened the near-term EPS path, because a clean-looking order beat contained SEK70 million of pre-ordering while broader component inflation and tariffs defer gross-margin recovery into 2027. HMS Networks delivered Q3 2026 revenue of SEK1,024 million and adjusted EBITA of SEK280 million against DNB Carnegie's forecasts of SEK1,032 million and SEK284 million, broadly matching the broker's above-consensus expectations on sales and profitability, while orders reached SEK1,104 million versus the SEK1,034 million forecast and 15% organic-growth expectation. Order quality was weaker than the headline, because management estimated SEK70 million of orders were brought forward before lead-time deterioration and January price increases, reducing underlying organic growth to approximately 14% in DNB Carnegie's reconciliation.

Geographic recovery broadened beyond North America, with organic orders up approximately 40% in China and 20% in Europe while North America grew just under 10%, supporting a more balanced industrial recovery than the pre-results market expected. The strongest demand pockets remained semiconductor and data-centre infrastructure, where HMS sells automation products into power, ventilation and related infrastructure rather than computing equipment itself, making the opportunity more durable than a single server component cycle although customer capex remains linked to hyperscale project timing. Industrial Data Solutions remained the weak link, reporting Q3 orders down 1% to SEK404 million and down 3% organically while revenue rose only 2%, falling short of the broader recovery implied by the group's order performance and leaving the largest division needing larger project orders before the medium-term EPS trajectory can be considered secure. Industrial Networks Technology delivered the strongest proof of recovery, with Q3 orders of SEK468 million up 94% reported and 70% organically, but the division contained most of the estimated pre-orders, so the exceptional order growth cannot be annualised without adjustment.

Margin resilience masked underlying gross-profit pressure, with Q3 adjusted EBITA margin reaching

27.4% versus 27.1% a year earlier and close to DNB Carnegie's SEK284 million estimate, but underlying gross margin fell to 62.4% after a SEK3 million United States tariff refund, showing that volume leverage more than offset higher input costs while the margin base weakened. Management said cost pressure had spread beyond memory to printed circuit boards, passive components and a broader portion of the product portfolio, while revised tariff rules also had a negative effect, creating a more concerning inflation profile than a singlecomponent disruption. - Earnings Calls DNB Carnegie reduced 2026 adjusted EPS from SEK15.7 to SEK15.3, a 3% cut, and trimmed 2027 adjusted EPS from SEK19.4 to SEK19.2, a 1% reduction, but remained 3% to 5% above pre-Q3 Bloomberg consensus for 2027 and 2028.

The broker left 2028 adjusted EPS unchanged at SEK21.9, indicating that it treated the gross-margin pressure and pre-order reversal as timing effects rather than a permanent reduction in three-year earnings power, making the 12-to-18-month bridge weaker than the structural terminal case.

EPS risk has shifted into 2027, because management expects gross margin to remain under pressure through Q4 and potentially into 2027 as component prices and tariffs affect a broader product base, while price increases begin on 1 January 2027 but will have full effect only later; DNB Carnegie consequently expects a gross-margin trough near 62.0% in Q1 2027 before pricing takes effect in Q2, making the next two quarters more important for EPS than the Q3 beat. - Earnings Calls Operating cash flow exceeded expectations materially, reaching SEK333 million versus SEK258 million a year earlier with DNB Carnegie estimating free cash flow at SEK291 million and 55% above its forecast, providing the clearest positive surprise beyond orders, although the company described cash flow as partly supported by temporary working-capital improvements including approximately SEK49 million from higher accounts payable.

Net debt excluding International Financial Reporting Standard 16 was SEK1,792 million and net debt to adjusted EBITDA excluding that standard was 1.52x at 30 September, down from 2.77x a year earlier, giving HMS substantially more financial capacity for acquisitions before the end of 2026.

The market reaction was disappointingly negative, with DNB Carnegie saying an in-line quarter was insufficient after HMS's share-price strength into the print and the stock declining 5% as investors questioned whether data-centre orders, pre-ordering and 2027 gross-margin pressure meant the business had reached an "as good as it gets" phase. DNB Carnegie reduced its target price from SEK661 to SEK654 while retaining Buy, and values HMS at 27.5 times 2027 estimated enterprise value to EBIT, approximately 10% below the company's fiveto-ten-year historical average of roughly 30 to 31 times, because earnings momentum is expected to slow and the share-price reaction showed that investors required cleaner growth evidence. 9 HMS's 2030 ambition remained unchanged, with CEO Staffan Dahlström and CFO Joakim Nideborn reaffirming the SEK7.5 billion revenue target and describing Q3's first quarter above SEK1 billion of sales as an early milestone rather than evidence that the plan is de-risked, while the company continues to require roughly 16% annual sales growth from its 2025 base with approximately half expected organically and half from mergers and acquisitions.

Management explicitly rejected increasing the long-term EBITA margin target above 25% even though Q3 delivered 27.4%, saying the headroom is intentional because acquisitions are often margin dilutive and HMS wants to retain capacity for organic investment.

Molex integration is progressing but revenue contribution remains gradual, with HMS completing the acquisition of parts of Molex's industrial communications business on 2 January 2026 and integrating the assets into Industrial Network Technology, while management said the acquired business generated approximately SEK140 million of Q1 orders and more than US$10 million of annual revenue above initial expectations, although salespeople still need time to convert the enlarged customer and product base into substantial incremental revenue.

HMS announced a friendly public offer for Spanish building-automation company Robot at an enterprise value of approximately EUR21 million, with settlement expected around 1 December 2026, and expects the acquisition to be earnings-per-share accretive from completion before non-cash acquisition amortisation, although the SEK5 million transaction and integration cost creates a short-term offset.

The industrial cycle is recovering from a late position, with HMS entering the quarter expecting a gradual automation recovery and management pointing to the global purchasing managers' index reaching its highest level since 2022, while DNB Carnegie still classified HMS as a late-cycle business with roughly 40% of sales dependent on industrial-automation capital expenditure; Q3 orders were stronger than expected but HMS attributed much of the strongest demand to semiconductors and data-centre infrastructure while larger Industrial Data Solutions projects remained absent, showing that factory and process automation spending has not recovered evenly.

Tariff exposure worsened through summer rule changes, with HMS previously expecting tariffs mainly on approximately 15% of group sales consisting of finished goods made in Europe and shipped to North America, but summer changes brought previously exempt categories into scope and contributed to the Q3 gross-margin decline from 64.1% to 62.7%, while revised Canada-US rules were still affecting HMS and its North American flows. The market expected HMS to pass tariff costs to customers as it had done in 2025, but price increases communicated during the quarter will apply only from 1 January 2027 and may not fully offset costs until later in the year, creating a regulatory timing drag on gross profit before any volume response is visible.

Competitive positioning remains credible but faces architectural threats, with HMS's advantage resting on its breadth across incompatible industrial networks, embedded connectivity, gateways and functional safety, while DNB Carnegie describes it as the number-one player in a structurally growing niche, but a Rockwell digitaltransformation leader viewed HMS as strong in protocol conversion and broad connectivity yet less competitive in embedded communications, high-availability networking and cloud-first architectures, implying that larger automation vendors can increasingly internalize connectivity. OEM insourcing remains an underappreciated risk, with DNB Carnegie explicitly warning that the number of original equipment manufacturers willing to outsource industrial communication hardware and software may be overestimated, while Industrial Ethernet represented 79% of newly installed nodes in 2026 and standardization reduces the number of interfaces HMS must support but also shifts value toward higher-level safety, cybersecurity and software where HMS can still differentiate. Protocol standardization is accelerating but not immediately disruptive, with HMS's 2026 market study finding Industrial Ethernet represented 79% of newly installed industrial nodes up from 76% in 2025 and 34% in 2015, confirming that the protocol-fragmentation moat is narrowing over time, while the immediate EPS risk is limited because installed machines still require migration, integration and support but the three-year risk is that fewer new protocols require less conversion hardware. Connectivity is moving into silicon, with NXP's MCX A5 industrial microcontroller integrating Ethernet, Single Pair Ethernet, topology discovery and security directly into edge nodes, allowing original equipment manufacturers to embed communications without a separate gateway, although that architecture could reduce HMS hardware content per machine while also creating demand for HMS software, configuration and diagnostics. Component inflation has broadened beyond initial expectations, with the market entering Q3 expecting memory and selected semiconductor costs to pressure gross margin but HMS reporting that inflation had spread to printed circuit boards and passive components across a broader part of the portfolio, while DNB Carnegie's Joachim Gunell had forecast a 62.6% Q3 gross margin and expected supply-chain headwinds to persist into mid- 2027, broadly matching the reported direction but underestimating the breadth of the cost issue before the release.

Lead times changed customer behaviour and increased working-capital requirements, with approximately SEK70 million of Industrial Network Technology orders brought forward because customers wanted to secure supply before lead times worsened or prices increased, while HMS's Q1 response to longer semiconductor lead times was to build larger inventories of critical components and by Q3 working capital included an inventory increase, so supply resilience is coming at a working-capital cost.

Leadership continuity remains intact, with Staffan Dahlström remaining Chief Executive Officer and Joakim Nideborn remaining Chief Financial Officer and Deputy Chief Executive Officer, both presenting the 21 October 2026 results, while the documented Group Management roster retains Chief Operating Officer Richard Skog, Chief Human Resources Officer Mira Jhaveri Winther, Industrial Data Solutions leader Alexander Hess and Industrial Network Technology leader Bartek S. Candell, with no departure or replacement rationale disclosed during the review period.

Section 1 – Brief company description and history

Section summary
  1. HMS entered the 21 October 2026 results with a stronger earnings base, broadening industrial demand and a credible acquisition engine, but its valuation still assumed sustained organic recovery and made any evidence of temporary orders or margin pressure material to EPS expectations. Niche leadership, structurally advantaged: HMS Networks supplies hardware and software for Industrial Information and Communication Technology, connecting machines, sensors, robots, drives, heating, ventilation and air-conditioning equipment, and other industrial assets to networks and information systems, while its installed base and product breadth support a market-leading position.
1.
Sticky business model: HMS sells through direct channels, distributors, solution partners and e-commerce, with the Industrial Networks Technology division relying heavily on long-term “Design-Win” agreements that require customers to integrate HMS products into their machines before production, creating a longer sales cycle but potentially durable revenue once adopted.
2.
Three-division architecture: Industrial Data Solutions connects, secures, diagnoses and visualizes industrial data; Industrial Networks Technology provides embedded wired and wireless communication across industrial protocols; and New Industries addresses building automation, vehicle communication and other specialist applications.
3.
High-margin core, diversified edges: Before the results, DNB Carnegie estimated Industrial Data Solutions at 48% of sales and 46% of adjusted EBIT, Industrial Networks Technology at 29% of sales and 33% of adjusted EBIT, and New Industries at 23% of sales and 22% of adjusted EBIT, making IDS the earnings anchor while INT supplied the strongest cyclical upside.
4.
Three-year growth reset: HMS reported 2023 revenue of SEK3,025 million against SEK2,506 million in 2022, while 2025 revenue reached SEK3,577 million, meaning the business had expanded materially from its pre- Red Lion base even though the intervening order cycle was volatile.
5.
Profitability recovered ahead of expectations: HMS achieved a 25.7% adjusted operating margin in 2023, compared with 25.0% in 2022, and then reported a 25.5% adjusted operating margin in 2025, showing that margin delivery had remained around or above the company’s earlier 25% objective despite the difficult industrial cycle.
6.
Cash generation strengthened: Operating cash flow rose from SEK431 million in 2022 to SEK519 million in 2023 and SEK877 million in 2025, so the company entered 2026 with a materially stronger cash-generation platform than it had during the post-pandemic inventory correction.
7.
Leverage had become a valuation variable: HMS’s net debt to adjusted EBITDA reached approximately 3.4x in 2024 during the Red Lion investment phase, while DNB Carnegie expected leverage to fall to 1.5x excluding International Financial Reporting Standard 16 by the end of 2026, making cash conversion and EPS growth important not only for earnings but also for renewed acquisition capacity. - Analyst/Investor EPS momentum was already visible: For the first nine months of 2026, reported basic EPS increased to SEK9.60 from SEK7.23, while adjusted basic EPS increased to SEK11.24 from SEK8.98, confirming that the earnings recovery had moved beyond revenue growth and into per-share earnings despite the enlarged share base following the 2024 equity issue.
8.
Geographic exposure was uneven: In 2025, EMEA represented 44% of HMS revenue, the Americas 42% and APAC 14%, while the Q3 2026 mix was more concentrated in the Americas for IDS and more balanced across regions in INT and New Industries, leaving HMS exposed to North American data-centre and semiconductor investment as well as European industrial recovery.
9.
North America became strategically central: Red Lion added a substantial US platform and broadened HMS’s product range, while the Molex industrial communications acquisition added North American customers and engineering capability to INT, so the market expected US exposure to accelerate growth even as it increased tariff and customer-concentration sensitivity. end-market mix was broader than factory automation: HMS serves factory and process automation, energy and infrastructure, transport and logistics, building automation, data centres and semiconductorrelated applications, reducing dependence on one vertical but leaving roughly 40% of sales exposed to industrial automation capital-expenditure cycles according to DNB Carnegie. Acquisition-led scale was part of the investment case: HMS completed or announced a sequence of acquisitions including Red Lion, Molex’s industrial communications business and the proposed purchase of Robot, while DNB Carnegie described Robot as the company’s 17th acquisition since 2012 and estimated that it could add about 2% to 2026 pro forma EPS before synergies. 2030 targets set a high bar: HMS targets more than SEK7.5 billion of 2030 sales, a 25% EBITA margin, annual recurring revenue equal to 10% of sales, gross margin above 65%, direct sales above 55%, a 30% to 50% adjusted EPS payout and net debt to EBITDA below 2.5x. The target implied substantial execution risk: DNB Carnegie calculated that the 2030 plan required roughly 16% to 18% annual sales and EBITA growth from the 2025 starting point, split approximately evenly between organic growth and mergers and acquisitions, which was ambitious relative to the broader industrial automation market’s expected 3% to 4% annual growth. Software was the long-duration rerating option: HMS aimed to increase annual recurring revenue from approximately 3% of sales to 10% by 2030 through cloud, remote access, edge computing, analytics and service-oriented offerings, but DNB Carnegie noted that the recurring-revenue base was still small and therefore could not yet fully offset the company’s cyclical hardware exposure.

Competitive positioning was strongest where fragmentation mattered: HMS’s value proposition rests on translating between multiple industrial communication protocols and embedding connectivity into customer equipment, while standardization remains a long-term threat because fewer incompatible networks could reduce the need for protocol-conversion products. The pre-results sell-side bar was above published consensus: DNB Carnegie’s 18 September preview forecast Q3 sales of SEK1,032 million, orders of SEK1,034 million and EBITA of SEK284 million, with EBITA 5% above Bloomberg consensus and 2026 to 2027 EPS 2% to 8% above consensus, so a merely in-line result was unlikely to satisfy investors after the share-price recovery.

The delivery was operationally strong but not a clean upside surprise: HMS reported Q3 sales of SEK1,024 million, orders of SEK1,104 million and EBITA of SEK280 million, broadly matching DNB Carnegie’s sales and EBITA expectations but exceeding its order estimate, while approximately SEK70 million of orders had been brought forward and therefore weakened the quality of the headline order beat. 21 Oct HMS Networks – 2026 Q3 Interim Report HMS Networks Q3 and nine-month 2026 operating performance Market expectations had shifted from recovery to proof: DNB Carnegie described the shares as falling 5% after the report despite calling the results good, attributing the reaction to concern that data-centre orders, pre-ordering and margin performance might represent an “as good as it gets” phase rather than a durable acceleration.

Section 2 – Sell-side and market focus prior to these recent earnings

Section summary

High order bar, bullish: DNB Carnegie expected Q3 order intake of SEK1,034 million, equivalent to 15% organic growth year on year and 6% sequential growth, because automation and information and communication technology peers were signalling a broadening recovery; the market therefore needed more than a low-base rebound to validate the earnings momentum story. Revenue expectation, measured: DNB Carnegie forecast Q3 sales of SEK1,032 million, broadly in line with Visible Alpha consensus, so revenue itself was not expected to drive a major positive revision unless HMS delivered stronger organic growth or demonstrated that orders would convert rapidly into sales. Section 13 – Value chain: disruption forces

Section summary
  1. Disruption risk is rising from protocol standardization, edge intelligence, open industrial wireless and integrated automation platforms, but HMS can still benefit if it moves from selling gateways toward secure, software-enabled lifecycle connectivity.
1.
Industrial Ethernet, accelerating: HMS’s 2026 market study found Industrial Ethernet represented 79% of newly installed industrial nodes, up from 76% in 2025 and 34% in 2015, so the market expected continued migration while the delivery confirmed that the protocol-fragmentation moat is narrowing over time. The immediate EPS risk is limited because installed machines still require migration, integration and support, but the three-year risk is that fewer new protocols require less conversion hardware.
2.
, changing rather than disappearing: A former HMS developer said customers still need products supporting multiple networks because different regions and industrial segments use different protocols, which supports HMS’s current value proposition. However, the same evidence indicates that Ethernet dominance is changing where research and development resources should be allocated, meaning HMS must capture value in gateways, diagnostics, safety and software rather than rely on protocol count alone.
3.
Open wireless, emerging threat: The OpenSTX Foundation launched in 2025 to develop a vendor-neutral, time-sensitive wireless standard for industrial automation, smart cities and cyber-physical systems. If adoption becomes meaningful over the next three years, a common wireless layer could reduce proprietary integration work and compress hardware differentiation, although the current evidence shows an initiative rather than commercial-scale deployment.
4.
Wireless architecture, broadening: Modern factories increasingly combine Wi-Fi 7, private 5G and software- defined wide-area networking according to HCL Technologies, with each technology serving different latency, mobility and density requirements. This expands the connectivity market but creates a substitution risk for HMS because customers may buy an integrated wireless-and-edge architecture from a telecom or automation vendor instead of assembling gateways from separate suppliers.
5.
Edge computing, value migration: Omdia data cited at an NXP event showed 23% of manufacturers had started using industrial edge and 38% expected to increase usage within 18 months, raising the market’s expectation that intelligence will move closer to machines. The resulting revenue opportunity is not automatically HMS’s because edge control can migrate toward semiconductor vendors, automation platforms and systems integrators.
6.
Connectivity moving into silicon: NXP’s MCX A5 industrial microcontroller integrates Ethernet, Single Pair Ethernet, topology discovery and security directly into edge nodes, allowing original equipment manufacturers to embed communications without a separate gateway. That architecture could reduce HMS hardware content per machine, although it may also create demand for HMS software, configuration, diagnostics and secure lifecycle management.
7.
Artificial intelligence, opportunity with uncertain capture: Jefferies’ 22 September 2026 expert call argued that physical artificial intelligence should produce more measurable productivity, utilization and cost benefits than office-focused applications, with intelligence increasingly embedded in machines and robots. This supports HMS’s Ekkono relationship and its investment in artificial-intelligence competence, but the Q3 update disclosed no artificial-intelligence revenue, subscription growth or quantified productivity benefit, so the near-term EPS contribution remains unproven.
8.
AI economics, hardware-led: The Jefferies expert expected the greatest long-term value from edge artificial- intelligence semiconductors and physical infrastructure rather than frontier models, because industrial applications often cannot tolerate cloud latency or connectivity dependence. HMS can participate through secure data access and embedded connectivity, but semiconductor and platform vendors may capture the largest share of the incremental gross profit unless HMS owns the device-to-service workflow.
9.
310 automation, competitive escalation: Schneider Electric was promoting open, softwaredefined automation and industrial artificial-intelligence foundations in October 2026, combining automation, electrification, software and data for more connected operations. Siemens’ longer-term automation thesis similarly identifies software-defined automation and operational-technology/information-technology convergence as secular growth drivers. HMS therefore faces larger competitors moving into the same higher-value layer that its 2030 software ambition assumes it can monetize.
10.
Cybersecurity, defensive moat: Gartner’s cybersecurity expert described exponential adoption, stronger platform integration and greater vendor consolidation as industrial security priorities. HMS’s global ISO/IEC 27001 certification therefore improves its credibility in design wins and installed-base management, but it also raises the standard customers will expect and increases the cost of maintaining secure products over their full lifecycle.
11.
AI-enabled cyber risk, underappreciated: A 19 October 2026 industrial-security account described artificial intelligence testing that adapted to supervisory-control interfaces and identified gateways with authenticated connections to multiple devices, illustrating how connected plants can become higher-value attack surfaces. For HMS, this increases demand for secure gateways and firmware management but also raises liability, support and reputational risk if its products become part of a compromised industrial path. Section summary
  1. Political exposure has become a direct near-term gross-margin risk through United States and Canada tariff changes, while cybersecurity, machinery-safety and environmental rules create longer-term product-cost and market-access obligations that HMS presents more as commercial opportunities than quantified earnings risks.
1.
Tariff exposure, worsened: HMS previously expected tariffs mainly on approximately 15% of group sales consisting of finished goods made in Europe and shipped to North America, plus higher costs for China- and Asia-sourced components entering US manufacturing, but summer rule changes brought previously exempt categories into scope and contributed to the Q3 gross-margin decline from 64.1% to 62.7%, or 62.4% excluding the SEK3 million refund.Summer tariff changes widened exposure.
2.
Canada, unexpected complication: Management had previously identified Canada as a manageable but surprising tariff risk because an electronics manufacturing-services partner operated there, whereas Q3 confirmed that revised Canada-US rules were still affecting HMS and its North American flows; the immediate EPS effect is higher landed cost and possible logistics redesign, while the company has disclosed no quantified tariff charge or recovery assumption.Canada became a renewed margin risk.

Section 15 – Value chain: supply side

Section summary
  1. Supply economics deteriorated materially after the previous quarter, with semiconductor allocation, broader component inflation and tariffs now affecting gross margin, while HMS’s sourcing controls, safety stocks and York capacity investment reduce availability risk but increase inventory and capital intensity.
1.
Inventory normalization has reversed selectively: Customer destocking had largely normalized in 2025 and HMS entered 2026 believing orders reflected real demand, but the new component shortage has created a different inventory requirement inside HMS itself; first-nine-month working capital contributed SEK65 million to operating cash flow versus SEK142 million in the prior year, with the company specifically identifying higher inventory and accounts payable, so the earlier inventory-release tailwind is no longer a reliable source of cash conversion.
2.
Supplier bargaining power has shifted upward: DNB Carnegie reported that memory prices had risen several hundred percent, with HMS’s CEO Staffan Dahlström indicating in September that increases could reach as much as tenfold because suppliers were prioritizing data-centre capacity over smaller industrial and automotive-grade components; this is materially worse than the March broker view that HMS was relatively insulated because its memory content was small and often based on older process nodes. Price pass-through is the main mitigation: HMS informed customers during Q3 that price increases would begin on 1 January 2027, whereas the market had expected pricing to offset memory and printed-circuitboard inflation; the timing still leaves Q4 and potentially early-2027 gross margin exposed because HMS expects the benefit only after the new prices have fully flowed through. Tariffs compound supplier inflation: Earlier tariff measures had been partly offset through price increases and exemptions, but revised Europe-US and Canada-US rules brought previously exempt product categories into scope during the summer; HMS said the broader tariffs negatively affected Q3 gross margin, while no quantified recovery rate or tariff sensitivity was disclosed, leaving the earnings effect less transparent than the company’s general pass-through message suggests. Dual sourcing and local production reduce concentration risk: HMS operates its own facilities in Halmstad and York and works with electronic manufacturing-services partners, while its responsible-sourcing framework includes alternative suppliers, critical-component safety stocks and digital forecasting tools; this is stronger resilience than the single-source vulnerability implied by the semiconductor allocation problem, although the company does not disclose supplier concentration, percentage dual-sourced or the proportion of spend covered by alternatives.
3.
York investment improves regional supply economics: HMS completed a major upgrade to its York, Pennsylvania facility, including new printed-circuit-board assembly equipment, an expanded surface-mount line, selective soldering, automated optical inspection and a new enterprise-resource-planning system; compared with the previous expectation that US production would mainly mitigate tariffs, the investment also increases throughput, quality control and inventory visibility for Red Lion and N-Tron products serving the Americas. Quality performance is an operational advantage: HMS targets quality down to 200 parts per million, equivalent to 0.02% errors, and its logistics centres in Sweden, Germany, Spain, the United States and China deliver more than 2,800 products to over 100 countries; this supports the company’s claim that supply flexibility can protect customer service, but the available Q3 disclosures provide no updated defect, expedite, freight or supplier-on-time data to prove that performance has held during the new component squeeze.
4.
Supplier financial health remains undisclosed: HMS uses supplier assessments, traffic-light risk ratings, audits and a Supplier Code of Conduct, and reported that 100% of strategic suppliers had accepted its ethical code, but neither HMS nor DNB Carnegie disclosed supplier returns on capital, insolvency exposure, paymentterm pressure or concentration by component; consequently, the report cannot establish whether suppliers are financially strong enough to sustain capacity investment outside the data-cententre boom. Freight is a smaller but unquantified risk: HMS reported higher logistics costs in Q1 without a material earnings impact, while the 2025 production model emphasized multiple regional logistics centres and electronic manufacturing-services partners; this suggests freight is less important than component pricing and tariffs, but the lack of Q3 freight disclosure means a future disruption in shipping, insurance or regional trade flows could still reach gross margin and inventory days without being visible in current forecasts.
5.
Other side of the story: The supply problem may be temporary and economically manageable if HMS secures allocation, converts its York investment into local production, receives customer acceptance of the January price increases and allows inventory to rebuild before the shortage peaks, but a deeper memory cycle or wider component scarcity would make the 62% margin trough too optimistic and force further 2027 EPS cuts.

Section 16 – Conclusion

Section summary
  1. The 21 October 2026 earnings strengthened HMS Networks’ medium-term demand case but weakened the near-term EPS path, because a clean-looking order beat contained SEK70 million of pre-ordering while broader component inflation and tariffs defer gross-margin recovery into 2027. Core outlook, cautiously improved: HMS delivered revenue of SEK1,024 million and EBITA of SEK280 million against DNB Carnegie’s forecasts of SEK1,032 million and SEK284 million, while orders reached SEK1,104 million versus SEK1,034 million expected; the result therefore beat the broker’s order bar but was broadly in line on revenue and EBITA rather than producing the clean operating upgrade the share price had required. Order quality, the key qualification: Reported organic order growth of 22% exceeded DNB Carnegie’s 15% expectation, but approximately SEK70 million of orders were brought forward because of lead times and January price increases, reducing underlying organic growth to about 14% and making the headline beat broadly in line with consensus after adjustment.
1.
Demand breadth, genuinely better: The recovery became less dependent on North America than the pre- results market expected, with organic orders up approximately 40% in China and 20% in Europe while North America grew just under 10%; this supports a broader industrial recovery, although data-centre automation and semiconductor demand remain the strongest pockets. IDS, the unresolved earnings gap: Industrial Data Solutions, which represented approximately 48% of 2025 sales according to DNB Carnegie, reported organic order decline of 3% and organic sales growth of 2%, while management said larger project orders were still needed; this fell short of the market’s expectation that the group recovery would increasingly include HMS’s largest division.
2.
INT, strong but non-repeatable headline: Industrial Network Technology delivered 70% organic order growth and 16% organic sales growth, well above the group’s pre-results recovery assumptions, but management said almost all of the SEK70 million pre-ordering occurred in INT; the segment is therefore the strongest evidence of demand and the largest source of subsequent comparison risk. - Earnings Calls Margins, worse beneath the surface: Q3 EBITA margin of 27.4% exceeded the company’s 25% long-term target and was close to DNB Carnegie’s SEK284 million EBITA expectation, but underlying gross margin was 62.4% after a SEK3 million tariff refund versus 62.7% reported, showing that operating leverage masked deteriorating product economics. 2027 EPS, now the critical bridge: DNB Carnegie cut adjusted EPS from SEK15.7 to SEK15.3 for 2026 and from SEK19.4 to SEK19.2 for 2027, while leaving 2028 at SEK21.9; the revisions imply a modest timing setback over the next 12 to 18 months rather than a permanent impairment of three-year earnings power. Margin trough, explicitly shifted forward: HMS expects pressure through Q4 and potentially into 2027 before January pricing fully reaches the income statement, while DNB Carnegie models a gross-margin trough near 62.0% in Q1 2027 and recovery when pricing takes effect in Q2; this makes the next two quarters more important to EPS than the Q3 beat. Cash flow, positive but not fully recurring: DNB Carnegie estimated Q3 free cash flow of SEK291 million, 55% above its forecast, but HMS disclosed that approximately SEK49 million of the SEK333 million operating cash flow came from higher accounts payable; cash therefore supports deleveraging and acquisitions, but should not be extrapolated at the reported conversion rate.
3.
Strategic position, still constructive: Lower leverage, the Molex platform and the proposed Robot acquisition improve HMS’s capacity to pursue its stated ambition that approximately half of growth come from acquisitions, while Robot adds building-automation sensors and room controls; however, neither transaction yet supplies enough quantified synergy or recurring-revenue evidence to change near-term EPS materially. Market reaction, a higher bar revealed: DNB Carnegie reported that the share price fell 5% after the release despite retaining Buy, because investors interpreted an in-line quarter as insufficient after the prior share-price strength and questioned whether pre-orders, data-centre demand and margin pressure represented an “as good as it gets” phase.
4.
Valuation, modestly reset: DNB Carnegie cut its target price from SEK661 to SEK654 and applied 27.5 times 2027 estimated enterprise value to EBIT, around 10% below the company’s five-to-ten-year historical average; the unchanged Buy rating and 2028 EPS forecast preserve the long-term case, but the lower multiple shows that investors now demand cleaner growth proof. Single most important change, timing risk: The central change is not a collapse in demand but the discovery that strong Q3 orders and future margin protection are occurring on different clocks, with customers ordering early while costs rise immediately and pricing benefits arrive later; that timing mismatch shifts risk from a 2026 volume question to a 2027 EPS question. - Earnings Calls Strongest case against this conclusion: The bullish interpretation may be too conservative because China, Europe and Japan improved together, inventory destocking has eased, and HMS has repeatedly exceeded margin expectations; if Q4 and Q1 orders remain strong after the SEK70 million pull-forward unwinds, the current DNB Carnegie EPS reductions could prove unnecessarily cautious.
