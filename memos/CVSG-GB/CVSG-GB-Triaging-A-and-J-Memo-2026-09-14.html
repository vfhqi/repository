[Materiality of] Investment Thesis (BB 1) Rating = D

Thesis. CVS Group is a cleared-overhang value case on a mid-quality veterinary consolidator, not a demand inflection and not a compounder. The regulator has stepped back, the cash now goes into Australian practices at about 6x EBITDA and into the company's own shares, and that carries earnings per share up at about 9 percent a year; at 1,295p the shares already reflect that much, so a return above the framework's 20 percent bar needs a UK organic recovery and a chief executive the market believes, and the evidence for neither exists yet.

  • The largest external change is the removal of a threat, not a new driver of the outputs.
    • The CMA's final decision of 24 March 2026 cleared the company with transparency remedies only: a £21 prescription-fee cap against the £16 proposed, and no forced divestments.
    • The market did not pay for it.
      • The shares were 20 percent lower in May 2026 than at the October 2025 provisional decision, and the 24-month-forward multiple has sat at its two-year median (12.04x) through the clearance.
    • The other external forces (competition from joint ventures and independents, statutory labour costs, weak UK consumer confidence) are headwinds of modest size, and they act on the one line the thesis most needs, UK organic growth.
  • The internal change is in where the cash goes, not in what the UK core earns.
    • UK acquisitions were paused for over two years
    • the Netherlands, Ireland and the Crematoria (at 10x EBITDA) were sold
    • Australia was entered in July 2023 and by June 2026 was 57 sites, 11 percent of revenue and 16 percent of EBITDA before central costs, bought at c.6x
    • a £20m buyback completed in January 2026 and a £50m programme began in May 2026, thirteen days after an activist letter.
    • At least three of the twelve internal forces are strong, which passes the framework's screen
      • but the chief executive announced his retirement on 30 March 2026 and no successor was named as of 7 August 2026, so the top seat is a vacancy, not an arrival.
  • The change has reached the financial statements but not the core's operating metric or the sell-side's estimates.
    • FY26 revenue was £712.8m (+5.9 percent) and adjusted EBITDA c.£141.5m (+5.1 percent) at a 19.9 percent margin, in line with consensus (23 July 2026).
    • Like-for-like sales grew 2.1 percent, slowing from +2.7 percent in the first half to c.+1.5 percent in the second, against a 4 to 8 percent framework the company has missed for three years.
    • Consensus earnings per share for FY27 has drifted down 2.2 percent over twelve months and is flat over three
      • after the July update the one Hold house trimmed and the Buy house held.
  • What we think it earns.
    • Earnings per share on the brokers' basis of £0.858 in FY26 rising to £1.12 in FY29, 9.0 percent a year: about 5.5 percent a year from revenue (acquisitions more than organic), 0.8 percent from margin, 1.0 percent from the lines below EBITDA and 1.5 percent from the falling share count.
    • That sits below the framework's 12 to 20 percent band at every horizon after FY27.
      • It does not depend on the UK core recovering, which is why it is probable
      • it also does not clear the bar, which is why the case needs more.
  • What it costs. 1,295p is 12.07x 24-month-forward earnings of £1.073 (11 September 2026), the 30th percentile of the stock's five-year range and the median of its last two years
    • about 7.7x next-twelve-month EBITDA
    • a dividend yield of 0.7 percent.
    • The price assumes the modest version of the case and nothing more: no organic recovery, no multiple above the two-year median, no deployment beyond FY27, no chief executive.
  • The return does not clear the bar on the modal path.
    • Over eighteen months the cash return adds 1.2 percent, the earnings 10.9 percent and a blended re-rating to 12.8x adds 6.0 percent: +18.8 percent in total, 12.2 percent a year
    • over three years 9.9 percent a year.
    • The re-rating is the swing.
      • On the cleared branch (14.0x, a chief executive named and UK volumes turning) the eighteen-month return is 19.0 percent a year
      • on the uncleared branch (11.33x) it is 3.5 percent a year.
      • We put the odds at 55 to 45.
    • On a plausibly worse drop-through from EBITDA to earnings per share (the R&D tax credit halved, net interest £3m a year higher, tax at 30 percent) the modal return falls to 3.9 percent a year and the 'Modal return' rating from D to F.
    • The modal path clears the 20 percent bar only at or below about 1,170p
      • the shares are 10 percent above that.
  • The pattern is 'Major Corporate Change', and it fits loosely.
    • The inputs pass the framework's screen, the outputs show in the level of earnings, and the trajectory of the core is flat
    • the fit is a C.

  • The case is therefore coherent but not compelling.

    • Earnings growth of 9 to 10 percent a year is probable and largely contracted (deals done, a buyback under way, a margin that held at 19 to 20 percent through the wage shock)
    • the part that clears the bar, a re-rating earned by UK volumes and a named chief executive, is a near-even call that the market has so far declined to make.

  • Rating = D, because the expected change is not material enough at this price: the change forces are a C (one large threat removed, capital-allocation forces strong, operating forces weak), the financial outputs a D (earnings growth of 9 to 10 percent a year with flat estimates), and the 'Modal return' a D (about 12 percent a year over eighteen months and about 10 percent over three, against the 20 percent bar).

    • Not C, because a C would need either a modal return of 13 to 17 percent a year or earnings growth inside the band, and neither holds on the evidence
    • C is the live neighbour because a single half-year of UK like-for-like growth at 4 percent or more, with the chief executive named, would move both the growth and the multiple odds.

Most plausible competing hypothesis, and our response.

  • The case against, at its strongest: the market is treating CVS as ex-growth on 7.7x EV/EBITDA and 12x 24-month-forward earnings, yet the company converts 75 percent of EBITDA to operating cash, buys practices at 6x in an Australian market that is 84 percent unconsolidated, buys its own shares at 8x, and faces a dated demand turn as the pandemic pet cohort ages.
    • On that view the 9 to 10 percent earnings growth is a floor, the £105m to £135m Australian EBITDA target adds 300p a share of value on the sell-side's own arithmetic at £50m a year of deployment, and the multiple returns to the five-year median or better, which is an 18 to 27 percent a year return.
  • What we concede: the cash conversion, the deal multiples and the buyback accretion are all real.
  • Why we hold the D: the organic engine that would justify the re-rating has been below the company's own framework for three years and decelerated in the second half of FY26.
    • The Australian deployment and the buyback compete for the same leverage headroom (1.63x heading towards the 2.0x ceiling), so both cannot run at full pace.
    • The 24-month-forward multiple has spent two years at its current level, through the regulatory clearance, which tells us the market is waiting for the organic evidence, as we are.
Change Forces: are the input change forces sufficiently strong? (E 1, BB 1) Rating = C
  • Rating = C, because the one strong external change is the removal of a threat rather than a driver of outputs, the demand, competition and labour forces are mild headwinds, and the internal forces, though at least three are strong, are capital-allocation forces (where the money goes) rather than an operating transformation, with the chief executive seat a vacancy rather than an arrival.

Internal forces: are there sufficiently strong internal change forces under way? (RA 2, E 1, BB 1) Rating = B
  • Rating = B, because at least three of the twelve forces are strong (new geography, acquisitions step-up, perimeter reduction, shareholder returns, balance-sheet use), which passes the framework's screen, while the chief executive change is pending rather than made and guidance has not been raised.
  • Red-flag check: no caretaker chief executive as such (the incumbent stays until a successor is appointed), though the seat is in transition
    • no presentation-only transformation (the deals and disposals are done)
    • no crown-jewel sale (the Crematoria were 3.2 percent of EBITDA)
    • no empire-building on the evidence (deals at c.6x, and the company says it has declined more deals than it accepted)
    • leverage of 1.63x against a 2.0x board ceiling and a 3.25x covenant is not dangerous.
  • Debt-funded buyback: the one red flag that partly applies
    • the remaining £38.3m of the £50m programme pushes leverage towards the 2.0x limit.

External forces: are there sufficiently strong external forces acting on the company, new or intensifying, and in which direction? (RA 1, E 1, BB 1) Rating = C

Political and regulatory: is a change moving demand, pricing or cost? (KQ 4, RA 1, E 1, BB 1) Rating = B
  • Rating = B, because the CMA's final decision of 24 March 2026 is the largest external change in the record and it moved in the company's favour: no structural remedies, a £21 (inclusive of VAT) cap on the first written prescription against the £16 proposed, plus £12.50 per additional drug, against a market average already around £20.
  • Residual cost: c.£6m of dual-branding signage
    • compliance orders due by 23 September 2026 with three to twelve months to comply
    • fines of up to 5 percent of global turnover for non-compliance under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act
    • a DEFRA white paper of July 2026 proposing business licensing under a replacement for the Veterinary Surgeons Act 1966.
  • Second-order effects: transparency may accelerate online leakage and may pressure manufacturer rebates.
  • Not A, because the change is the removal of a feared harm with a small net cost, not a force that raises revenue or margin
    • the market's reaction was muted, with the shares 20 percent lower in May 2026 than at the October 2025 provisional decision.
Demand: is end-market demand changing, structurally or cyclically (a swing with two-plus years of visibility)? (KQ 1, RA 1, E 1, BB 1) Rating = C
  • Rating = C, because UK end-market demand is flat to soft in routine care and resilient in acute and referral care, and the promised turn (the pandemic cohort reaching five to six years of age by late 2026 and FY27) is a hypothesis stated by the company and its peer rather than a swing evidenced by industry data.
  • Routine visits: healthy adult pets are in the low-spend phase
    • price-sensitive owners are deferring vaccinations, dental cleaning and routine checks
    • Morgan Stanley's practitioner surveys of April and July 2026 describe a persisting soft patch.
  • Cohort turn: both CVS and Pets at Home expect the 2020 to 2022 pet cohort to reach five to six years by late 2026 and FY27, the age at which spend rises on the U-shaped curve
    • spend per visit and diagnostics utilisation are already rising and partly offset volume.
  • Sector base: UK pet care of c.£10bn growing at a 5 percent ten-year rate
    • only 5.8 percent of owners would cut pet spend in a recession
    • 4.6m UK policyholders.
  • Not D, because the demand is defensive and the acute end is growing
    • not B, because the framework's 'Demand Inflection' test (sector-level, evidenced by industry data, two-plus years' visibility) is not met.
Competition: is competitive intensity easing or tightening? (KQ 2, RA 1, E 1, BB 1) Rating = D
  • Rating = D, because competitive intensity is tightening for the fully corporate model in the UK (independents and de novo start-ups winning on trust, the joint-venture model winning on organic growth) and rising in Australia (private-equity consolidators bidding multiples up to 5x to 8x).
  • Three-speed UK market: independents are taking share on consumer trust
    • joint ventures use local clinical leadership
    • fully corporate groups are challenged by the brand-visibility mandates.
    • Pets at Home's Vet Group grew consumer revenue 5.0 percent to £688m and profit before tax 10.4 percent to £83.8m in FY26, against CVS's UK companion like-for-like of +1.4 percent in the second half of FY26.
  • New entrants: de novo practices such as Harrison Family Vets in affluent areas.
  • Australia: multiples of 5x to 8x and rising with corporate entry
    • CVS is still completing at c.6x.
  • Not C, because the peer's organic delivery is direct evidence that the competitive position of the fully corporate model has weakened.
Supply side: input costs, availability, concentration? (KQ 5, RA 1, E 1, BB 1) Rating = D
  • Rating = D, because labour is the dominant input (employment costs 52.2 percent of revenue in FY25, up from 51.9 percent) and statutory cost rises of c.£11m a year (£8m of employer National Insurance plus £3m of minimum wage, annualised from April 2025 to April 2026) are a real headwind that the company has so far offset with head-office cuts and rosters rather than with pricing.
  • Easing at the margin: the vet vacancy rate fell to 6.5 percent in FY25 from 9.3 percent
    • EU vets are returning
    • new veterinary schools are projected to close the shortage within five to six years
    • average vets employed rose 1.3 percent in 2025.
  • Medicines: two significant wholesaler contracts with alternatives available
    • manufacturer rebates and white-label lines support net drug costs
    • Windsor Framework friction in Northern Ireland.
  • Not C, because wage inflation in a labour-short profession is structural and the offsets used so far (central cost cuts) are finite.
Revenue and demand cycle: where is the company's own cycle, and is it turning? (KQ 6, RA 1, E 1, BB 1) Rating = C
  • Rating = C, because the company's own cycle troughed in FY25 (like-for-like +0.2 percent) and turned (FY26 +2.1 percent), but the turn lost pace within the year (H1 +2.7 percent, H2 c.+1.5 percent, UK companion H2 +1.4 percent) and remains below the 4 to 8 percent framework the company has missed for three years (FY23 +7.3 percent, FY24 +2.9 percent, FY25 +0.2 percent).
  • Not B, because a turning cycle would show acceleration, not the second-half deceleration.

Summary: Written Summary of the Investment Case (not rated) (E 3, BB 4)
The case in 8 to 10 sentences (the thesis as tested), kept in full at both ends of the record (Part 1, E 3, BB 4)
  • This is a cleared-overhang value case on a mid-quality consolidator, not a demand inflection and not a compounder.
  • CVS Group is a UK veterinary platform (89 percent of revenue) that has spent two years under a CMA investigation, paused UK acquisitions, sold its non-core units, redirected its cash into Australian practices at c.6x EBITDA and, prompted by an activist, started buying back its own shares at c.8x EBITDA
    • on 24 March 2026 the regulator cleared it with transparency remedies only, and on 30 March 2026 its chief executive announced his retirement with no successor yet named.
  • The change has travelled from choices to actions to the financial statements: Australia is 11 percent of revenue and 16 percent of EBITDA before central costs, the Crematoria proceeds are recycled, the share count is falling.
  • It has not reached the key operating metric of the core (UK like-for-like growth was 2.1 percent in FY26 and c.1.5 percent in the second half, against a 4 to 8 percent framework missed for three years) or the sell-side's estimates (flat to down 2 to 4 percent over twelve months).
  • On our modal path earnings per share on the brokers' basis grow about 9 percent a year (FY26 £0.858 to FY29 £1.12), roughly half from acquisitions, a little from margin, a quarter from a flat depreciation line and the buyback
    • the yield is 0.7 percent
    • the 24-month-forward multiple is 12.07x, the 30th percentile of five years and the median of two.
  • The compelling version of the case needs the multiple to return to the five-year median or better, and that needs UK organic growth to recover on the pandemic pet cohort's ageing and a chief executive the market believes
    • the evidence for the first is a hypothesis shared by the company and its peer, and the evidence for the second does not yet exist.
  • The case is coherent but not compelling: the modal return is about 12 percent a year over eighteen months and about 10 percent over three, below the 20 percent bar, and it clears the bar on the modal path only at or below about 1,170p.
  • What we are betting on, in one line: that a disciplined but leaderless consolidator can compound earnings per share at 9 to 10 percent a year through acquisitions and buybacks while the UK core idles, and that the market pays 14x rather than 11x for it once a chief executive is named and one half-year shows UK volumes turning
    • the first is probable, the second is a near-even call, and at 1,295p the price needs the second.
