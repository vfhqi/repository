Section 1 – The Key Question and Why It Matters

Section summary
  1. Maersk's earnings remain highly sensitive to freight rates as the container industry faces a massive oversupply wave and potential geopolitical normalization.
  2. A relatively small shift in average loaded rates can swing the company between multi-billion dollar profits and severe cash drains.

Defining the Key Question

1.
Critical baseline: "how much pricing power does this company have, and how does it hold up across different operating conditions, specifically: a container supply wave with fleet growth well above demand growth (2027-2028); a return of container shipping to the Suez Canal and the Red Sea; a renewed route closure or port congestion episode; a falling or rising bunker price; rising carbon costs under EU ETS and FuelEU Maritime; a demand air pocket after tariff-driven front-loading?"

According to Morgan Stanley, a change of only one hundred dollars per FFE in container freight rates impacts Maersk's EBIT by one billion dollars. This means a modest rate correction can rapidly erode the unusually strong earnings performance recorded during recent congestion.
3.
Volatile projections: Financial forecasts for the logistics giant demonstrate how quickly profitability is expected to contract as market disruptions normalize. Analyst estimates from Deutsche Bank project that Maersk's underlying EPS will slide from over five hundred dollars in fiscal year 2026 to under eighty-three dollars in fiscal year 2027. This stark drop underlines how the company's earnings are highly vulnerable to spot market adjustments.
4.
Drastic declines: Operational forecasts from alternative financial models show an even steeper drop-off in cash generation. Projections from DNB Carnegie suggest adjusted EPS will fall from over five hundred dollars in fiscal year 2026 to around two hundred and ten dollars in fiscal year 2027, followed by a negative adjusted EPS in fiscal year 2028. These bearish expectations highlight that long term profitability remains highly fragile once current supply chain bottlenecks clear.

Section 2 – Market Structure and Supply-Demand Shocks

Section summary
  1. Structural container oversupply from a massive orderbook will clash with normalizing demand to depress shipping rates through 2028.
  2. The sudden unwinding of tariff-related volume front-loading is highly likely to trigger a severe demand air pocket.
  3. Historical precedents from 2016 and 2023 demonstrate that fleet overcapacity quickly forces carriers to surrender their pricing power.
  4. Geopolitical route closures and subsequent port congestion historically trigger massive spot freight rate surges and elevated carrier pricing power.
  5. The rapid return of container shipping to the Suez Canal in late 2026 is quickly unwinding these pricing gains by releasing effective capacity.
  6. Maersk's Gemini huband-spoke network aims to secure high schedule reliability but faces competitive pressure from MSC's direct-call strategy.

Imminent Fleet Growth and Oversupply Risks

1.
Rapid expansion: Global cellular capacity is projected to expand dramatically as a massive ordering wave delivers new containerships through 2028. Broker research from Deutsche Bank in October 2026 estimates that fleet capacity will grow by 9.5% in 2027, far outstripping projected demand growth of only 4.1%. This extreme mismatch is driven by an orderbook that stands at approximately 44% of the active global fleet. This imbalance threatens to severely depress ocean freight rates as newly built vessels enter service.
2.
Accelerated deliveries: The physical volume of vessel completions scheduled for the near term is set to reach unprecedented heights. Jefferies reports in November 2026 that the global orderbook contains 14.90 mm TEU of capacity, with 3.50 mm TEU arriving in 2027 and another 5.50 mm TEU scheduled for 2028. These massive additions represent 6.90% and 8.90% of the projected global fleet capacity respectively. This influx will almost certainly drown out positive demand trends once regional routing normalizes.
3.
Prolonged pain: Container lines are expected to suffer from a prolonged supply-demand imbalance as vessel demolition remains insufficient to offset additions. Bernstein Research notes in October 2026 that shipping lines are playing a game of chicken, collectively refusing to scrap vessels in order to defend individual market shares. Even with 2.50 mm TEU of expected scrapping, capacity will still vastly outpace structural trade growth. Consequently, Maersk's ocean segment faces long term freight rate compression that will severely test its operational cash flow.
4.
Contrary evidence: Some analysts argue that actual fleet growth could be significantly mitigated if environmental regulations or vessel scrapping accelerate faster than expected. Fubon Research via Jefferies in November 2026 suggests that the phasing out of aging vessels, which comprise 10% to 15% of the global fleet, could largely offset the impending oversupply. Furthermore, they argue that ongoing port congestion and geopolitical route disruptions will continue to absorb excess vessel capacity. This alternative view suggests container rates could remain at elevated levels throughout 2027.

Expert insights from a former CSX director in July 2026 show that tariff announcements trigger short-term volume surges that temporarily spike rates. However, these artificial peaks are inevitably followed by a sharp trade slowdown as inventories normalize. This post-tariff air pocket will leave ocean carriers with massive idle capacity and zero pricing leverage.
6.
Fading momentum: The artificial demand generated by pre-emptive retail stockpiling is already projected to dissipate rapidly. Public Investment Bank Berhad reported in November 2025 that global trade has been fairly resilient, underpinned by front-loading activities. However, they confirm that this front-loading effect is expected to fade, leading to slower trade growth. This collapse in booking activity will expose the underlying fleet overcapacity and accelerate the downward rate spiral.
7.
Temporary spikes: Importers have aggressively accelerated shipments to beat impending tariff deadlines, causing temporary space tightness. Rabobank Economic Research reported in August 2026 that spot rates on Trans- Pacific and Asia-Europe trades spiked to two-year highs due to tariff-related front-loading. Importers prioritized supply chain resilience ahead of the July tariff deadlines, tightening vessel space. This front-loading activity has temporarily masked structural market overcapacity before normalizing demand returns.
8.
Severe corrections: Trade data suggests that container volume growth will experience a sharp cyclical contraction after front-loading passes. Huatai Research reported in January 2026 that previous US tariff changes on Chinese goods prompted shippers to suspend bookings, causing freight rates to plunge. They expect container throughput growth to slow down significantly as elevated overseas inventories are gradually digested. This inventory destocking will force Maersk to offer steep discounts to maintain its vessel utilization.

According to Maersk's Q4 2024 results, the average loaded freight rate surged 38% year-on-year to $2,659/FFE in late 2024 due to persistent Red Sea diversions. This sudden route closure forced carriers to divert around the Cape of Good Hope, absorbing about 7% of global fleet capacity. Ultimately, these route disruptions acted as a massive windfall for short-term ocean earnings before capacity normalizes.

ZIM reported an average freight rate of $1,888/TEU for the full year of 2024, representing a 57% increase compared to $1,203/TEU in 2023. By comparison, Hapag-Lloyd's average rate was relatively flat at $1,492/TEU in 2024 compared to $1,500/TEU in 2023 due to its higher long-term contract share. This instant capacity release will have a much more severe impact on rates than any gradual delivery of new containerships. Gemini Alliance Operational Mechanics

1.
Ambitious integration: Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd established the Gemini Cooperation in February 2025 to overhaul their shared East-West ocean network. Across our research, we found 24 relevant sources, 6 companies, and 2 comparable past cases. This alliance design revolves around a unique hub-and-spoke model connecting mainline services to regional shuttle services. This structural integration seeks to maximize vessel capacity utilization and lower unit costs across major trades.
2.
Superior operational targets: Hapag-Lloyd reports that the alliance successfully reached a schedule reliability of around 90% in its first year of operation. This performance compared favorably to the historical global container shipping schedule reliability average of approximately 60%. Maersk has utilized this baseline to argue that the cooperation offers unprecedented predictability for global supply chains while reducing costly vessel delays.
3.
Structured terminal synergy: Maersk’s management has actively directed Gemini's transshipment volumes toward its own APM Terminals to drive operational synergies. The partners expect this terminal concentration to yield annualized EBIT benefits of $820 mm to $1.10 bn through fuel efficiency and faster vessel turn times. However, concentrated transshipments at select hubs like Tanjung Pelepas introduce risks where a single failure can disrupt the entire network during periods of high congestion.

Evaluating the Schedule Reliability Premium

4.
Skeptical market response: Shippers appear highly unwilling to pay any pricing premium for the schedule reliability promised by the Gemini partners. Historical analysis by Carnegie from 2019 to mid-2024 demonstrates that high schedule reliability does not coincide with elevated freight rates. In contrast, rates are historically highest when supply chains are severely constrained and reliability collapses. Shippers frequently prioritize low rates over schedule integrity, meaning reliability alone rarely translates into premium pricing power.
5.
Severe historical precedent: The post-pandemic supply chain disruption of 2021-2022 saw global schedule reliability collapse to a record low of 36% as vessels missed berthing windows. Despite these severe delays, major container terminals experienced burst congestion that kept cargo volumes artificially constrained. This historical precedent suggests that freight rates are driven by aggregate capacity supply and demand rather than individual network reliability metrics.
6.
Modest interest savings: Calculations show that even if Gemini achieves its 90% target, the financial benefit to cargo owners is highly limited. Assuming a cargo value of $55,000 per box and a 5% interest rate, reducing delays by 2 to 3 days yields interest savings of only $40 per box. This saving is extremely small compared to an average freight rate of $1,000 per TEU. Shippers are therefore unlikely to accept rate hikes in exchange for marginal inventory holding cost reductions.

Impact of the August 2026 Congestion

7.
Severe network stress: Global shipping networks suffered extreme disruptions in August 2026 as typhoons and landside bottlenecks crippled key ports. Global container schedule reliability fell by 5.90 percentage points month-on-month to just 49.90% in August 2026. This congestion tied up 4.30 mm TEUs of capacity, which represented approximately 12.60% of the active global fleet. The disruption proved that external environmental shocks can rapidly degrade alliance reliability targets regardless of network design.
8.
Eroded reliability differences: Sea-Intelligence data reveals that Gemini’s reliability advantage over competitors narrowed significantly during the late-2026 congestion episode. In August and September 2026, Gemini recorded an average schedule reliability of 68.40%, which actually fell slightly below Mediterranean Shipping Company's standalone direct-call reliability of 68.90%. This narrow gap confirms that complex hub-and-spoke models possess no structural advantage over direct-call networks during severe system-wide port congestion.
9.
