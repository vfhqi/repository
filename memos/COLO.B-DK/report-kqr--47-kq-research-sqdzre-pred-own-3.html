Section 1 – The Key Question and Why It Matters

Section summary
  1. The upcoming 2028 Medicare competitive bidding round represents a pivotal regulatory risk that could compress the US operating margins and earnings per share of Coloplast and Convatec starting in 2028.

Regulatory context and scope

Critical regulatory challenge: "For Coloplast A/S (COLO.B-DK), what is the exact US exposure to the Medicare DMEPOS competitive bidding round for ostomy, tracheostomy and urological supplies starting 1 January 2028, by channel (Comfort Medical retail, independent wholesale and distributors, Medicare fee-for-service against Medicare Advantage and commercial payers) and by product (including the separate hydrophilic catheter product category under the January 2026 HCPCS codes), what did manufacturers' net prices do after earlier rounds for CPAP, diabetic testing supplies and mobility and over what period, did Medicare Advantage plans and commercial payers follow the single payment amounts, and what is the legislative progress (committee referrals, co-sponsors, scoring) of H.R. 10787, the Access to Medical Supplies Act?"
2.
Significant margin uncertainty: Coloplast faces a critical regulatory transition as the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services implements competitive bidding for chronic care consumables starting no later than January 2028. This program targets ostomy, continence, and tracheostomy supplies, which represent key growth drivers in the underpenetrated US market where the company currently holds a 20% share in ostomy. If implemented, the resulting pricing pressure could reduce the company's long-term operating margins and earnings per share if distributors pass costs backward.

Section 2 – Coloplast and Peer Exposure by Channel and Product Category

Section summary
  1. Coloplast is relatively insulated from Medicare competitive bidding because only 6% of group revenue is exposed, whereas peer Convatec has 7% direct exposure through 180 Medical and higher overall US channel vulnerability.
  2. The 2026 Medicare coding shift establishes distinct reimbursement pathways for hydrophilic catheters, potentially shielding these premium products from the severe price cuts expected under the 2028 competitive bidding program.

Sizing the US channel exposure

1.
Limited group exposure: Coloplast possesses a relatively insulated position with only 6% of group revenue exposed to the US Medicare chronic care competitive bidding program. This modest exposure is split between roughly 5% flowing through independent wholesale channels and 1% derived from its direct-to-consumer retail arm, Comfort Medical. As a result, the financial impact from direct reimbursement cuts is tightly constrained compared to peers with larger direct US distribution footprints.
2.
Elevated retail exposure: Convatec faces a more significant direct headwind because 7% of its group revenue is directly exposed to competitive bidding through its retail subsidiary, 180 Medical. Continence care accounts for 6% of this direct retail exposure, while ostomy care represents the remaining 1%. Consequently, any regulatory price reduction will directly compress Convatec's highly profitable US retail margins unless offset by substantial volume gains.
3.
Vulnerable wholesale channels: Both manufacturers also face indirect pricing pressure through independent wholesale distributors that represent additional US Medicare exposure for their chronic care portfolios. Coloplast estimates that about 50% of its US chronic care sales are related to Medicare, while Convatec estimates its indirect distributor Medicare exposure at roughly 2% of group sales. These wholesale segments will likely experience margin compression as independent distributors attempt to renegotiate purchasing agreements to shield themselves from Medicare cuts.
4.
Dominant distribution scale: Jefferies Research reports that Convatec's 180 Medical holds a dominant lead as the largest US continence distributor with a 40% market share. This leading position is followed by Hollister's ABC Medical and Coloplast's Comfort Medical, which remains a distant third with a 5% to 10% share. Consequently, this massive scale advantage positions Convatec to absorb industry-wide margin compression far more effectively than its smaller competitors.

Product and coding differentiation

5.
High premium mix: Coloplast has successfully converted its US intermittent catheter portfolio to premium products, with hydrophilic catheters accounting for 76% of its US catheter sales. This is significantly higher than the broader US market, where hydrophilic penetration remains low at approximately 40%. Convatec also lags behind Coloplast with hydrophilic products representing about 60% of its US intermittent catheter sales. This high-margin mix provides a resilient cushion against pricing pressure, as premium products typically maintain superior clinical preference over standard alternatives.
6.
Beneficial coding shift: The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services introduced three dedicated billing codes on January 1, 2026, to distinguish hydrophilic intermittent catheters from uncoated options. These new codes, A4295, A4296, and A4297, replace the shared codes A4351, A4352, and A4353 that previously offered no financial incentive for distributors to supply premium coated products. Consequently, this regulatory segregation will allow Medicare to establish separate bidding parameters for hydrophilic devices in 2028.
7.
Mitigated price pressure: The creation of a separate bidding category for hydrophilic catheters is expected to soften upcoming regulatory price pressure for premium manufacturers. Because hydrophilic catheters require advanced manufacturing and provide superior patient comfort, Medicare is expected to implement smaller reimbursement cuts for these codes than for standard uncoated catheters. This differentiation will safeguard gross margins for suppliers like Convatec that offer premium product lines like GentleCath Air. This product-level insulation will protect the overall average selling price of the continence division from catastrophic erosion.
8.
Contrary evidence: Bernstein Research suggests that the financial benefit of the 2026 catheter coding changes will build very gradually because the new codes initially provided no higher Medicare reimbursement for hydrophilic products over uncoated alternatives. Because distributors still face identical immediate reimbursement, they have limited economic incentives to proactively transition patients who are already comfortable using cheaper uncoated catheters in the community setting.

Own-brand substitution and consolidation

9.
Profitable brand capture: Convatec has aggressively increased the share of its own-manufactured continence products within its retail platform to 55% of the sales mix. This represents a substantial increase from less than 50% several years ago, allowing the company to capture double-margin economics. By shifting patients to its proprietary GentleCath range, Convatec can easily absorb distributor-level reimbursement cuts. This higher concentration of own-brand manufacturing acts as a robust financial buffer, protecting the company's operating margin even if the retail bidding process enforces steep pricing concessions.
10.
Defensive portfolio shifts: Coloplast plans to protect Comfort Medical's retail profitability by increasing its own- brand catheter mix by 5 percentage points in its base case. Management can also choose to discontinue or withdraw selected low-margin uncoated products if Medicare pricing becomes completely uneconomic. These defensive portfolio adjustments should help Coloplast contain group EBIT margin contraction to approximately 30 basis points during the competitive bidding round.
11.
Historical regulatory precedent: The implementation of the initial Medicare competitive bidding round in 2011 demonstrates that reimbursement cuts trigger severe industry consolidation. In earlier rounds for other medical equipment, the massive reduction in the number of contract suppliers allowed dominant distributors with nationwide reach to capture substantial displaced patient volumes. This historical precedent suggests that the 2028 bidding round will likely eliminate smaller independent distributors, leaving only a handful of scaled players like 180 Medical.
12.
Significant volume offset: Scaled manufacturers with vertically integrated retail operations are highly likely to neutralize competitive bidding pricing headwinds through market consolidation. While a projected 30% reimbursement cut will pressure gross margins, the exit of smaller distributors will allow scaled players to capture up to one-third of the tendered market. Therefore, Convatec and Coloplast will protect their long-term earnings per share through massive volume gains and a shift toward higher-margin, proprietary hydrophilic products. These consolidation gains will ultimately rebalance the competitive landscape in favor of high-quality, verticallyintegrated suppliers.

  1. Historical DMEPOS competitive bidding rounds reveal that retail-level reimbursement cuts of approximately 45% led to only partial pricing pass-through to manufacturers in consolidated market categories.
  2. Distributor consolidation during previous bidding rounds allowed vertically integrated medical manufacturers to successfully offset downward pricing pressure through significant volume gains and premium product substitution.

Section 4 – Commercial Payer and Medicare Advantage Adoption of SPAs

Section summary
  1. Private commercial insurers and Medicare Advantage plans historically follow public reimbursement cuts with a typical one to two year contract renegotiation lag.
  2. Vertically integrated manufacturers can mitigate immediate pricing pressure by unlinking managed care contracts and substituting low-margin products with proprietary premium brands.

Managed care pricing alignment

1.
Gradual policy alignment: Commercial payors often align their payment structures with Medicare policies over a multi-year timeframe. These private insurers typically adopt Medicare reimbursement guidelines with a one to two year lag because of contract renegotiation schedules. While not legally mandated to follow federal rate-setting, private payors frequently use public fee schedules as their baseline pricing benchmarks. This delayed alignment means that immediate Medicare rate cuts do not instantly compress commercial reimbursement margins.
2.
Lower baseline rates: Commercial payment rates for durable medical supplies are historically set below traditional Medicare fee-for-service schedules. Private plans typically reimburse at 70 to 80 percent of the established Medicare levels. Because these rates are established as a percentage of public benchmarks, any downward adjustment to the national fee schedule eventually exerts downward pressure on commercial contracts. This relationship reinforces Medicare's position as the primary pricing anchor for the entire intimate healthcare industry.
3.
Standardized benchmark rates: Managed care organizations and commercial payors consistently utilize public Medicare rates as their primary negotiation baseline. Health insurance directors report that private contracts typically pay 100 percent of Medicare as the standard industry benchmark. Payers with larger membership volumes leverage their scale to negotiate rates below this threshold. Consequently, any reduction in traditional Medicare fee-for-service rates directly compromises the baseline from which private contracts are negotiated.
4.
Widespread reimbursement tracking: Private insurers and Medicare Advantage plans tend to mimic the annual rate adjustments enacted by public programs. Commercial healthcare partners historically lock their annual reimbursement increases to traditional Medicare rate changes or flat percentage caps. This lockstep behavior prevents manufacturers from raising commercial prices to offset public sector margin compression. Consequently, the pricing pressure from public competitive bidding cycles quickly spills over into private contract discussions.

Contract unlinking and risk mitigation

5.
Defensive contract unlinking: Historical competitive bidding rounds forced durable medical equipment providers to restructure their managed care contracts. Prior to competitive bidding, most commercial contracts were linked to a percentage of Medicare rates. This linking exposed providers to immediate pricing pressure across both public and private channels when Medicare rates fell. Providers subsequently unlinked these managed care contracts to insulate their private revenues from federal bidding results.
6.
Delayed commercial renegotiation: Public reimbursement cuts create a strong incentive for private insurers to demand similar pricing concessions from medical device manufacturers. Private payors closely monitor Medicare cuts and move quickly together to renegotiate their wholesale purchasing agreements. This cascading pricing pressure typically mirrors the pattern of generic drug entry in pharmaceutical markets. Consequently, initial Medicare competitive bidding cuts represent the first step in a broader market-wide margin erosion cycle.
7.
Challenging private reimbursement: Medicare Advantage plans and commercial insurers utilize contracted rates and utilization controls to manage rising medical cost trends. Private payers represent roughly half of homecare provider revenues, making them key counterparties for reimbursement negotiations. Because managed care organizations face their own profitability pressures, they actively seek to squeeze homecare provider rates. This weak private reimbursement environment creates an additional cyclical headwind for medical device manufacturers.

Market resilience and competitive impact

8.
Elevated policy risk: The expansion of competitive bidding to ostomy and continence products threatens to disrupt the historical pricing premiums maintained over private payers. MedPAC reports that Medicare historically paid 18 to 57 percent more than private insurers for top non-bidded medical supplies. By establishing single payment amounts, Medicare seeks to eliminate this pricing spread and bring public rates in line with private contracts. This convergence will directly reduce the average selling prices that manufacturers can command in the US market.
9.
Direct retail headwinds: Vertical integration through retail channels exposes manufacturer margins directly to Medicare Advantage and commercial reimbursement pressure. While direct retail operations like 180 Medical provide market share security, they also heighten exposure to private payor rate cuts. In contrast, companies with higher wholesale mixes face indirect pressure as independent distributors demand wholesale discounts to preserve their retail margins. Therefore, the total addressable market's pricing resilience depends on the balance between retail and wholesale channels.
10.
Indirect payment influence: Government healthcare changes regularly influence the payment structures of private third-party payors who rely on Medicare guidelines. Commercial insurers frequently peg their payment systems to portions of Medicare payment rates to streamline administrative complexity. Because public reimbursement serves as the baseline, any legislative reduction in Medicare spending triggers a corresponding decline in private sector rates. This operational dependency exposes manufacturers to a secondary wave of margin compression as private plans adjust.
11.
Significant premium spreads: Commercial insurers historically pay substantially higher rates than public programs, creating a highly profitable channel for medical suppliers. Research indicates that traditional Medicare often pays as little as fifty percent of what commercial insurers allow. This wide pricing spread helps insulate manufacturer profitability from local public sector reimbursement shocks. However, as private payers optimize their networks, they are increasingly looking to close this premium gap to improve margins.
12.
Contrary evidence: Jefferies Research reported on January 14, 2022, that no direct correlation exists between public Medicare reimbursement trends and negotiated commercial payor rates. The vast majority of private healthcare contracts are separately negotiated without direct ties to public benchmarks. Consequently, commercial payor rates are expected to hold steady for the majority of healthcare volumes regardless of public cuts. This separation suggests that the pricing resilience of Coloplast and Convatec's private payor segments may remain fully intact.
13.
Significant pricing insulation: Vertically integrated manufacturers are highly likely to maintain pricing resilience because their private managed care contracts have been successfully unlinked from public Medicare schedules. By negotiating private commercial rates independently, Coloplast and Convatec can protect their managed care revenues from the immediate spillover of competitive bidding. This commercial insulation ensures that the retail bidding process does not immediately damage wholesale margins. Therefore, independent contracting strategies will successfully shield long-term earnings per share from sudden regulatory shocks.
14.
Other side of the story: Managed care organizations may aggressively accelerate their adoption of public single payment amounts to protect their own compressing margins. If Medicare Advantage plans enforce immediate rate alignment, Coloplast and Convatec could face an abrupt compression of private channel revenues. This accelerated pass-through would eliminate the timing buffer that manufacturers typically rely on to adjust operations. Consequently, the dual pressure of public and private rate cuts could trigger severe wholesale margin erosion.
