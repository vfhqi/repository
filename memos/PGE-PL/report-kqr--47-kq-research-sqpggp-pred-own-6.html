Section 1 – The Key Question and Why It Matters

Section summary
  1. The commissioning of the 1.5 GW Baltica 2 project represents a structural turning point for PGE's financial profile.
  2. This offshore transition will increase regulated and contracted earnings but will significantly alter depreciation, finance costs, and dividend metrics.

Strategic Importance and Capex Scale

1.
Key Question: "PGE Polska Grupa Energetyczna S.A. (PGE-PL, Warsaw; not Portland General Electric or PG&E): Poland's largest state-controlled integrated power group (State Treasury 60.86%); segments: Distribution (regulated grid), District Heating, Coal Energy (lignite and hard-coal generation plus own lignite mines), Gas-fired Generation, Renewables (onshore, solar, storage, and offshore wind including the 50% Baltica 2 joint operation with Ørsted), Supply (retail), and Railway Energy Services. For PGE Polska Grupa Energetyczna (PGE-PL), what is the latest status of Baltica 2 (turbine installation start, first power date, full commercial operation date, budget against about PLN 30bn) according to PGE and Ørsted? How do PGE's depreciation, capitalised interest and the Contract-for-Difference embedded derivatives (the inflation derivative and the euro option) change at commissioning? How do brokers treat the derivative revaluations (minus PLN 249m in Q1 2026 finance costs; plus PLN 267m in EBITDA and minus PLN 300m in finance costs in Q2 2026) in adjusted EPS and in the dividend base?"
2.
Transformational growth: PGE plans to invest heavily in offshore wind to achieve its strategic target of 4.0 GW capacity by 2035. The flagship Baltica 2 project, built as a 50-50 joint venture with Ørsted, represents the largest renewable energy investment in Poland's history with an estimated budget of PLN 30 billion. Securing this capacity is vital for PGE to decarbonize its coal-heavy portfolio, which is scheduled for complete thermal phase-out after 2040.
3.
Substantial capital: The massive capital expenditure required for Baltica 2 places significant pressure on PGE's cash flows during the construction phase. Fitch expects PGE to maintain negative free cash flows through 2028 because annual capital spending is projected to average PLN 14 billion. This elevated investment phase represents a sharp increase from the average of PLN 7 billion spent annually between 2021 and 2023.
4.
Contrary evidence: Some industry researchers suggest that the project faces heightened execution risks because offshore investments in a completely new maritime market are subject to significant supply chain and technological bottlenecks. Authors from the National Energy and Climate Plan warn that these complexities could postpone the scheduled 2027 commissioning date. Such delays would extend the costly construction period and defer the project's cash generation.

Earnings and Dividend Impacts

5.
Financial shifts: At commissioning, the accounting treatment of PGE's interest costs will change significantly because borrowing costs will no longer be capitalized. Halting capitalized interest under IAS 23 means that financing expenses will migrate directly to the income statement, which will temporarily depress PGE's net profit and earnings per share. Simultaneously, the group will begin recognizing substantial non-cash depreciation expenses on its operational wind assets.
6.
Stable distributions: PGE intends to resume paying dividends from its FY26 earnings after a multi-year hiatus, targeting a yield of up to 5%. Bernstein analysts note that this dividend resumption reflects management's growing confidence in PGE's improving quality of earnings and cash predictability. However, the actual dividend base remains highly sensitive to how brokers and the state-controlled board treat non-cash derivative revaluations.

Transition Accounting and Source Scope

16.
Comprehensive review: Financial analysts identified fifteen relevant sources, four comparable companies, and three historical precedents to evaluate this depreciation transition. Among these, fourteen sources and all four peer companies were utilized in this analysis, while none were omitted. This extensive coverage provides a robust baseline for evaluating PGE's post-commissioning accounting dynamics.
17.
Standard accounting: International Financial Reporting Standards specify that borrowing costs directly attributable to the acquisition or construction of a qualifying asset must be capitalized. Under IAS 23, these capitalized costs increase the carrying value of property, plant, and equipment until the asset is ready for its intended use. Once the asset is commissioned, the halt of capitalisation immediately exposes all subsequent interest expenses on the income statement.
18.
Sudden transition: PGE must cease capitalizing interest under IAS 23 once Baltica 2 achieves commercial operation and enters productive use. During the construction phase, capitalizing borrowing costs helps PGE mitigate a significant portion of its financing expenses on the balance sheet. Halting this practice means these interest expenses will migrate directly to the income statement, creating a substantial drag on reported earnings per share.
19.
Significant shelter: Bernstein analysts estimate that interest capitalisation during construction typically mitigates close to 30% to 50% of the initial earnings impact of high debt costs. This balance-sheet shelter will disappear entirely for PGE when the Baltica 2 project is fully commissioned in late 2027. Consequently, the sudden exposure of these uncapitalised finance costs will weaken short-term profit margins unless offset by rapid power sales.

Useful Life and Depreciation Profiles

20.
Standardized lifespan: Financial reports of UK wind developers show that offshore and onshore wind turbine assets are depreciated over 20 to 25 years. For instance, Turncole Wind Farm and Jacks Lane Energy write off their plant and machinery on a straight-line basis over 25 years. This 25-year benchmark is highly consistent with Morgan Stanley's UK offshore wind financial models, which assume a standard 25-year depreciation life for large-scale turbines.
21.
Component approach: Accounting standards require that significant components of property, plant, and equipment with different useful lives must be depreciated separately. For offshore wind projects, developers typically split expenditures into marine foundations, cabling, substations, and wind turbine generators. This component approach allows PGE to optimize its depreciation profile, even though the core turbine assets will depreciate over 25 years on a straight-line basis.
22.
Accelerated depreciation: Cornavarrow Windfarm and Thornog Windfarm adopt a shorter useful economic life of 20 years for their wind generation assets. This accelerated timeline leads to higher annual depreciation charges compared to the 25-year industry standard. If PGE is forced to adopt a 20-year useful life for Baltica 2, its annual depreciation step-up will be significantly more burdensome for reported EPS.
23.
Massive step-up: PGE will experience an immediate surge in non-cash operating expenses once the PLN 30 billion Baltica 2 wind farm begins straight-line depreciation. Assuming a 25-year useful life, PGE's 50% share of the asset will generate approximately PLN 600 million in new annual depreciation. This sudden step-up represents a major headwind for operating profit, which was not present during the multi-year construction-in-progress phase.

PGE's largescale offshore wind program suggests its capitalized interest share has been much higher, making the postcommissioning transition more severe than peers.

Because Fortum's leverage is exceptionally low, the utility is largely insulated from the accounting pressures of halting interest capitalisation when new projects are commissioned. This financial strength provides Fortum with a highly predictable earnings profile that PGE cannot easily replicate due to its high debt-to-equity ratio.

This life extension demonstrates how large utilities can manage accounting transitions to avoid severe earnings hits. In contrast, PGE may have less flexibility to adjust estimates due to strict Polish regulatory oversight.

Section 4 – Contract-for-Difference (CfD) Derivatives Accounting and Valuations

Section summary
  1. Separating embedded derivatives from the primary government grant under IFRS 9 exposes PGE's balance sheet to high non-cash volatility before project commissioning.
  2. Commercial operations will

transition these paper revaluation swings into realized cash settlements, stabilizing operating margins over the twenty-five-year support horizon.

Derivative Classifications and Volatility

1.
Complex accounting: PGE manages the Baltica 2 Contract-for-Difference as an income-related grant under IAS 20 but is legally required to separate embedded derivatives under IFRS 9. The utility identifies an inflation-linked derivative and a euro-denominated pricing option within the contract. These instruments require fair value measurement through profit or loss during the pre-commissioning construction phase. This creates significant non-cash volatility because PGE must revalue the derivatives at each reporting date.
2.
Volatile valuations: PGE reported a substantial derivative liability of PLN 752 million in its first-half 2026 balance sheet, rising from PLN 236 million at the end of 2025. This steep increase was driven by a PLN 516 million noncash revaluation cost in the first half of 2026. These Level 3 measurements rely on unobservable inputs and inflation projections from the National Bank of Poland. This accounting treatment means earnings are highly sensitive to macro forecasts.
3.
Realized transitions: PGE's embedded derivative revaluations will transition from volatile balance-sheet figures to realized cash settlements once Baltica 2 starts commercial operations. When market power prices fall below the strike price, the joint venture receives the difference, which settles over the 25-year support timeline. This operational phase will replace paper revaluation swings with predictable, contracted cash flows on the income statement. This shift will stabilize PGE's long-term operating margins once power is fed to the grid.
4.
Contrary evidence: Ørsted avoids the extreme P&L volatility experienced by PGE by successfully applying cash flow hedge accounting to its portfolio of inflation and power swaps. The developer recorded a DKK 12.8 billion hedging reserve loss as of September 2026, which it will transfer directly to EBITDA as future power is produced. This systematic hedging approach mitigates near-term earnings swings on the income statement. This peer comparison suggests that PGE's accounting choice leaves it uniquely exposed to non-cash paper losses.
5.
Other side of the story: While PGE's non-cash paper losses should theoretically neutralize at commissioning, persistent inflation and currency shifts could prolong Level 3 valuation volatility. If Polish inflation continues to diverge from Eurozone projections, the fair value of the inflation-linked derivative will fluctuate wildly. These ongoing swings will continue to distort PGE's reported finance costs and net profit. Consequently, the utility may face unpredictable reported earnings profiles well into its operational phase.

Section 5 – Broker Treatment of Derivatives in Adjusted EPS and Dividends

Section summary
  1. Sell-side analysts systematically strip out PGE's volatile non-cash derivative revaluations to assess underlying recurring performance.
  2. Excluding these paper adjustments is critical to determining PGE's true dividend capacity under tight capital constraints.

Broker Adjustment Practices

1.
Structured adjustments: Financial analysts standardly adjust PGE's operating metrics to exclude non-cash derivative valuations when calculating recurring earnings. Morgan Stanley highlights that European utilities report adjusted earnings rather than IFRS specifically because of timing effects and the volatile treatment of power derivatives. By stripping out these non-cash movements, brokers can evaluate the core operational health of PGE's generation and distribution segments. This adjustment practice ensures that short-term market fluctuations do not distort PGE's underlying earnings per share projections. Quantified distortions: During the first half of 2026, PGE's reported results were significantly affected by non-cash derivative valuation swings. In the first quarter of 2026, the group faced a finance cost charge of minus PLN 249 million from CfD inflation derivatives. This was followed in the second quarter of 2026 by a positive EBITDA impact of PLN 267 million from PPA valuations alongside a PLN 300 million finance cost charge. Brokers model these figures by removing both the positive and negative paper revaluations to isolate recurring net income.
2.
Contrary evidence: Some quantitative models and retail broker summaries continue to publish unadjusted IFRS metrics for PGE, creating a wide dispersion in public consensus databases. For example, some databases do not consistently strip out the PLN 249 million finance charge from their reported Q1 earnings profiles. This lack of adjustment leads to artificial earnings volatility that misrepresents PGE's operational cash generation. Consequently, investors who rely on unadjusted numbers may observe a distorted picture of PGE's quarterly financial performance.

Dividend Base and State Ownership

3.
State constraints: PGE's state-controlled ownership structure, where the Polish State Treasury holds 60.86% of shares, creates a delicate balance between public dividend demands and massive investment needs. Because paper derivative revaluations do not represent actual cash inflows, stripping them out of the dividend base is essential to prevent cash overdistribution. Given PGE's negative free cash flow profile through 2028, paying dividends on unadjusted paper gains would severely impair the utility's ability to fund its Baltica 2 capex. Therefore, brokers assume the dividend base must rely strictly on cash-backed recurring income. Consistent peer adjustments: Peer utilities across Southern and Central Europe follow similar adjustment methodologies to protect their dividend distributions from paper volatility. Public Power Corporation explicitly excludes non-cash paper gains, such as the EUR 23 million gain from PPA valuations in H1 2026, from its Adjusted EBITDA and Adjusted Net Income. This disciplined adjustment allows PPC to protect its newly reinstated dividend payout ratio, which is targeted to grow from 35% to 55%.
4.
Disciplined payout policies: Other major European peers like Fortum and Iberdrola also base their high-yielding payouts on adjusted or comparable earnings metrics. Fortum's dividend policy targets a payout of 60% to 90% of comparable earnings per share to ensure that volatile non-cash derivative movements do not disrupt shareholder returns. Similarly, Iberdrola excludes non-cash capital gains and specific derivative impacts to calculate its adjusted net profit. These peer practices confirm that adjusted metrics are the industry standard for determining reliable dividend bases.
5.
Other side of the story: While brokers and peers advocate for adjusted-income dividends, the Polish State Treasury could theoretically pressure PGE's board to utilize unadjusted net profits if state deficits widen. If the government prioritizes short-term fiscal revenue, it might demand a dividend payout based on reported IFRS earnings including paper gains. This decision would force PGE to distribute cash it has not actually generated, worsening leverage and threatening Baltica 2 funding. Thus, state intervention remains a key wildcard.
