Section 2 – Cost Base Structure and Regional Volume Drop-Through

Section summary
  1. Kerry Group's cost base is heavily dominated by variable raw materials, creating moderate operating leverage that is highly dependent on high-margin regional drop-through.

Kerry Cost Base Analysis

1.
Structural review: Kerry Group reported substantial expenditures by nature in its annual financial report for the fiscal year of 2025. The company recorded raw materials and consumables of EUR 3,263.70 mm, staff costs of EUR 1,255.10 mm, and other general overheads of EUR 1,005.10 mm. This represents a total cost base of EUR 5,523.90 mm (calculated) from these primary categories. Management notes that these lines are heavily optimized to support ongoing margin development across regions.
2.
Variable split: Kerry Group does not explicitly identify these P&L cost lines as strictly fixed or variable within its annual financial report. Independent financial analysts typically treat raw materials as fully variable, while factory labor and general overheads carry significant fixed elements. The high proportion of raw materials means the overall business model carries moderate operating leverage. Under this framework, volume fluctuations directly impact plant utilization and fixed-cost absorption.
3.
Contrary evidence: Kerry Group might face weaker operating leverage than expected due to its production-to-order manufacturing model. This customized strategy operates with near-zero inventory levels, which inherently reduces operational efficiency compared to peers. Former commercial personnel believe that producing without seasonal stock prevents the company from maximizing its plant capacity utilization. This inefficiency could ultimately cap the margin benefits usually expected from volume growth.

Section 3 – Historical Margin Movements Across Macro Cycles

Section summary
  1. Kerry Group and its peers experienced margin compression in 2021-2022 due to inflation, followed by mixed performance during the 2023 customer destocking and a slow recovery in 2024-2026.
  2. Specialty ingredients players like Givaudan and Novonesis maintained high pricing resilience, whereas more commoditized processors suffered significant fixed-cost under-absorption during down-cycles.

Hyper-Inflation Headwinds in 2021-2022

1.
Inflated cost pressures: Kerry Group managed unprecedented inflation in fiscal year 2022 through strategic pricing increases of 11.7%. However, the mathematical pass-through of these costs compressed the group's reported EBITDA margin by 120 basis points to 13.9%. This pricing action protected absolute cash margins but diluted the reported operating margins of the core Taste and Nutrition division.
2.
Diluted profit margins: Tate and Lyle experienced gross cost inflation totaling GBP 85 mm in corn, energy, and logistics during the first half of fiscal year 2023. Although strategic pricing recovered these input costs, the division suffered margin pressure before contracts were renewed. This lag demonstrates how pricing delays temporarily compress profitability during hyper-inflationary phases.
3.
Substantial margin dilution: Symrise AG experienced severe raw material and energy inflation during fiscal year 2022, driving its raw material quota from 43.4% to 45.5%. The company reported a Taste, Nutrition and Health EBITDA margin of 21.6%, representing a year-on-year contraction of 110 basis points. This drop shows how rapid input cost inflation outpaced early price increases.
4.
Severe gross contraction: Givaudan SA suffered input cost inflation of CHF 360 mm in fiscal year 2022, with raw materials rising by 10%. The company's gross profit margin contracted from 42.7% in 2021 to 38.8% as pricing actions carried a delayed timing effect. To defend its bottom line, management maintained strict expense discipline to protect its comparable EBITDA margin of 20.9% in its Taste and Wellbeing division.
5.
Resilient gross margins: Novonesis reported an adjusted gross margin of 54.6% in fiscal year 2022 despite paying significantly higher prices for energy and soft commodities. Its EBIT margin before special items came in at 26.4%, which was only 40 basis points lower than the previous period. This performance proves that biosolutions players maintain superior pricing power during inflationary cycles compared to standard food processors.
6.
Contrary evidence: Tate and Lyle experienced an unexpected expansion of 60 basis points in its operating margin during the first half of fiscal year 2023. The company achieved this margin expansion through early contract renewals and a rapid program of supplementary price increases. This outcome contradicts the common industry view that inflation inevitably compresses operating margins before cost recovery is completed.

Reinvestment: R&D Step-up and Margin Trade-offs Medi um Kerry plans to raise R&D spending to 5-6% of revenue by 2030 from ~4.6% in 2025; Deutsche Bank attributes half of Q3 2026 margin deceleration to increasing R&D spend; Kerry's production-to-order, low-inventory model minimizes storage costs but affects utilization [23 3] [23 4] The evidence base reveals several critical gaps that prevent a definitive answer to whether Kerry can achieve its 20-21% margin target by 2030 through operating leverage alone or requires exceptional volume outperformance. First, internal plant utilization rates by region and facility remain completely undisclosed in public filings. Kerry's production-to-order model operates with near-zero inventory, which inherently reduces operational efficiency and prevents maximizing plant capacity utilization, yet the company provides no quantitative disclosure of actual utilization percentages across its 119 manufacturing sites. This information gap is particularly critical because continuous operations at peers like Tate & Lyle are highly sensitive to plant capacity utilization rates, and any demand drop leads to immediate fixed-cost under-absorption. To close this gap, investors require expert interviews with former plant managers who can quantify typical utilization bands (e.g., 70-85% for customized flavors vs. 90-95% for standardized ingredients) and detail how Kerry's operational planning handles demand volatility. Additionally, quarterly investor presentations should include a standardized disclosure of group-level capacity utilization alongside regional volume metrics to allow proper assessment of fixed-cost absorption trends.

Second, the split between fixed and variable costs within staff and overhead lines lacks precision. Kerry does not explicitly identify P&L cost lines as fixed or variable, and analysts typically treat raw materials as fully variable while factory labor and overheads carry significant fixed elements. However, the proportion of direct production labor (variable with volume) versus engineering, maintenance, and supervision (largely fixed) is never quantified. This ambiguity makes it impossible to model the true operating leverage coefficient with confidence. The solution requires Kerry to provide a supplemental cost accounting disclosure in its annual report, breaking down staff costs into volume-variable direct labor and fixed support functions, similar to the granularity provided by certain US-listed industrials. Alternatively, sell-side analysts could conduct detailed primary research interviews with former CFOs or FP&A directors who can reconstruct the cost structure using internal management accounting frameworks.

Third, regional variable cost structures remain opaque, preventing accurate modeling of segment-level dropthrough rates. The Americas segment achieves ~20% EBITDA margin, more than 300bps higher than Europe, and generates over 60% of group profits, making growth in this region highly accretive, yet Kerry discloses neither the raw material intensity nor the labor cost per unit across regions. This gap is material because Tate & Lyle's Americas segment delivered 25.7% margin while EMEA fell to 11.7% due to energy cost inflation, showing that regional cost bases diverge dramatically.

Investors need regulatory filings that break out cost of goods sold by segment or, at minimum, disclose the percentage of group raw material spend sourced in each region. In the absence of public disclosure, expert network calls with former regional supply chain directors can triangulate typical variable cost ratios and identify structural advantages (e.g., proximity to US corn belt, European labor regulations).

Fourth, the net EBITDA impact of R&D step-up is poorly quantified in forward guidance. Deutsche Bank reports that accelerated R&D investments directly offset operational savings from Accelerate 2.0, yet the precise absorption rate–whether 25%, 50%, or 75% of the EUR 100m savings–remains unclear. Management commentary has been qualitative rather than quantitative, stating only that R&D will rise as a percentage of sales without modeling the absolute EUR spending trajectory or confirming whether these costs sit in cost of sales or operating expenses.

To resolve this ambiguity, Kerry must publish a multi-year bridge in its investor day presentations, showing Accelerate 2.0 savings gross (EUR 100m), R&D step-up cost (EUR XX m), and net EBITDA benefit (EUR YY m) by year through 2028. Absent this disclosure, investors should seek expert transcripts from former innovation directors who can estimate the incremental headcount, lab equipment, and application trials required to move from 4.6% to 5-6% intensity. Fifth, pricing elasticity and customer contract pass-through terms are never explicitly detailed. Elevated raw material pass-through agreements dilute operating margins during inflation, as contract structures protect absolute EBITDA but compress margin percentages when pricing rises, yet Kerry does not disclose what percentage of its revenue operates under formula-based pricing versus negotiated fixed contracts.

This distinction determines whether margin expansion comes from true operating leverage or merely from favorable input cost deflation. Closing this gap requires expert interviews with former commercial directors who managed contract renewals, supplemented by analysis of peer disclosures from IFF or Givaudan that provide more transparency on pricing mechanisms. Sixth, the timing and cadence of factory closures under Accelerate 2.0 lacks specificity.

Kerry reduced sites from 124 to 119 in 2025 and plans a further 10% reduction, but the company has not published a facility-by-facility closure schedule or identified which geographic clusters will be consolidated. This uncertainty creates execution risk because rapid factory consolidation carries significant transitional risks that can disrupt customer relationships and shift focus away from top-line growth. Investors need quarterly earnings transcripts to include management commentary on closure milestones and confirmation that customer transfers are proceeding without volume attrition.

Additionally, expert calls with former operations VPs can provide historical context on Kerry's past restructuring timelines and typical margin payback periods.

- Earnings Calls Finally, competitive M&A and biotechnology positioning remain under-analyzed. Rothschild Redburn warns that Kerry must execute bolt-on M&A in biotics and enzymes while managing higher R&D spend to bridge its margin gap, yet no detailed pipeline of acquisition targets or integration cost assumptions has been disclosed. Understanding whether Kerry can acquire its way into higher-margin biotechnology at reasonable multiples requires analysis of recent transaction comparables (e.g., DSM-Firmenich's acquisition strategy, Novonesis pricing) and expert perspectives from investment bankers or former corporate development officers within the flavor and fragrance sector.

Future M&A announcements and integration updates in quarterly filings will be critical to assess whether Kerry can supplement organic leverage with inorganic margin accretion. In summary, while the evidence provides strong directional confidence that Kerry possesses moderate-to-good operating leverage and a credible EUR 100m cost program, the lack of granular cost accounting, utilization metrics, regional variable splits, and R&D-to-EBITDA phasing prevents a definitive quantitative answer.

Closing these gaps requires a combination of enhanced company disclosure (cost structure supplements, facility roadmaps, R&D bridges), targeted expert primary research (former plant managers, CFOs, commercial directors), and disciplined tracking of quarterly operating metrics and competitor benchmarking over the next 12-18 months.

Section 6 – What Is Known and What Is Not

The overall confidence level in answering the Key Question regarding Kerry Group's fixed-cost operating leverage and cost reduction potential can be characterized as medium to high. The evidence demonstrates that Kerry benefits from highly attractive incremental drop-through margins, with incremental Americas volumes dropping through at approximately double the group EBITDA margins, generating a 40 basis points tailwind to group margins for every 1% volume growth, and the Accelerate 2.0 initiative is expected to deliver annualized recurring savings of EUR 100m by 2028. However, significant uncertainty remains regarding the interaction between volume leverage, cost savings, and strategic R&D reinvestment, particularly given Kerry's planned rise in R&D spending to 5-6% of revenue by 2030 from approximately 4.6% in 2025. The evidence provides a robust foundation for understanding the structural operating model but leaves critical gaps in granular cost accounting, regional plant utilization dynamics, and the precise phasing of margin benefits net of reinvestment.
