Section 2 – Grey Cement Price Trends and Carbon Cost Pass-Through

Section summary
  1. European grey cement prices in 2026 show positive structural trajectory driven by carbon regulatory pass-through despite persistent volume weakness.
  2. Producers successfully differentiate pricing levers by combining base price indexations with specific energy and carbon surcharges.
  3. Spain and Italy lead southern European cement price inflation while the German and French markets face near-term volatility.

Geographic cement pricing metrics in 2026

Section summary

Proposals

Commission legislative reform proposals

1.
Shallowing emissions cap: The European Commission's reform proposal of July 17, 2026, aims to adjust the emissions trajectory post-2030. According to Deutsche Bank's report of 19 October 2026, the Commission proposes to lower the linear annual reduction factor from 4.4% to 3.7% for the 2031-2035 period and further down to 1.7% starting in 2036. This gradual decline means that the total supply of carbon allowances will extend well into the 2040s rather than hitting a hard zero in 2039. This change will reduce the immediate compliance burden on industrial energy users and protect long-term earnings per share.
2.
Extended free allocations: The Commission's July 2026 proposal offers substantial transition relief by delaying the final phase-out of free allocations. Morgan Stanley's report of 17 July 2026 highlights that the free allocation phase-out for CBAM-covered sectors has been extended from 2034 to 2038. Under this revised timeline, free allocations will stand at 59% in 2030 and drop to 15% between 2034 and 2037 before declining to zero in 2038. This extended cushion helps domestic cement producers smooth their transition costs and sustain profitability.

Strict investment conditionality: The receipt of free allowances under the new proposal is strictly conditional on corporate green spending. Deutsche Bank's research of 19 October 2026 notes that companies will only receive 80% of their free allocations upfront upon submitting an approved investment plan for climatefriendly transformation. Critical gaps in pricing and import data

Unverified import data: The actual impact of the border carbon tariff on clinker imports remains unverified. This is because official declarations under the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism covering the 2026 import period are not due until late 2027. Consequently, analysts must rely on incomplete trade estimates and expert surveys rather than confirmed administrative logs. Securing early regional customs registry filings would fully close this critical information gap before the formal declarations.
2.
Inexact pricing attribution: The precise share of cement price increases driven by carbon compliance remains highly contested. Jefferies Research highlights that continental price indices continue to show mixed trends across France and Spain. However, current disclosures do not systematically isolate carbon surcharges from underlying logistics or energy cost recovery. Reviewing future audited product invoices would isolate the exact carbon cost pass-through from temporary fuel inflation.

Hidden allowance transactions: Off-market transfers of emission allowances between regional cement producers remain entirely opaque. Bernstein Research warns that corporate stockpile models cannot track private transactions or plant acquisitions. Consequently, estimated depletion horizons could shorten significantly if companies liquidate reserves. Accessing the official European Union Registry transaction logs would verify private carbon trading activities and clarify actual corporate compliance buffers.

Depressed carbon asset values: A soft-law scenario with prolonged free allowances would suppress future carbon prices and strip value from banked corporate stockpiles. This correction would directly devalue the massive carbon reserves held by players like Vicat. Unless market volumes recover, the implied value of corporate stockpiles would shrink, removing a critical balance sheet cushion for investors.

Lower cement price growth: If political pressure dilutes carbon regulations, the compound annual growth rate of domestic grey cement prices will fall far short of consensus targets. Under these conditions, domestic producers would fail to recover their underlying cost inflation, leading to persistent profit margin contraction.
5.
Fragile operating profits: The sensitivity of domestic cement earnings to even minor pricing weakness is high because of flat lining regional demand and rising base costs. Deutsche Bank Research modeled on 20 March 2026 that a mere 3% drop in European cement prices would trigger a 4% decline in group EBIT. This drop would propagate into a five percent contraction in reported earnings per share. This high operational leverage means that minor price concessions quickly destroy shareholder returns, unless producers implement aggressive localized cost-cutting programs.
6.
Revived volume battles: Lower carbon prices would change the underlying economics of local cement markets and incentivize producers to pursue market share over price discipline. Rothschild & Co Redburn argued on 3 March 2026 that a drop in carbon prices to €35 would make marginal volume sales highly profitable. Consequently, manufacturers would be financially incentivized to cut cement prices by up to 10% to capture extra tons. This change in competitive behavior would shatter the industry's cooperative pricing paradigm and trigger severe price wars across central Europe.
