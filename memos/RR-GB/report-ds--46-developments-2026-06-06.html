2.
Oil price – massive relief: Crude oil benchmarks plummeted by over 10 percent immediately following the ceasefire, crushing the bear thesis that sustained 184 dollar jet fuel prices would cripple airline balance sheets
3.
Traffic rebound – rapid normalisation: By late April, the company confirmed that Trent XWB engine flying hours in the Middle East had already fully recovered to pre-conflict levels, entirely destroying the sell-side bear case

First-quarter trading update delivery

4.
Flying hours – expectation-beating: First-quarter large engine flying hours printed at 115 percent of 2019 levels, sitting at the bottom of published guidance but massively outperforming the degraded buy-side whisper
5.

6.
Shop visits – predictable execution: Large engine shop visits increased by 12 percent, proving that the long-term service agreement model effectively locked in maintenance schedules despite macro volatility
7.

Section 4 – Peer and sector context on the same events 6 findings

Section summary
  1. Rolls-Royce demonstrated superior resilience compared to narrowbody-exposed peers like MTU Aero Engines, shielded by its long-term service agreement model that minimises airline cash-conservation risks.
1.
Sector correlation – broad sell-off: Across the commercial aerospace sector, European peers including Airbus, Safran, and MTU Aero Engines universally suffered 15 to 20 percent drawdowns during the initial conflict outbreak
2.
Business model – unique protection: Unlike peers reliant on time-and-materials maintenance, Rolls-Royce generates 90 percent of its engine revenues from long-term service agreements, meaning airlines cannot easily conserve cash by delaying shop visits
3.
Flight data – tracked divergence: FlightRadar24 tracking data revealed that by April, Rolls-Royce's value-weighted flying hours were up 1 percent year-on-year, while MTU's value-weighted hours had contracted by 4 percent
4.
Widebody resilience – structural advantage: The vast majority of airline capacity reductions announced year-to-date were concentrated in the narrowbody segment, directly shielding Rolls-Royce's predominantly widebody portfolio
5.
Retirement risk – peer contrast: While Safran faced risks from the accelerated retirement of older CFM56 aircraft, Rolls-Royce confirmed no change in the retirement profile of its Trent 700 fleet, which remains contracted into the 2030s
6.
Other side of the story: The alternative read is that MTU and Safran are actually better positioned for the long term because narrowbody domestic routes historically recover much faster than the international widebody routes Rolls-Royce relies upon
