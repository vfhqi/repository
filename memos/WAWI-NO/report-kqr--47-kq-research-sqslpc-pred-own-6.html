Historical Suez and Ever Given Disruptions

1.
Prolonged closure: Suez Canal transits ceased entirely during the eight-year shutdown from 1967 to 1975 following the Six-Day War. This extensive geopolitical disruption forced shipowners to build massive supertankers designed specifically to permanently adapt to longer routes around Africa. Consequently, the canal lost substantial leverage because global trade patterns restructured during the long closure. This historical precedent indicates that long-term rerouting can permanently lower Suez utilization and alter the cost base of deep-sea operators.
2.
Structural realignment: LNG shipping patterns shifted drastically after Suez Canal transits became restricted, echoing the historical 1967-1975 disruptions. By 2025, approximately 88% of United States liquefied natural gas exports to Asia utilized the Cape route, up from nearly zero after January 2024. This dramatic pivot shows that energy carriers are highly willing to sustain longer transit times indefinitely when chokepoint security is compromised. For car carriers, this suggests that the return of fleets to the Red Sea will similarly face severe operational lags as contracts are renegotiated.
3.
Brief disruption: The Suez Canal blockage of March 2021 demonstrated that even a short six-day closure triggers severe global logistics congestion. This brief grounding of the Ever Given held up approximately USD 9.60 bn of daily trade between Europe and Asia. Although salvage teams refloated the vessel quickly, the sudden bottleneck removed substantial effective vessel supply from the global market. This capacity contraction drove ocean spot rates up, showing how rapidly short-term freight rates react to chokepoint blockages.

Port Congestion and Delayed Network Recovery

4.
Delayed recovery: Maersk Line reported that 50 of its vessels were delayed during the March 2021 Suez Canal blockage, causing widespread schedule chaos. Morgan Stanley highlighted that the resulting port congestion and vessel delays lingered into the third quarter of 2021, keeping capacity artificially tight. This prolonged disruption supported shipping line earnings well past the initial reopening, proving that network recovery lags are substantial. Consequently, ocean carriers can expect elevated freight rates to persist for several months after any initial political settlement is reached.
5.
Capacity absorption: Container spot rates surged by 10% immediately after the Ever Given was freed on March 29, 2021. Jefferies noted that the blockage temporarily reduced Asia-Europe container capacity by approximately 25%, compounding existing equipment shortages. Shippers were forced onto alternate routes, which extended voyage turnaround times and drove freight rates to record highs during the quarter. This response shows that even short chokepoint closures have a highly non-linear impact on effective global fleet capacity.
6.
Elevated risk: Marine insurers designated the Gulf of Aden as a war risk zone in May 2008 due to escalating Somalia piracy. This classification caused war risk premiums to rise significantly, heavily increasing the operating costs of transiting tankers. Nordic Shipholding noted that piracy remained a major threat to seafarer safety and merchant operations throughout the region. Shippers were forced to choose between paying exorbitant insurance premiums or undertaking the lengthy detour around the Cape of Good Hope.

Route efficiency and transit savings

1.
Substantial route savings: The maritime transit between Singapore and Rotterdam highlights the immense efficiency gains from reopening the Suez Canal route. Maybank Investment Banking Group reported on March 26, 2026, that sailing via Suez spans 8,288nm compared to 11,755nm around the Cape of Good Hope, saving 3,467nm one-way. This detour adds approximately 8,500nm on a round-trip voyage, which equates to ten extra transit days for vessel operators. These additional sailing days have artificially absorbed significant global fleet capacity, keeping utilization rates extremely elevated.
2.
High capacity absorption: Container lines and car carriers experience differing levels of operational disruption and capacity absorption from the Cape detour. Morgan Stanley MUFG reported on July 6, 2026, that Cape of Good Hope route diversions absorb approximately 2.30m TEU. This persistent diversion has tightened global freight markets because it removes roughly 7% of global shipping capacity. Returning to normal Suez operations would reverse this trend and release significant effective capacity.
3.
Significant capacity release: Quantitative modeling shows that resuming transits through the Suez Canal will directly expand effective vessel supply across different shipping segments. Morgan Stanley MUFG reported on November 25, 2025, that the supply impact of a full return is estimated at roughly 10% for container ships. For the specialized pure car and truck carrier segment, the same return is projected to release approximately 5% of effective capacity. This suggests that the 4-7% capacity release claim is the most realistic estimate for car carriers.

Spot to Contract Transmission Lags

1.
Delayed transmission: Global shipping networks exhibit a significant lag when spot market corrections translate into long-term freight agreements. Bernstein Research noted on October 14, 2026, that contract rates historically absorb only about half of the gap between spot and contract prices over a 12-month period. This pricing lag means that a sudden drop in spot rates does not immediately damage the average yields of deeply contracted operators like Wallenius Wilhelmsen. Consequently, corporate earnings per share remain highly insulated from immediate routing normalizations.
2.
Significant divergence: Container freight indices demonstrate how spot volatility behaves differently from contract rates during chokepoint normalizations. Deutsche Bank Research reported on October 29, 2026, that the spot-focused Shanghai Containerized Freight Index is narrower and more volatile than the China Containerized Freight Index, which includes long-term contracts. While spot indices fall rapidly as Suez transits resume, the contract-heavy index remains far more stable. This structural pricing difference ensures that ocean carriers with high contract coverage preserve their average container yields.
3.
Contrary evidence: Some analysts argue that rapid route normalizations can trigger an abrupt collapse in spot freight rates that immediately impacts operator margins. Morgan Stanley reported on October 5, 2026, that weekly container transits through the Suez Canal reached 54% of pre-disruption levels, which immediately weakened Asia-Europe spot rates by 16.8% month-on-week. This swift capacity release suggests that a synchronized return of car carriers would rapidly erode spot pricing power. If this occurs, spot rate declines will quickly transmit to short-term charter fixtures.

Structural Pricing and Contract Rigidity

4.
High contract defense: Pure car and truck carriers maintain high contract coverage to protect their average net freight rates from sudden spot market corrections. Höegh Autoliners reported on April 15, 2026, that approximately 85% of its revenues are derived from contracts with an average duration of 2.9 years. Similarly, Wallenius Wilhelmsen reported a weighted contract duration of 2.8 years, locking in an average net rate of USD 54 per cbm. These long-term contract structures will delay the negative earnings impact of any Suez Canal route normalization by several quarters.
5.
Stubbornly elevated: Global car carrier charter rates have reached historic heights because finished vehicle exports from China continue to surge. NH Investment and Securities reported on October 12, 2026, that oneyear charter rates for 6,500 CEU vessels rose to USD 95,000 per day. This rate matches the peak of the 2022- 2023 upcycle and keeps vessel operating costs elevated for operators relying on short-term charters. This tight capacity means a Suez reopening would be unlikely to trigger an immediate rate collapse because vessel demand remains structurally undersupplied.
6.
Rigid adjustments: Bilateral renegotiation processes prevent shipping lines from unilaterally adjusting contract freight rates during mid-contract route normalizations. Global Ship Lease explained on April 24, 2025, that shipping agreements are bilateral take-or-pay contracts that can only be renegotiated when there is a mutual benefit. Shippers using these agreements are protected from spot market volatility, while tonnage providers enjoy predictable cash flows. This structural rigidity means that even after Suez routes reopen, elevated contractual yields will persist for several years until renewals occur.
7.
side of the story: High contract coverage protects carriers from near-term spot drops, but operators face severe renewal risk during prolonged market downturns. Frontline PLC warned on November 6, 2026, that charterers have incentives to default or renegotiate when spot rates fall far below fixed charter rates. Rechartering vessels at lower rates would cause corporate earnings to decline substantially across the fleet. Consequently, contract structures only delay the negative earnings impact of route normalizations.
