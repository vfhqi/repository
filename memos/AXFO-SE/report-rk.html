Appendix

Plausible problems to monitor

1.
Signal 1: the discount core is losing ground to peers and to Axfood's own premium banners (Section 3, Section 5, Section 6, Section 10, Section 14). This is the worst signal because of how much of the group depends on it. Willys generates 54% of group sales and operating profit. Hemköp's like-for-like sales are rising 4.80% sequentially while Willys has slowed to just 0.30%. That Willys figure also missed a consensus of 1.30%. Peers are moving the other way. ICA Sweden reported a margin improvement of more than 100 basis points year-on-year. DNB Carnegie expects Axfood's store sales to keep lagging the broader Swedish grocery market. Rival pressure is also building. Coop launched permanent price cuts of 6.50% to 6.90% on about 600 products. Lidl plans at least 100 new stores. The report's own verdict is that Willys underperformance represents the primary risk to medium-term earnings.
2.
Signal 2: the currency profile is less protected than peers' (Section 16, Section 19–Section 21, Section 23). Axfood's exposure is one- directional. The euro accounts for 95.10%, or SEK 8,916 mm, of the net transaction outflow. The cost table records no natural hedge against revenue. By contrast, ICA Gruppen's finance policy keeps the earnings impact of a 5% exchange rate change within SEK 10 mm. The report's unhedged sensitivity work implies that a 10% EUR/SEK move would swing EPS by ±25.39%. This is a hypothetical figure that real hedging should substantially absorb. A policy-versus-practice gap also appears. Policy calls for hedging 100% of foreign currency investments above SEK 10 m, yet only about 70% of the Witron payment flows are hedged. The passthrough defence is weakest where it is needed most, because falling prices make passing on incremental currency costs commercially challenging. The sensitivity numbers measure gross exposure rather than expected losses, so this signal is real but conditional.
3.
Signal 3: a premium valuation sits against weakening capital returns (Section 3, Section 9, Section 12, Section 13). This is a valuation-risk signal, not a price view. Bernstein highlights that Axfood has historically traded at a substantial premium to its international European grocery peers. The same broker expects the historically high return on invested capital to compress because of margin pressure across multiple banners. It also argues that this should drive a derating. A premium that depends on stable profitability is exposed when estimates move down. DNB Carnegie cut its 2027 EPS projection by 5% as food price deflation lingered. One counterpoint is that Coop Sweden's separate cooperatives have reported persistent operating losses exceeding SEK 1 bn annually. That suggests Axfood's relative quality may still be intact.
4.
Signal 4: a loss-making acquisition and heavy automation capital spending drain cash more than peers' investment programmes do (Section 3, Section 4, Section 6, Section 11). DNB Carnegie says the City Gross turnaround remains a highly challenging integration risk that will keep diluting group operating margins by approximately 0.30%. The turnaround has made some progress. City Gross posted an adjusted operating profit of SEK 24 mm in the third quarter of 2026, against a loss of SEK 4 mm a year earlier. The cash demands are separate. The SEK 2.9bn second automated centre is expected to compress free cash flow by 13% to 14% over six years, which threatens future dividend growth potential. Meanwhile, Dagab's profit was flat despite a 25% productivity gain at Bålsta. Efficiency gains are not yet reaching reported earnings, which is the "unclear link" risk (Section 13).
5.
Signal 5: governance is more concentrated than is typical for widely held peers (Section 4, Section 7). Axel Johnson AB controls 50.10% of total shares and voting power. Three directors are non-independent because of their executive roles at Axel Johnson. Deloitte has served as principal auditor since 2016. The report offers no peer governance benchmark, so this is the weakest signal. Concentration also has a mitigating side, since it makes hostile activist campaigns structurally difficult to execute. The signals interact. A weak discount core (Signal 1) leaves less room to pass through currency costs (Signal 2). It also pressures the earnings that support a premium valuation (Signal 3) and strains the cash needed for automation and the City Gross turnaround (Signal 4). Mitigants exist for each. Axfood adjusts shelf prices to the hedged import rate, Hemköp growth partly offsets Willys weakness, and Bålsta productivity is rising. The weaknesses are therefore best read as stress points to monitor, not conclusions about earnings. Future work should obtain disclosed short-interest and implied-volatility data, plus consistent peer hedge ratios and governance benchmarks, so these signals can be tested quantitatively.

Outliers worth noting

Across the report, three anomalies stand out as statistical outliers or unexplained gaps between the evidence and the reported outcome. None is a confirmed accounting problem. Each marks a place where the numbers do not fit the surrounding narrative. The first outlier is the gap between modeled and realised currency sensitivity (Section 15, Section 17, Section 19, Section 20).

Revenue is almost entirely in kronor: the Swedish Krona accounts for over 99% of total revenue annually. Even so, the Section 20 sensitivity table shows extreme profit leverage to the euro. A 10% adverse move in EUR/SEK implies an unhedged EBITDA impact of ±10.59%. The net profit effect equates to an EPS swing of ±SEK 3.28 or ±25.39%, and a 20% move would change EPS by ±SEK 6.56 or ±50.77%.

These are the largest figures in the report, and they sit beside a treasury mandate that requires 100% of import purchase orders to be hedged with forward contracts at order placement. Reported earnings also moved far less than the sensitivities suggest. Group net profit fell from SEK 2,373 mm in fiscal year 2023 to SEK 2,219 mm in fiscal year 2024, and recovered to SEK 2,375 mm in fiscal year 2025. The sensitivity appears to measure a hypothetical unhedged state that the hedging and pricing framework largely prevents.

The residual risk sits in the exclusions: treasury policies explicitly exclude foreign currency overheads from hedging mandates, and 30% of the future payment flows on the EUR 265 mm Witron automation agreement remain unhedged. The anomaly is therefore real but conditional on pricing pass-through, which the deflationary backdrop in Section 18 and Section 23 puts in doubt.
