Section 2 – Net Financing Costs and Refinancing Pressures

Section summary
  1. Vodafone faces a transition to higher net financing costs post-FY26 as high-yielding disposal cash balances are depleted and the Zegona preference shares are fully redeemed.
  2. Refinancing pressures remain manageable due to Vodafone's long average bond life of 13 years, but peers like Telefónica and BT Group present distinct interest cost risks.
  3. Vodacom's consolidation of Safaricom significantly raises reported revenue and EBITDA but introduces a multi-layered minority interest leakage that dilutes attributable earnings.
  4. Peer comparisons show that managing consolidated non-controlling interests requires a careful balance between high funding costs and active capital return structures.

Post-Disposal Finance Income Compression

1.
Operational transition: Vodafone faces a structural step-up in net interest expenses as its massive disposal cash cushions are deployed. The company previously benefited from substantial investment income on short-term deposits and holding proceeds. As these cash balances are spent on consolidation and buybacks, investment income will decline sharply, driving net costs higher. This transition directly threatens to compress the group's adjusted earnings per share.
2.
Redemption impact: The full redemption of Zegona preference shares in January 2026 removes a reliable high- yield income stream from Vodafone's balance sheet. Zegona paid a special dividend of €1.4 bn to redeem the €900m of preferred equity in full. This transaction eliminates the lucrative five percent coupon payments that previously offset Vodafone's gross financing costs. The loss of this income stream creates a clear headwind for net finance costs from FY27 onward.
3.
Financing costs: Vodafone reported an adjusted net financing cost of €819m in FY26 which was heavily subsidized by temporary cash returns. Analysts estimate that net financing costs will rise as these nonrecurring interest benefits are fully exhausted. Without these offsets, the underlying gross cost of debt will more fully reflect current market rates. This escalation will directly pressure the group's net income margin.
4.
Portfolio simplification: Management has actively used asset sales to reduce leverage and simplify its complicated balance sheet structure. The company has secured significant cash inflows by reducing its stakes in Vantage Towers and selling underperforming European assets. While this strategy has successfully brought net debt down to lower levels, it simultaneously reduces the group's overall operational scale. Consequently, the remaining business must support a more concentrated debt stack.

Refinancing Pressures and Maturity Profiles

5.
Refinancing obligations: Vodafone faces continuous senior debt refinancing requirements that will test its borrowing costs in a high-rate environment. The company maintains average annual senior refinancing needs of approximately €1 bn between FY27 and FY31. This steady refinancing requirement means that new debt will be issued at prevailing market yields. The gradual repricing of this debt stack will steadily push up the group's average interest rate.
6.
Maturity cushion: The long-dated profile of Vodafone's existing bond stack provides a significant buffer against immediate refinancing shocks. The group boasts an average bond maturity life of 13 years, which is longer than most European peers. This extended duration ensures that only a small fraction of total debt matures each year. Therefore, the immediate P&L impact of higher interest rates remains highly contained.
7.
Leverage metrics: Vodafone's leverage ratio temporarily increased following cash outflows for major consolidation transactions. The group reported net debt of €33.5 bn in H1 FY27, up significantly from €25.4 bn at the end of FY26 due to UK minority and Kenyan acquisitions. Analysts expect that upcoming asset sale proceeds will reduce leverage back toward target levels. This balance sheet repair is critical to supporting the group's long-term credit rating.
8.
Refinancing drag: The incremental interest burden from refinancing older, low-coupon bonds will gradually dilute free cash flow over the next four years. Debt issued during the historical low-rate era must be replaced with higher-yielding instruments. Deutsche Bank estimates that the gradual repricing of maturing debt represents a modest but persistent headwind for the wider sector. For Vodafone, this drag remains manageable because the majority of its debt stack is locked at fixed rates.
