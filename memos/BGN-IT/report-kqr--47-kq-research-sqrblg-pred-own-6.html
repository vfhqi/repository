Networks

FinecoBank deferred incentive structures

1.
Structured deferred compensation: FinecoBank implements multi-year personal financial adviser incentive systems where 40% of the bonus is deferred over five years to align adviser performance with group sustainability. In its historical PFA 2015-2017 plan, FinecoBank recognized these costs as commission expenses over the vesting period, which helped maintain a stable adviser headcount. This deferred equitysettled structure reduces immediate voluntary departures because advisers face the forfeiture of unvested equity, stabilizing long-term EPS.
2.
Defensive multi-year vesting: FinecoBank's 2018-2020 Long Term Incentive Plan for personal financial advisers utilized strict capital and compliance malus conditions to deter adviser churn. The board resolved on 5 February 2026 to distribute deferred shares over up to six years for identified staff. This extended timeline means that unvested equity acts as a constant drag on adviser departures, protecting FinecoBank's recurring management fee engine.
3.
Regulatory deferral guidelines: FinecoBank aligns its adviser payouts with Bank of Italy and European rules that mandate deferring a significant portion of variable compensation for identified staff. Its 2024 incentive plan distributed bonuses over up to five years using a structured mix of cash and phantom shares. These strict regulatory guidelines mean that all major networks share similar retention barriers, reducing the relative competitive advantage of any single rival network.
4.
Contrary evidence: In March 2023, Jefferies reported that FinecoBank experienced accelerated deposit outflows despite its strong advisor network, demonstrating that structural retention schemes do not fully insulate platforms from retail liquidity shifts. While the bank recruited 29 senior advisers in early 2026, it still faces competitive pressure as rival networks offer aggressive upfront packages. Consequently, legacy schemes may fail to prevent sudden client asset outflows during periods of intense competition.

Azimut stability pacts and ownership

5.
Contractual clawback mechanisms: Azimut Holding structures its recruitment incentives around a strict contractual stability pact that obligates departing advisers to return unearned upfront bonuses. Under this framework, Azimut amortizes customer acquisition costs over a ten-year horizon to reflect average relationship longevity. This long amortization period means that advisers face substantial financial penalties if they leave prematurely, protecting Azimut's operating margins from sudden churn.
6.
Shareholder alignment models: Azimut mitigates historical advisor departures by allowing its self-employed financial advisers to participate directly in the equity of the parent holding company. This cooperative structure supports a high average corporate seniority of approximately ten years among its Italian distribution networks. Such equity ownership creates a powerful cultural lock-in because advisers share in the group's dividend payments, reducing asset outflows and supporting EPS stability.
7.
Advisor network dynamics: Azimut's domestic network showed 45 new engagements compared to 72 outgoing advisers in the first nine months of 2025, proving that even structured networks experience net departures. This net loss of 27 advisers contrast with FinecoBank's recruitment of 60 advisers in the first quarter of 2026. These figures suggest that equity-based loyalty schemes are less effective during corporate restructuring phases, which can temporarily pressure asset management fee growth.

Banca Mediolanum succession and portfolio rules

8.
Managed portfolio transfers: Banca Mediolanum employs a highly structured portfolio transfer framework where retiring advisers transfer client relationships directly to junior successors within the network. The bank facilitates this transition by compensating the transferring adviser in up to 60 equal monthly instalments, which are subsequently recovered from the successor. This internal pipeline ensures that client assets remain within the network during generational transitions, insulating Mediolanum's recurring fee base.
9.
Strict non-compete covenants: Banca Mediolanum conditions its manager adviser promoter allowances on strict compliance with post-termination non-compete agreements. Retiring managers only receive their supplementary old-age pension benefits if they refrain from performing competing financial activities for two years after their mandate ends. These covenants effectively neutralize competitive recruitment threats from rival networks, preserving Mediolanum's premium valuation multiple and core advisory EPS.
10.
Liquidity conversion strategies: Banca Mediolanum utilizes high-rate promotional time deposit campaigns as a tactical client acquisition channel rather than relying solely on adviser recruitment. This broker argues that Mediolanum successfully converted these promotional cash deposits into high-margin managed assets after their six-month expiration. This product-led strategy shows that client asset inflows can be sustained independently of adviser recruitment, lowering the average cost of adviser acquisition.

Fideuram and competitive recruitment dynamics

11.
Scaled advisory dominance: Fideuram Intesa Sanpaolo Private Banking leverages its immense scale to maintain an advisor network of 5,819 personal financial advisers as of early 2026. Intesa Sanpaolo reported that its private banking division generated €1.21 billion in net fee income in the first half of 2026, up 6.6% year-on-year. This scale enables Fideuram to absorb unvested loyalty costs of recruited advisers, making it a primary threat to smaller networks with expiring schemes.

Aggressive recruitment positioning: Deutsche Bank reported in September 2025 that Banca Generali is highly competitive in recruiting experienced senior advisers from disrupted networks due to its premium positioning. These senior recruits historically contribute approximately 30% of new asset inflows for Banca Generali. This recruitment capacity means that BGN can offset voluntary departures by pulling talent from competitors, stabilizing its core asset management fees.

Other side of the story: Although legacy loyalty scheme expirations theoretically expose networks to elevated adviser churn, the historical precedent suggests that competitive buyouts are too costly to execute at scale. Remaining Cycles

Framework Loyalty Programme Mechanics

1.
Structural inception: Banca Generali established its Framework Loyalty Programme in March 2017 to improve adviser and customer retention. The programme consists of eight annual plans designed to expire on 31 December 2026. The accumulated indemnities will be disbursed cumulatively in early 2027 within sixty days of approving the annual accounts. This structured timeline delays potential adviser departures until the cash and equity components finally vest.
2.
Seniority requirements: Participation in the annual plans is reserved exclusively for advisers with five years of company seniority. To be eligible for benefits, advisers must achieve a minimum volume of total assets under management. This tenure threshold means that the most experienced financial professionals are locked into the system. Consequently, this strict eligibility secures the bank's core assets against short-term competitive poaching.
3.
Strategic suspension: Shareholders resolved on 21 April 2022 to suspend the loyalty programme, preventing the activation of the sixth cycle. Consequently, no additional annual cycles have been initiated since the fifth cycle in 2021. This tactical pause means no new legacy loyalty obligations accrued after the 2021 annual plan. This decision limits the total outstanding liability of the bank as the remaining cycles approach their expiration.
4.
Share-based mechanics: The programme permits up to 50% of the accrued indemnity to be paid out in Banca Generali shares. The remaining half of the bonus is settled in cash using specific provisions. As of December 2025, the total number of shares in service of the five plans stood at 1.41 million shares. This share-based structure is governed by strict regulatory guidelines to ensure sufficient free float.

If advisers leave before this date, they risk facing significant financial clawbacks on these advanced funds.
