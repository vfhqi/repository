However, this debt-funded approach carries material second-order risks, as Deutsche Bank Research notes that leveraging to fund dividend deficits pushes net debt toward the 2.0x EBITDA target ceiling and risks negative outlook actions from S&P Global Ratings and Moody's, while Rothschild & Co Redburn emphasizes that replacing sub-1% legacy debt and the €300 million bond maturing in 2027 at prevailing market rates above 3.5% creates a persistent net earnings drag that further depresses organic payout coverage. & Co Redburn Management has implemented a €40 million transformation program to bridge cash flow gaps through operational efficiencies.

Management launched a major transformation program in late 2025 to simplify operations and increase productivity, targeting €40 million in annual cost savings during 2026, with the first phase leading to a reduction of 357 to 360 jobs across the organization. Additionally, Elisa unified its international software portfolio under the brand Elisa Industriq, targeting AI-based operational intelligence across manufacturing, telecom, and energy sectors, and completed the acquisition of the remaining 81% stake in Italian industrial software firm sedApta Group in early 2025, adding €49 million in annual revenue.

Yet following Q2 2026 project postponements driven by global macroeconomic uncertainties, management lowered full-year 2026 organic revenue growth targets for International Software Services to 5–10%, down from over 10%, undermining confidence in the growth pillar.

The Nordic and European telecom peer landscape provides sobering base rates for unsustainable payout policies. European and Nordic telecom peers demonstrate that balance sheet leverage within target corridors is consistently prioritized over maintaining unsustainable dividend payouts during periods of cash flow tightness, with Vodafone halving its dividend per share by 50% from 9.0 euro cents to 4.5 euro cents in FY25 following the asset sales of Spain and Italy to align with a 65% clean free cash flow payout ratio.

Critically, Elisa stands out among European peers by distributing 100-102% of net profit, whereas European sector peers maintain average dividend payout ratios around 65% of free cash flow. The investment implications for concentrated long-only holders center on asymmetric downside risk. Funding dividends through incremental debt triggers equity yield compression and credit rating pressure, restricting Elisa's M&A flexibility, with DNB Carnegie noting that absorbing balance sheet capacity to fund ordinary dividends eliminates financial room for inorganic growth, stalling expansion targets for Elisa IndustrIQ across international industrial software markets.

The Board possesses both the authority and the balance sheet capacity to sustain distributions for an additional two to three fiscal years through leverage drawdown, yet this path risks compounding earnings dilution and eventual forced policy reset when leverage capacity exhausts. The alternative scenario–a proactive dividend cut to below 80% of net profit–would align Elisa with sector base rates but trigger immediate equity de-rating given the company's twelve-year growth track record and embedded shareholder expectations.

Resolving the investment question requires monitoring three critical near-term catalysts: whether mobile service revenue recovers by Q4 2026 to stabilize organic EBITDA growth above 2% annually, whether International Software Services margins rebound from negative 14% in Q2 2026 to validate management's turnaround thesis, and whether working capital optimizations can sustainably contribute €30-50 million annually without triggering vendor financing risks observed at Nordic peer Telia.

The interplay of these operational drivers against the Board's unwillingness to formally adjust distribution policy creates a binary outcome path for equity holders by mid-2027, with balance sheet leverage acting as the primary temporal buffer before policy resolution becomes unavoidable.
