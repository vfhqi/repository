The one-line view. Neutral, working hypothesis: Indra has the best-evidenced demand tailwind in its peer set and an unusually complete corporate change, but the case rests on whether a company with a poor cash history can convert a doubled backlog into cash, at a multiple already at the ninety-seventh percentile of its own history.

What the company does

Indra is a Spanish technology group in mid-transformation. On fiscal 2025 sales of 5,457 million euros, up 13 per cent, Minsait information technology services was still the largest piece at 57 per cent, with Defence at 26 per cent, Air Traffic Management at 10 per cent and Mobility at 7 per cent. It is an information technology services company whose defence and space arms are growing fast enough to redefine the whole.

Its position is protected in two places. Indra is prime contractor on 17 of Spain's 31 Special Modernisation Programmes, with 77 per cent of Spanish defence procurement directed to domestic suppliers, and over 90 per cent of global daily flights use air traffic solutions the group designed, replaced roughly once a decade.

A plausible investment thesis

Demand is the reliable part. Spain's Special Modernisation Programmes total 34 billion euros to 2037, and Spanish equipment procurement is projected to double to 21 billion euros by 2030. Indra's backlog doubled to 20.5 billion euros in the first half of 2026, over 3.3 years of sales, with the defence backlog alone up 159 per cent to 11.7 billion euros.

The mechanism to earnings per share is operating leverage on that volume. Second-quarter 2026 revenue rose 43.5 per cent and operating profit 73.4 per cent to a 10.7 per cent margin. Morgan Stanley's fiscal 2026 operating profit estimate of 804 million euros sits against consensus of 706 million. Drivers to track: the defence margin against the 17 per cent first-half reading, underlying free cash flow excluding prepayments, Minsait's book-to-bill against 1.0 times, and inventories against the 1,926 million euro June 2026 level.
