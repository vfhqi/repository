Section 1 – The Key Question and Why It Matters

Section summary
  1. The investor’s Key Question addresses the fundamental tension between PGE’s heavy state control, its massive decarbonization capital requirements, and the sustainability of its earnings and dividends as coal subsidies expire.
1.
The Key Question: "PGE Polska Grupa Energetyczna S.A. (PGE-PL, Warsaw; not Portland General Electric or PG&E): Poland's largest state-controlled integrated power group (State Treasury 60.86%); segments: Distribution (regulated grid), District Heating, Coal Energy (lignite and hard-coal generation plus own lignite mines), Gas-fired Generation, Renewables (onshore, solar, storage, and offshore wind including the 50% Baltica 2 joint operation with Ørsted), Supply (retail), and Railway Energy Services. Key question: how comprehensively are these dynamics articulated in the current narrative of sell-side commentary and of investor commentary: How widely do sell-side analysts and investors (including on Twitter/X and Polish investor forums) discuss each of these for PGE?"

Impact on Earnings and Risk Answering this question is critical for investors because the identified

dynamics represent the primary drivers of PGE’s valuation and long-term viability. The end of coal capacity payments in 2028 creates a potential earnings cliff that could render existing coal assets loss-making, as noted by the Polish Ministry of State Assets, which estimated a Zloty 53.8 bn financial gap for coal units through 2040. If sell-side analysts are not fully pricing this loss or the potential for 22% corporate income tax, the current EPS consensus for 2026-2028 may be overly optimistic, especially as Baltica 2 and Rybnik CCGT enter service and drive up depreciation and interest expenses.

Furthermore, the state’s 60.86% ownership means dividend decisions are often political; a dividend paid while free cash flow is negative could further strain a balance sheet already approaching a 3.5x net debt/EBITDA ceiling.

Related Investor Questions Closely related to this inquiry is the question of the "coal carve-out" or a

post-2028 strategic reserve, which remains the single largest source of uncertainty for the Polish utility sector. Investors are also focused on the 2027 General Rate Case (GRC) and whether the Polish regulator will allow higher bond yields (now exceeding 6%) to lift the distribution WACC, which is currently a primary pillar of PGE’s EBITDA growth strategy. Finally, the execution risk of the Baltica 2 offshore wind project, a 1.5 GW joint venture with Ørsted, remains a key factor in whether PGE can replace its coal earnings with low-carbon contracted cash flows before the 2028 subsidy cliff.

2.
Contrary evidence: While most sell-side commentary focuses on these strategic shifts, some reports from earlier periods suggest that the market has historically underestimated the political pressure to keep coal plants running for national security, regardless of their unprofitability. Bernstein Research noted in May 2025 that it would not be surprised to see coal assets further supported by the Polish government to avoid a potential power generation gap, suggesting that the "losses" expected after 2029 might be mitigated by new, as-yet-unannounced subsidies. This suggests the narrative may be too focused on market economics rather than the political reality of statecontrolled utilities.
3.
Other side of the story: The prevailing focus on the coal cliff and leverage may ignore the significant upside from Poland's accelerating electrification and data center demand. Jefferies and Bernstein both highlight that Polish distribution assets are entering a "capex supercycle," with RAB growth targets that could fundamentally re-rate the companies as infrastructure plays rather than legacy generators. If the regulator provides a supportive WACC in 2027, the regulated earnings growth could more than offset the coal losses, rendering the current sell-side skepticism about the dividend sustainability unfounded.

Section 2 – Coal Subsidy Cliff and Post-2028 Solutions

Section summary
  1. Sell-side commentary is highly focused on the 2028 capacity payment deadline, which most analysts view as a major pivot point for PGE’s valuation. Confidence in this dynamic is high because it is rooted in specific, multi-year contracts that are publicly disclosed and widely modeled by brokers like Bernstein.

The 2028 Capacity Payment Cliff Sell-side analysts, led by Bartlomiej Kubicki of Bernstein Research,

have extensively documented that PGE "outsmarted its peers" by securing five-year capacity market contracts that expire at the end of 2028. This is a critical point of differentiation, as Enea and Tauron’s five-year contracts, which began in 2021, leave those utilities facing a revenue cliff as early as 2026. PGE’s Conventional Generation division earned Zloty 2.2 bn in capacity market contracts in FY23 and Zloty 2.25 bn in FY24, and Bernstein expects these payments to stabilize revenues specifically through the end of 2028. After 2028, however, the lack of new mechanisms justifies what PGE management calls "greater flexibility" in planning, as the current auctions do not extend to legacy coal.

Coal Losses and Economic Viability The scale of coal losses after 2028 is a recurring theme in news

and regulatory analysis, though specific sell-side EPS models for the 2030s are rare. The Polish Ministry of State Assets stated in late 2025 that hard coal and lignite-fired power plants will become unprofitable by 2028. The Ministry’s analysis, included in a draft resolution, estimated a cumulative financial gap of Zloty 53.8 bn for the 2025-2040 period for assets owned by PGE, Tauron, Energa, and Enea. Fitch Ratings projects ongoing pressure on coal profitability due to rising CO₂ costs and lower electricity prices, asserting that balancing services will likely be insufficient to keep generation profitable after 2028.

Strategic Reserve and NABE Carve-out The narrative regarding a "post-2028 coal solution" is

characterized by high uncertainty and shifting government policy. Most analysts previously focused on the National Energy Security Agency (NABE) carve-out, but Bernstein reported in September 2025 that the Ministry of State Assets had proposed repealing the resolution to spin off these units into a single entity, calling it "economically unjustified." Instead, the narrative has shifted toward potential strategic reserves or individual company-led decommissioning plans. Bernstein notes that there is currently "little visibility" on the carve-out process, though they would not be surprised by new government support given the risk of a power generation gap if coal units are decommissioned too quickly.

Peer Comparisons and Past Cases Compared to regional peers, PGE’s coal exposure is uniquely

weighted toward lignite, which traditionally has lower variable costs than the hard coal used by Enea and Tauron. In a comparable case from 2013, CEZ faced similar disputes over lignite power plants and coal pricing, which Morgan Stanley noted was essential to resolving through asset sales or strategic repositioning to avoid earnings deterioration. Similarly, PPC (Public Power Corporation) in Greece has pursued a much more aggressive lignite phase-out, targeting a full exit by year-end 2026, which Morgan Stanley views as a "full redesign" of the group's operating base toward renewables and flexibility. This contrasts with PGE’s slower transition, where coal is expected to remain a "medium-term drag" even as capacity payments phase out.

1.
Contrary evidence: Contrary to the view that coal will be a total loss, Bernstein Research pointed out that PGE has successfully increased ancillary service revenues in its renewables and conventional divisions, reaching Zloty 1.1 bn in FY24. These revenues, related to the balancing market, could provide a partial buffer against the loss of capacity payments, a dynamic that some simpler "cliff" models might overlook.
2.
Other side of the story: While the 2028 cliff is real, the state’s 60% stake in PGE and the necessity of coal for system stability mean the "losses" are more likely to be socialized or subsidized than to result in corporate insolvency. Macro sources like Erste Bank note that Poland’s transition is delayed by grid unreadiness, which may force the government to extend coal support mechanisms through 2030 and beyond to ensure security of supply. Thus, the "cliff" might be repeatedly pushed back, as already seen with the EU's 2028 derogation approval, providing a longer earnings tail for PGE than current sell-side consensus assumes.

Section 3 – Distribution Returns and WACC Dynamics

Section summary
  1. The allowed return on distribution is articulated as the primary engine of PGE's future earnings growth, with moderate confidence that the 2027 GRC will reflect higher bond yields. Analysts focus heavily on the growth of the Regulatory Asset Base (RAB) rather than the minute fluctuations of the WACC itself.

The 2027 General Rate Case (GRC) PGE’s distribution earnings are fundamentally tied to the

Regulatory Asset Base (RAB) and the allowed WACC set by the Energy Regulatory Office (ERO). Bernstein Research forecasts PGE’s electricity distribution RAB to grow at a CAGR of >9% through FY30, supported by accelerating modernization and renewables integration. The 2027 GRC is identified as a key catalyst, as it will determine the returns for the next regulatory period. Jefferies notes that for US peers like Portland General Electric (POR), the 2027 GRC is focused on a "bridge mechanism" to support recovery until a multi-year rate plan in 2029, a structural evolution that Polish investors are watching for similar regulatory modernization.

Bond Yields and WACC Adjustment A central question for investors is whether the spike in Polish

government bond yields, which exceeded 6% in 2026, will be reflected in a higher allowed WACC. Bernstein Research highlights that electricity networks offer the most visible earnings growth profile for PGE, underpinned by supportive regulatory dynamics that factor in the cost of capital. Peer data from Tauron shows that WACC was as high as 10.5% in 2024 and 10.8% in 2025 (including reinvestment premiums), but Fitch expects it to normalize to 9.5% in 2026 and average 8% in 2027-2030. If bond yields remain elevated, there is an expectation among investors that the regulator will have to adjust these returns upward to ensure that massive Capex plans (Zloty 117 bn by 2030 for PGE) remain financeable.

RAB CAGR and Segment EBITDA Bernstein projects that combined EBITDA from distribution and

railway energy services will reach Zloty 7.7 bn by FY30, accounting for approximately 55% of PGE’s total group EBITDA. This structural shift towards regulated, lower-risk earnings is a core part of the sell-side "Outperform" thesis. By comparison, Energa reports a total financing limit of Zloty 30.83 bn for its Operator (distribution) business, highlighting the massive scale of network financing across the Polish sector.

Jefferies observes that "stable and expected" investment property growth in related sectors continues to benefit from well-run regulatory frameworks, which bolsters the case for PGE’s network-led transition.

International Peer Comparisons PGE’s allowed returns are often compared to other European state

utilities to judge their relative attractiveness. Morgan Stanley notes that Endesa in Spain has an "Allowed return on electricity Networks" of 5.6% for 2025, rising to 6.6% for 2026-2028, significantly lower than the ~9% seen in Poland. However, Spanish regulators are facing similar pressure to adapt to "persistently higher inflation," which Morgan Stanley says could trigger an adaptation of the nominal regulatory framework. In Romania, Public Power Corporation’s distribution units have a regulated rate of return set at 6.94% for the 2025-2029 period, further suggesting that Polish returns remain among the highest in the CEE region.

1.
Contrary evidence: While higher yields usually lift WACC, regulators often use "affordability" as a counter-weight. Jefferies points to a recent case where WEC Energy had its ROE cut to 9.5% from 9.8% due to a regulatory focus on consumer affordability, a risk that could easily manifest in Poland during the 2027 election year. If the Polish regulator prioritizes capping consumer bills, the WACC might not rise in tandem with bond yields, leading to a significant EBITDA miss compared to current broker models.
2.
Other side of the story: The focus on WACC percentage may be secondary to the sheer volume of RAB growth. Bernstein’s forecast of 55% of EBITDA coming from distribution by 2030 suggests that even if the allowed return stays flat or dips slightly, the Zloty 210 bn cumulative Capex plan will drive absolute earnings higher. Investors may be over-analyzing the rate of return while under-estimating the compounding power of a regulated asset base that is targeted to double in size over the next decade.

Section 4 – Fiscal Stress, Taxation, and Windfall Levies

Section summary
  1. The narrative regarding tax rises and windfall levies is characterized by macroeconomic concern but limited company-specific sell-side modeling. Confidence is medium, as the fiscal deficit is well-documented, but the specific mechanism for energy-sector surcharges remains speculative in current research.

Polish Fiscal Stress and Deficit Poland’s fiscal position is a major point of discussion in CEE macro

reports, with the budget deficit reaching 7.3% of GDP in 2025 and expected to remain at 6.5% in 2026. Erste Bank notes that Poland’s fiscal outlook has deteriorated, leading to a Moody’s downgrade to A3 with a stable outlook in mid-2026. This environment of "fiscal stress" is cited as a driver for higher government bond yields, which have exceeded 6%, increasing the state's borrowing requirements and potentially leading to "growthdistorting taxes." 56

Potential Tax Hikes and Windfall Levies While the specific "22% corporate income tax rise" mentioned

in the Key Question is not explicitly confirmed in retrievals, macro reports frequently mention the risk of new fiscal measures to narrow the deficit. Swedbank notes that plans to narrow the deficit have been hindered by political gridlock, but "tax increases" are a recurring topic of presidential vetoes. Bernstein Research observed in early 2026 that the Italian government was looking to increase taxes for utilities, suggesting a broader European trend of targeting the sector for fiscal repair. In Poland, the "Act on Emergency Measures" has already limited the revenues of electricity generators, a mechanism that could be extended if fiscal pressures persist into the 2027 election year.

EPS Impact of Taxation PGE currently reports a corporate income tax rate of 19%, but the effective tax

rate is often lower due to energy-related credits. Bernstein’s EPS forecasts for PGE (PLN 2.19 in 2026e, PLN 1.89 in 2027e) would be significantly impacted by a three-percentage-point rise in the base rate. Furthermore, "recurring one-off" provisions for things like actuarial changes (Zloty 20 mm in 2025) and PDP Fund contributions (Zloty 605 mm for the Supply segment) suggest that the "true" tax burden and regulatory surcharge profile is highly volatile.

Comparable Regional Cases In Spain, the government recently authorized Zloty 17.9 bn in power grid

investment but has also faced backlash from the utilities lobby (Aelec) over "state intervention" and market caps that analysts say make "no sense" now that gas prices have stabilized. Morgan Stanley identifies "Government intervention in Spanish Utility markets" as a primary downside risk for Endesa, a sentiment mirrored by CEE investors regarding the Polish government’s history of price caps and levies. Similarly, French lawmakers are considering extending energy voucher schemes and amending feed-in tariffs to reduce the state budget burden by Zloty 30 mm, further highlighting the sector's vulnerability to fiscal maneuvering.

1.
Contrary evidence: Despite the fiscal stress, some analysts argue that the government’s need for PGE to fund the energy transition (Zloty 210 bn by 2035) makes a massive new windfall levy counter-productive. Bernstein Research notes that 70% of planned Capex is expected to be supported by regulated returns or CfDs, which would be undermined if the state clawed back the same funds through aggressive taxation.
2.
Other side of the story: The "fiscal stress" narrative may be overblown as a risk to utilities if the government shifts the burden to the banking sector or high-income individuals instead. Erste Bank notes that income tax revenue growth in Poland remains "strong," and the current gridlock prevents major new tax legislation from passing before the 2027 election. Thus, PGE may enjoy a "tax holiday" simply because the coalition government cannot agree on new revenue measures, potentially leading to a near-term EPS beat compared to conservative macro-tilted models.

Section 5 – Dividend Sustainability and Free Cash Flow

Section summary
  1. The resumption of dividends while free cash flow is negative is a major point of debate, with sell-side analysts like Bernstein expressing cautious optimism. Confidence is medium because while the dividend policy is clear, the actual cash flow path depends on the highly variable execution of large Capex projects like Baltica 2.

Resumption of Dividends A key surprise in late 2026 was PGE’s announcement that it intends to

resume dividends from FY26 earnings, targeting a yield of up to 5%. This was a "positive surprise" for Bernstein, as both their estimates and Bloomberg consensus had previously assumed no distributions over the forecast period. The dividend policy is subject to meeting criteria such as recurring net profit, a prospect of positive free cash flow (FCF) for at least two years, and maintaining an investment-grade rating. However, Bernstein notes that this is subject to "earnings, leverage, and developments in the coal business."

Negative Free Cash Flow and Debt PGE’s aggressive Capex plan (Zloty 117 bn by 2030) puts

significant pressure on its cash position. In 2025-2026, analysts like ING historically predicted that high Capex would push FCF into negative territory, a trend that persists in current models due to the Zloty 30 bn cost of Baltica 2. PGE’s separate statement of cash flows for H1 2026 already shows a net change in cash of Zloty 2.68 bn, but with operating cash flow of negative Zloty 3.6 bn, the "sustainability" of paying dividends while borrowing to fund Capex is a central investor concern. Net debt reached Zloty 4.2 bn by end-2025, reflecting higher financing needs.

The State’s Vote and Political Influence As a 60.86% state-controlled entity, PGE’s dividend is often

seen as a tool for the State Treasury to fill budget gaps.

Historically, the government has filed motions for higher dividends than management proposed, as seen with Enea and Tauron in 2011-2012., the current strategy emphasizes "capital discipline," and Bernstein notes that management is selective about new gas projects to preserve returns. The State Treasury’s "special rights" in appointing the Supervisory Board ensure that the dividend ultimately aligns with political needs, which in an election year (2027) could favor payouts over debt reduction.

Peer Payout Comparisons Compared to peers, PGE’s dividend stance has been more conservative.

Tauron paid Zloty 350 mm in dividends for FY25, and CEZ maintains a policy of paying 50-60% of net income., Endesa maintains a 70% payout ratio with a DPS floor of 1.0, despite rising net debt (targeted at Zloty 10- 11 bn by 2027). Fortum, which is also state-owned, announced a special dividend of Zloty 0.20 in early 2015, highlighting that state utilities often prioritize shareholder returns even during periods of declining profitability.

1.
Contrary evidence: Deutsche Bank historically warned that PGE’s Capex would exceed operating cash flow by Zloty 3 bn annually, driving net debt/EBITDA from 0.3x to 2.5x, which would make a 40% payout ratio "ambitious." If FCF remains negative through 2028, any dividend payment could be viewed by credit agencies as a "weakening of the business profile," potentially triggering a rating downgrade.
2.
side of the story: The dividend might be more sustainable than it looks if PGE successfully secures "preferential" financing for its green projects. The company received a "Distribution Project loan" of Zloty 1.62 bn at fair value, which it recorded as a government grant (subsidy) because the interest rate was below market. If green Capex is effectively "free" or subsidized, the negative FCF headline is misleading, as the core recurring earnings can be safely returned to the State and minority shareholders.

Section 6 – Leverage, Debt Ceilings, and Equity Risk

Section summary
  1. The path toward the 3.5x net debt/EBITDA ceiling is a primary focus for credit analysts, though sell-side analysts remain neutral on the risk of a near-term equity raise. Confidence is medium; the debt path is visible, but the "risk" of an equity raise is often downplayed by management.

The 3.5x Debt Ceiling and Covenants Credit rating agencies, specifically Fitch, explicitly use FFO net

leverage of 3.5x as a negative sensitivity for Tauron and similar utilities, while PGE is given more headroom at 3.7x due to its scale. PGE’s management maintains that their "key rule" is not to exceed 2.5x net debt/EBITDA, but the intensification of investments has already pushed net debt to Zloty 4.2 bn by end-2025.

notes that leverage is a primary constraint on the "Outperform" thesis, and any slippage in EBITDA could rapidly push the group toward its covenants.

Risk of an Equity Raise The possibility of an equity raise is a "shadow" dynamic in the narrative–

frequently mentioned as a risk but rarely predicted as a baseline. Jefferies observes that other utilities facing "sizable upfront equity needs" (like HE) see their earnings outlook offset by dilution risk. PGE successfully issued shares in 2022 to maintain liquidity, and while management has not signaled a new issuance, the Zloty 117 bn Capex program through 2030 creates a massive funding gap. Enea is already considering "hybrid bonds" with 50% equity credit to protect its rating, a strategy PGE might follow to avoid outright dilution.

Financing Baltica 2 and Rybnik Specific project financing is a key bridge to avoiding equity. For Baltica

2, PGE secured a Zloty 3.9 bn loan from BGK and Zloty 11.1 bn from a consortium of 25 banks, which covers the majority of the project's Zloty 30 bn cost. Rybnik CCGT is also supported by inflation-indexed capacity market revenues, which Bernstein says reinforce gas as a transition pillar that provides "downside protection" for the balance sheet. If these projects generate contracted returns as expected, the "leverage cliff" may be avoided through project-level de-risking.

Comparative Leverage Metrics PGE currently enjoys the "lowest leverage among the peer group,"

according to Fitch, which gives it a structural advantage over Tauron and Enea. Tauron’s net debt/EBITDA stood at 1.2x in H1 2026, while PPC (Public Power Corporation) in Greece has deleveraged to 1.2x following a Zloty 4.5 bn equity raise. contrast, Endesa’s leverage is modest at 1.8x, but Morgan Stanley warns that Zloty 7.4 bn in debt maturities in 2027-2028 could trigger higher financial costs that impact earnings.

1.
Contrary evidence: Contrary evidence suggests that PGE’s debt might be higher than reported due to "off-balance sheet" items. ING historically noted that for peers like Tauron, part of the debt was not captured in the headline leverage, and for PGE, "preferential" loans with Zloty 2.88 bn in deferred income grants suggest a complex liability structure that could be reclassified by auditors or agencies.
2.
side of the story: The "risk of equity raise" may be overstated because the Polish state, owning 60%, is unlikely to want its stake diluted. Bernstein notes that management is becoming more "selective" about new projects, cutting Zloty 25 bn from the total Capex plan (from Zloty 235 bn to Zloty 210 bn) to maintain capital discipline. If the company prioritizes its rating over expansion, it can simply slow down its renewables rollout to stay within its 3.5x net debt/EBITDA limit, avoiding the need for an equity raise entirely.

PGE maintains that its key rule is not to exceed 2.5x net debt/EBITDA in upcoming years, even though capex is set to grow in upcoming years due to Opole and CHPs and potential wind farms investments.

Section 7 – EBITDA to EPS: Depreciation and Interest

Section summary
  1. The relationship between EBITDA and EPS is a "widely discussed" but often misunderstood dynamic, with depreciation from new assets expected to significantly compress EPS margins. Confidence is high; both company filings and broker models confirm a significant step-up in non-cash expenses from 2027.

The Depreciation Step-Up PGE’s aggressive investment in Baltica 2 (offshore wind), Rybnik CCGT, and

modernized grid assets will lead to a massive increase in Depreciation and Amortization (D&A). Bernstein’s forecasts show that while EBITDA is expected to grow from Zloty 12.1 bn in 2026e to Zloty 14.6 bn in 2030e, net income and EPS will not follow the same trajectory. For example, 2028e EBITDA is projected at Zloty 14.83 bn, but EPS is only Zloty 2.59, compared to Zloty 2.19 EPS on only Zloty 12.1 bn EBITDA in 2026e. This compression is driven by D&A, which historically outpaced investment at Fortum and is now catching up at PGE as major assets enter service.

Rising Interest Expense As PGE approaches its 3.5x debt ceiling, the cost of servicing that debt is

rising, especially given the "risk-off market" and bond yields >6%. Bernstein projects "Net interest (cash flow statement)" to remain a significant drag, forecasted at negative Zloty 1.15 bn in 2026e and rising to negative Zloty 1.35 bn by 2028e. Jefferies notes that for geothermal peers, "strong D&A expenses" and personnel costs led to a "stark miss" in earnings despite decent topline growth, a pattern that could easily repeat for PGE as it commissions its new fleet.

Consensus and Estimates The FactSet consensus as of 1 November 2026 (EBITDA Zloty 13.37 bn /

EPS Zloty 2.90 for 2026) appears to bake in a relatively high conversion rate that Bernstein’s more conservative model (EBITDA Zloty 12.1 bn / EPS Zloty 2.19) questions. Bernstein’s model assumes a higher WACC of 20% for Conventional Generation and 12% for Heat, reflecting the higher risk and financing costs of these legacy units. If depreciation from the Zloty 2.46 bn in capital additions expected by May 2028 is higher than modeled, the EPS downside could be as much as 10-15%.

Peer Depreciation Trends Peers are seeing similar "cost catch-up" dynamics. Tauron reported a 12%

decline in EBITDA for H1 2026, partly due to the "negative impact of the regulatory account settlement" and lower distribution margins., Endesa’s Morgan Stanley model applies a DCF to 2060, noting that terminal value resides almost entirely in Networks (~50% of total EV), highlighting that the generation segment’s D&A effectively wipes out its long-term value. Fortum’s comparable operating profit was also affected by Zloty 103 mm in "items affecting comparability," similar to the "recurring one-offs" that plague PGE’s reported net income.

1.
Contrary evidence: Contrary evidence from Jefferies suggests that for some utilities, "lower other O&M after 2026 flex spending" could offset higher parent interest. If PGE achieves its targeted Zloty 15 bn EBITDA by 2030 through operational efficiency gains (as management guides), the EPS compression might be less severe than Bernstein’s "conservative" model suggests.
2.
Other side of the story: The "EBITDA to EPS" narrative may be irrelevant to valuation if investors value PGE on an EV/EBITDA or RAB-multiple basis. Morgan Stanley values PPC (Public Power Corp) using a blend of EV/EBITDA and DCF, applying a 9.0x multiple to 2028e EBITDA, well ahead of historical averages. If the market shifts to valuing PGE as a "regulated infrastructure compounder" like National Grid or Iberdrola, the non-cash depreciation drag on EPS will be largely ignored by the narrative.

Section 8 – What Is Known and What Is Not

Section summary
  1. Overall confidence in the evidence regarding the Key Question is medium. While the strategic targets and coal subsidy timelines are clear, the specific fiscal impacts (22% tax) and the exact path of leverage after 2028 remain poorly defined by sell-side consensus. The Post-2028 Coal Subsidy Gap The single largest gap in the evidence is the specific mechanism that

will replace capacity payments after 2028. While the Ministry of State Assets has identified a Zloty 53.8 bn gap, it has not proposed a concrete alternative to the failed NABE carve-out. Closing this gap would require a new regulatory filing from the Polish government or a state-aid decision from the European Commission regarding a strategic reserve. Investors currently have to guess whether these units will be shut down, socialized, or subsidized through a new levy.

The 22% Corporate Tax Claim The research findings do not explicitly confirm a confirmed legislative

move to a 22% corporate income tax rate, despite the Key Question identifying it as a dynamic. Current company filings and macro reports still refer to a 19% rate, with no specific broker report modeling a Zloty 1.5 bn annual tax hit. A definitive government budget plan for 2027 or a thematic broker report on "Polish Utility Taxation" would be required to close this gap and determine if FactSet consensus is missing a major EPS headwind.

Baltica 2 Derivative Revaluations The "recurring one-off" provisions mentioned in the Key Question are

visible in H1 2026 reports, but the specific revaluation of Baltica 2 derivatives is poorly articulated in the current sell-side narrative. While Bernstein and others mention the project's construction status, they do not provide a forecast for the quarterly "noise" these financial instruments will create in reported net income. An expert call with a utility accounting specialist or more granular "underlying earnings" bridges in future PGE results would be needed to understand this volatility.

Election-Year Price Caps (2027) The risk of a 2027 household price cap is frequently cited by macro

sources like Swedbank and Erste Bank, but sell-side analysts have yet to "stress test" PGE’s Supply segment EBITDA for a potential freeze. The 2027 election is a major "known unknown" that could fundamentally decouple

EBITDA from power price trends. Waiting for the first draft of the 2027 energy tariff or political platform announcements from the major coalitions would be necessary to size this risk.

The 3.5x Leverage "Point of No Return" While analysts track the 3.5x ceiling, there is no consensus on

exactly when PGE hits it. Bernstein projects Zloty 117 bn in Capex through 2030, but does not provide a year-byyear net debt/EBITDA bridge that incorporates potential divestments or the Zloty 1.62 bn in "government grants" already received.

A detailed "sources and uses" table from management, similar to what Jefferies expects from POR, would be the primary document to resolve whether an equity raise is a 2027, 2029, or non-existent risk.

Section 9 – If the Answer Differs from the Common View

Section summary
  1. If the common view of a "smooth transition" to networks is wrong, PGE faces a significant EPS downgrade cycle, potentially triggered by a combination of fiscal surcharges and a hard coal subsidy cliff.

Impact on Earnings and Cash Flow The common sell-side view, represented by Bernstein’s

"Outperform" rating, is that PGE can successfully pivot to regulated networks and renewables while managing the coal decline for cash. However, if the Polish government imposes the suspected 22% tax or new energysector surcharges to address its 7.3% fiscal deficit, the EPS consensus for 2027-2028 would likely fall by 15-20% immediately. Furthermore, if the "post-2028 coal solution" is not found, the Zloty 53.8 bn gap could force PGE to suspend the very dividend it just promised to restart.

Knock-on Effects for the Sector A failure to resolve the coal cliff at PGE would have immediate knock-

on effects for suppliers and competitors. Lignite mines owned by PGE would face accelerated closure, impacting local employment and regional economic growth. For competitors like Tauron and Enea, who have higher leverage and less headroom, a "no-compensation" coal exit would likely trigger a sector-wide equity raise cycle as they breach the 3.5x FFO net leverage ceiling. Conversely, if the regulator allows a massive WACC increase due to 6%+ bond yields, the sector could re-rate as a high-yield infrastructure play, attracting a "new group of investors" currently excluded by the coal exposure.

Political and Regulatory Power The primary entities with the power to push the outcome away from the

"common view" are the Ministry of State Assets and the Energy Regulatory Office (ERO). The Ministry has already demonstrated its power by unilaterally killing the NABE carve-out, surprising analysts who had modeled a clean asset split. In 2027, the ERO could prioritize "residential rate trends relative to CPI" over utility returns, as seen in the US with Ameren, where rates were grown below inflation. Such a move would destroy the "compounding RAB" thesis that underpins PGE’s current valuation.
