Executive Summary

Section summary
  1. The evidence partly answers the Key Question. Verbund faces significant earnings volatility because its low-cost alpine hydropower fleet has high operating leverage, and the near-term scope for management-driven cost relief looks narrow. However, contract-level hedge terms, concession relief mechanics and grid opex categories are undisclosed, so the four-year trajectory cannot yet be pinned down. Confidence is medium.
1.
Fixed-cost structure: Alpine hydro costs are overwhelmingly fixed and independent of output. Swiss operators pay a legal water rate of CHF 110 per kilowatt, which alone is roughly twenty percent of production costs. Verbund's 2025 hydro segment earned €2,708.5m of revenue and €2,024.3m of EBITDA, a calculated margin of 74.7%. That compares with a calculated 33.8% for Fortum's Generation segment and 24.9% for Uniper's Green Generation segment. Verbund's margin is the product of near-zero variable cost, and that same feature makes any volume or price shortfall fall straight through to profit.
2.
Volume shock, 2025: A hydro coefficient of 0.79 cut hydropower generation by 24.2% to 25,370 GWh. Hydro segment EBITDA fell 31.8% to €2,024.3m. EBITDA fell by more than volumes, which shows fixed costs do not flex down with output.
3.
Drought and buybacks, 2026: The run-of-river coefficient was 0.61 over nine months and 0.49 in the third quarter, and group EBITDA fell 29% to €1,498.5m. Gross buyback costs reached €470m over nine months, or €120m net of optimization gains. These purchases pulled the average achieved price down to €79.7/MWh. The 2022 precedent is similar: a hydro coefficient of 0.86 and a negative EBITDA impact of €1,095m. The result is a recurring pattern of hedge-driven buyback losses on top of pure volume loss.
4.
Price sensitivity and hedging: Achieved hydro prices fell from €167.1/MWh in 2023 to €116.3/MWh in 2025. Hedging stands at 86% for 2026 and 34.5% for 2027, and the windfall-tax price cap of roughly €103/MWh makes returns asymmetric. A €10/MWh price move shifts 2028 net profit by 13.7%, against 4.9% for Iberdrola. Verbund therefore has uniquely leveraged price exposure among the peers compared.
5.
What broker and consensus figures assume: Consensus estimates frequently assume hydrology returns rapidly to historical averages after a single dry year. Morgan Stanley indicates the share price discounts a long-term power price of roughly €74/MWh. Deutsche Bank estimates the Limberg outages cause a €50m earnings headwind in 2026. Bernstein projects group D&A rising from €625m in 2025 to €768m by 2028. Broker numbers therefore embed a normal-hydrology rebound and a firm price floor, and neither is yet evidenced.
6.
Peer cost programmes: Fortum targeted €100m of annual fixed-cost savings by end-2025 and delivered, reaching about €873m. Statkraft cut roughly 600 employees in 2025 and expects a further 350 in 2026. Ørsted booked DKK 750m of rightsizing charges in 2025. Large cuts came from corporate overhead and portfolio exits and carried upfront restructuring costs. Core generation and concession costs were not what moved.
7.
Verbund cost rigidity: Nine-month 2026 personnel expenses rose 7.4% to €477.3m, helped by a 2.8% collective- agreement increase and headcount growth. State ownership constrains rapid payroll reductions. Verbund's own base is moving in the opposite direction to Statkraft and Ørsted. Only non-personnel lines, such as other operating expenses of €384.8m, show modest incremental cuttability.
8.
Grid and capital plan: The 2026–2028 capex plan totals €6,789m, with about €2.24bn for regulated grid investment. The regulatory account is expected to reach about €590m by end-2026. Management has limited scope to lift earnings through grid opex elimination, because opex is largely passed through. The grid stabilizes earnings, but the programme also builds a permanently higher depreciation base.
9.
Limberg outages: Limberg III unit two began recommissioning at the end of September 2026, with unit one scheduled for December 2026. The €50m drag should disappear from 2027 onward. This is the most clearly reversible earnings headwind in the report, although the insurance and warranty recovery timeline is undisclosed.
10.
Reconciling conflicting figures: Four differences need explaining. The "€86/MWh" hedge reference is a conflation of two figures. The 86% is the 2026 hedge ratio, and the hedged price is €90.3/MWh. The gap to the €79.7/MWh achieved price is consistent with buybacks eroding realisations, so the hedge ratio and price are distinct. Gross and net buybacks measure different things. The €470m nine-month gross figure and the €120m net figure differ by optimization offsets. The brief's €196m H1 gross figure implies a calculated Q3 gross of about €274m. Grid growth figures use different bases. The €9bn APG programme carries a 14% RAB CAGR through 2033, whereas the regulatory asset base moves from €3.2bn to €3.7bn. These figures have different horizons and definitions and are not reconciled in the body. Ørsted's targeted 25% workforce reduction is a target. The 12% headcount reduction is delivery to date.
11.
Offsets and gaps: Cushions exist. The Sales segment reached €280m in nine months, flexibility guidance was raised to €310m, and regulated transmission could reach 22% of group EBITDA by 2030. The main gaps are that Verbund discloses only aggregate hedging ratios and prices, not contract-by-contract terms, along with unquantified concession relief and grid opex categories. The risks to the consensus view are a persistent dry cycle, fiscal dividend demands from the state owner and windfall-style levies targeting about €400m annually. Resolving them requires the 2026 annual report, further quarterly results and regulatory filings.

Section 1 – The Key Question and Why It Matters

Section summary
  1. Verbund faces significant earnings volatility because its low-cost alpine hydropower fleet possesses high operating leverage.

The Investment Thesis and Financial Risks

1.
Critical inquiry: "how much fixed-cost operating leverage does this business model carry, how much scope is there for it to work over the next 18 months to four years, and, where it is relevant to the company's strategy, how feasible is cost elimination or reduction, both incremental and in material large cuts?"
2.
Significant volatility: Verbund and its peer utilities face substantial fluctuations in earnings per share due to the highly sensitive relationship between achieved power prices and fixed-cost baseload hydro generation. For instance, a small shift in wholesale power prices can drive disproportionate changes in net income because hydro fleets run on near-zero variable operational costs. Consequently, understanding operating leverage is essential for investors projecting long-term capital distribution plans and potential share buybacks.
3.
Hydrological exposure: Hydroelectric generators face severe downside earnings risk during dry years when low water volumes force them to purchase expensive electricity on the spot market. These forced buybacks to fulfill forward hedges can quickly erode profit margins and trigger negative earnings revisions. Assessing how these buyback costs scale with hydrological shortfalls is critical for evaluating defensive cash flows during multiyear drought periods.
4.
Strategic hedging: High forward hedging ratios provide revenue visibility but amplify the risk of forced power buybacks when generation falls below contracted levels. Utilities like Verbund employ extensive hedging books to lock in power prices, yet volume-driven buybacks can severely hit financial results. Therefore, the balance between forward sales and volume risk is a primary driver of EPS stability in the sector.

Related Structural and Regulatory Themes

5.
Regulatory interference: National price caps and windfall taxes introduce asymmetrical downside risks that limit the earnings upside of merchant power generators. Furthermore, the grid segments operate under incentive regulation, where management's ability to retain cost-efficiency savings is determined by regulatory frameworks. This makes cost control in regulated grids a key driver of stable infrastructure earnings growth over the medium term.
6.
Inflexible costs: State-owned utilities often operate with highly rigid personnel expenses and rising depreciation charges from multi-billion capital expenditure plans. In Austria, universal collective bargaining coverage limits management's capacity to execute rapid, material workforce cost reductions. Investors must monitor whether cost-saving targets are realistic or undermined by inflationary pressure and salary adjustments.
7.
Accelerated expansion: Verbund's large capital program will significantly alter its cost profile by driving up depreciation charges as new assets are commissioned. This rising asset depreciation represents a growing fixed-cost burden that must be offset by volume growth or higher wholesale electricity prices. Consequently, the timing of commissioning plays a major role in shaping the group's margin trajectory over the next four years.
8.
Instructive benchmarking: Strategic cost-reduction programs executed by European peers like Fortum, Statkraft, and BKW provide essential historical precedents for cost flexibility. These past cases demonstrate that while corporate overhead and procurement costs can be trimmed, core operational and concession fees remain stubborn. These peer insights help clarify which cost lines are truly under management's control during down-cycles.
9.
Extensive research: This evaluation of operating leverage and cost flexibility analyzed 10 utility companies and 3 historical cost-reduction precedents across more than 100 industry documents. The research incorporates filings and transcripts from European peers to establish baseline operating benchmarks. Understanding these parameters helps clarify how earnings per share will evolve over the next four years.
10.
Other side of the story: While Verbund's fixed-cost hydro model carries high operating leverage, active dispatch optimization and strong grid segments can cushion earnings during dry years. The growing regulatory asset base of Austrian Power Grid offers highly stable, regulated cash flows that are independent of hydrological volatility. Consequently, structural grid growth and flexible pumped-storage capacity can substantially mitigate the overall downside risks to earnings per share.

Section 2 – Hydro Fleet Cost Structures and Fixed-Variable Splits

Section summary
  1. Alpine hydro fleets operate with highly rigid, predetermined fixed cost bases that are dominated by public water concessions and depreciation charges.
  2. Verbund exhibits superior profitability compared to peers because its hydro segment achieves an EBITDA margin of seventy-four point seven percent.
  3. Regulatory water rates in Alpine regions are highly inflexible and insulate public authorities from power market volatility at the expense of operators.

Alpine Hydropower Cost Splits

1.
Rigid structures: Alpine hydroelectric assets are characterized by extremely fixed cost allocations that are practically independent of generation volume. For example, Swiss operators pay a legal water rate of CHF 110 per kilowatt of gross capacity to cantons and municipalities. This specific charge alone represents roughly twenty percent of total production costs and matches the depreciation of the assets. Management cannot reduce this fee because water concessions are rigidly defined under national law.
2.
Sizable charges: Public fees and water rates impose a massive structural cost burden on Alpine hydro operators. Total charges paid to the public sector average 1.4 Rp. per kilowatt-hour, representing more than twenty-five percent of overall production expenses. These high levies weaken the competitiveness of Swiss hydropower plants relative to other generation technologies. Consequently, these fixed levies increase production costs significantly in dry years when generation volumes fall.
3.
Stubborn expenses: Variable costs at Alpine hydro plants are extremely low because water flows naturally and requires no fuel purchases. Apart from minor maintenance outlays, almost the entirety of operations and maintenance, personnel, and public fees are fixed. This structural cost setup means that operating leverage is exceptionally high for baseload hydro fleets. Thus, any drop in generation or wholesale prices directly harms the net earnings per share.
4.
Fixed concessions: Hydroelectric operators must pay substantial public taxes and concession fees regardless of the power market state. Nationwide, Swiss hydropower companies generate around CHF 2.2bn in annual market value but pay CHF 550m back in water rates. This represents a flat fee of around twenty percent of their overall revenues. Thus, these public fees act as a permanent drag on utility profitability over time.

Highly profitable: Verbund’s hydro segment shows exceptional margin strength due to its low variable operating costs. In 2025, the Austrian company’s hydro business generated €2,708.5m in total revenue and €2,024.3m in EBITDA. This implies an outstanding hydro EBITDA margin of seventy-four point seven percent (calculated). These strong results prove that the hydro fleet generates immense cash flow during normal operational years.

Comparative Peer Performance

8.
Superior margins: Verbund achieves a much higher EBITDA margin than diversified Nordic and European peers. While Verbund recorded a seventy-four point seven percent (calculated) hydro EBITDA margin, Fortum’s Generation segment delivered €1,098m of comparable EBITDA on €3,245m of sales in 2025. This translates to a lower peer margin of thirty-three point eight percent (calculated) for Fortum. The gap exists because Fortum’s segment includes nuclear and retail contracts with much higher variable cost elements than pure hydro.
9.
Varying returns: Uniper's Green Generation segment displays lower profit conversion than Verbund's hydro business. In 2025, Uniper's segment, which consists of Swedish nuclear and German and Swedish hydro, recorded Adjusted EBITDA of €626m on €2,512m of sales. This represents an Adjusted EBITDA margin of twenty-four point nine percent (calculated), which is significantly below Verbund’s segment profitability. This lower margin is driven by heavy Swedish nuclear O&M costs and asset retirement provisions.
10.
Stable expenses: BKW and Swiss peers face similar high fixed-cost structures due to Alpine water concession fees. For example, Swiss operators pay CHF 550m in annual water rates, which represents a highly rigid cost block. This fixed cost pressure remains high even when wholesale electricity market prices drop. Consequently, these Swiss utilities experience severe margin squeeze during dry years when generation volumes are low.

Operational and Capital Dynamics

11.
Capital intensive: Building and maintaining Alpine hydro fleets requires immense upfront investments but very low ongoing variable operational spending. For example, Iberdrola is progressing with the continent's largest pumped-storage plant in Ourense, Spain, requiring an investment of €1.5bn. Approximately €570m of this capital expenditure is dedicated to engineering and electromechanical equipment. These large projects lock in high depreciation charges that inflate the company's fixed cost base.
12.
Flexible options: Pumped-storage power plants provide operators with valuable optimization options that help offset fixed costs. Iberdrola and EDP leverage pumped-hydro assets to capture high peak electricity prices and stabilize margins. However, Iberdrola expects that growing battery installations will gradually begin to cannibalize pumped-hydro revenues. This technological shift could weaken the long-term profitability of flexible hydro fleets.
13.
Other side of the story: Although Alpine water rates are rigid, Swiss regulators can apply market premiums to support large-scale hydropower plants. Under Switzerland's Energy Act, operators selling electricity below production costs are entitled to financial subsidies. Furthermore, the sliding market premium provides a crucial safety net for new installations and major renovations. These regulatory subsidies can substantially cushion the fixed-cost burden of Swiss hydro operators.

Section 3 – Hydrological Volatility and Hedge-Driven Buyback Losses

Section summary
  1. Hydrological volatility in dry years causes massive earnings drops for Verbund and its peers due to forced power buybacks at elevated market prices.
  2. Severe droughts in 2022 and 2026 triggered substantial financial penalties for overhedged utilities that failed to produce their contracted baseload volumes.
  3. Diversified peers like Fortum and Hidroelectrica mitigate hydrological risks by shifting toward client- centric retail supply or active trading operations.

Hydrological Shock and Forced Buybacks at Verbund

1.
Severe drought: Verbund faced a major hydrological challenge during the historically dry year of 2022 when run- of-river generation volumes fell significantly below average. The company's hydro coefficient dropped to 0.86, representing a decline of 14 percentage points below the long-term historical norm. This sharp supply shortfall forced management to buy back electricity at high market prices to satisfy its pre-sold forward contracts. These forced transactions resulted in a massive negative EBITDA impact of 1,095 million euros for the full year.
2.
Lower production: Verbund experienced another dry period in 2025 when precipitation levels and water flows dropped significantly across Europe. The company reported a hydro coefficient of 0.79, which was 21 percentage points below the long-term average. This hydrological deficit reduced total generation from hydropower by 24.2% to 25,370 GWh. Consequently, the hydro segment's EBITDA fell by 31.8 percent to 2,024.3 million euros.
3.
Extreme dryness: Verbund suffered from historically unprecedented water scarcity in 2026 as Alpine river flows dropped to their lowest levels in a century. The company's hydro coefficient for run-of-river plants fell to 0.61 during the first nine months of the year. In the third quarter of 2026, the coefficient reached a low of 0.49, indicating extremely weak generation conditions. This persistent deficit led to a 29 percent drop in group EBITDA to 1,498.5 million euros.

Financial strain: Verbund incurred severe financial penalties in 2026 due to the compounding effect of low generation and elevated wholesale prices. Net buyback costs after offsetting optimization activities amounted to 120 million euros for the same period. These costly market repurchases depressed the average achieved sales price to 79.7 euros per megawatt-hour.

Comparative Operational Exposure of Peers

5.
Peers compared: Fortum faces similar volume-driven margin pressure during dry Nordic years when water reservoirs run low. The Finnish utility saw its Q3 hydro production drop to 2.9 TWh compared with 5.0 TWh in the previous year. This volume drop prevented the company from fully capturing the financial benefits of elevated spot electricity prices. Consequently, Fortum's generation segment comparable operating profit declined by 32 percent during the quarter.
6.
Nordic deficit: Fortum experienced a substantial reduction in its hydroelectric generation during the dry year of 2025. The company's hydro output fell to 17.8 TWh, which was significantly below its normal range of 20 to 20.5 TWh. This volume shortfall was accompanied by prolonged unplanned outages in its nuclear fleet. Therefore, these combined volume losses dragged down Fortum's operational performance despite positive price optimization.
7.
Contrary evidence: Verbund managed to upgrade its flexibility products guidance in 2026 despite experiencing extremely low run-of-river water flows. The Austrian utility raised its full-year flexibility earnings forecast to 310 million euros after delivering a strong performance. This optimization outperformance demonstrates that flexible storage assets can partly offset hydrological deficits by capturing peak prices.
8.
Swiss shortfalls: BKW AG has been repeatedly impacted by challenging Alpine hydrology that restricts its seasonal electricity generation. The Swiss utility's hydroelectric output fell by 15% to 3.3 TWh during the dry summer of 2022. Persistent drought conditions in 2026 further depressed BKW's water production and trading results. These hydrological headwinds slashed BKW's segment EBIT by 24.2% to 156.9 million Swiss francs.
9.
German weakness: Uniper SE experienced severe earnings pressure in its German hydroelectric operations during dry years. The German utility's Green Generation segment recorded an EBITDA decline to 350 million euros in 9M 2026. This financial underperformance was driven by exceptionally low water flows in Germany and nuclear outages in Sweden. Thus, Uniper's results show that unfavorable hydrology directly weakens profitability of conventional fleets.

Romanian Hydrology and Comparable Precedents

10.
Romanian drought: Hidroelectrica SA suffered a severe financial blow in 2025 when the Danube River flow dropped 40% below normal levels. The Romanian utility's net electricity production declined by 23% to 8.4 TWh during the first nine months. To meet its contractual supply obligations, the company had to buy 2.2 TWh of expensive electricity from the wholesale market. This forced buying program shrunk Hidroelectrica's net margin by 11 percentage points.
11.
Strategic pivot: Hidroelectrica SA reacted to hydrological volatility by expanding its retail supply business to capture more stable consumer demand. The company increased its retail market share to approximately 20% despite facing intense local competition. However, continuing low water levels in 2026 forced the company to purchase additional market power. These high procurement expenses offset the revenue benefits of retail expansion during dry summer months.
12.
Comparable past case: BKW AG provides a clear historical precedent of how alpine water shortages undermine utility margins during dry cycles. During the European energy crisis of 2022, BKW experienced a 15% reduction in hydropower output while wholesale spot prices surged. This combination forced the company to optimize its trading position under extreme market volatility. Ultimately, BKW's trading desk successfully hedged its production deficits through active market management.
13.
Other side of the story: While dry years present severe volume risks, they historically trigger sharp increases in wholesale power prices that benefit unhedged operators. Utilities with flexible reservoir assets can strategically shift generation to peak evening hours to capture maximum prices. This pricing uplift can fully compensate for lower generation volumes if the spot prices rise high enough.

Section 5 – Strategic Cost Reduction Programmes and Personnel Rigidity

Section summary
  1. Corporate cost-cutting initiatives among European utility peers demonstrate that substantial fixed-cost reductions require aggressive workforce downsizing and asset exits.
  2. Verbund exhibits high cost rigidity due to its state-owned status and collective bargaining agreements that drive persistent personnel expense growth.
  3. Strategic programs at Fortum, Ørsted, and Statkraft have successfully delivered hundreds of millions in savings but faced high restructuring charges.

Peer Cost-Cutting Programs and Execution

1.
Ambitious targets: Fortum Oyj initiated an efficiency programme targeting annual fixed-cost reductions of 100 million euros by the end of 2025. The company successfully delivered on this target, reducing its annual fixed costs to approximately 873 million euros. These operational improvements are expected to yield a steady run rate of 870 million euros in 2026, helping to offset Swedish property tax hikes. This discipline means Fortum is better positioned to sustain comparable operating profits.
2.
Efficient consolidation: Fortum Oyj achieved its structural cost savings by implementing brand mergers in its consumer Solutions division and restructuring finance functions. These targeted negotiations resulted in the elimination of 62 positions, highlighting the focus on corporate overhead. However, these administrative cuts represent only a small fraction of the group's total headcount of 4,654 employees. This implies that corporate functions offer far greater flexibility for reduction than core power generation roles.
3.
Aggressive downsizing: Statkraft AS pursued a comprehensive strategy to reduce its annual payroll and operating expenses by 2.9 billion Norwegian kroner by the end of 2026.
