APM Mini-Memo: Genmab

The one-line view. Genmab is a royalty-rich antibody company spending its franchise to buy its own medicines before the Darzalex royalties expire from 2029, and the case turns on three trial results due before the end of 2026.

What the company does

Genmab is a Danish antibody company whose proprietary platforms power eight approved medicines, a footprint far larger than its mid-sized biotech peers. Sales grew from 804 million dollars in 2019 to 3,720 million in 2025, but 79 percent of revenue is royalties on partners' sales, chiefly Johnson & Johnson's myeloma medicine Darzalex, which brings about 2.83 billion dollars of 2026 revenue at the guidance midpoint. The Darzalex United States patents expire in 2029 and the royalties are modelled to cease from 2032.

Genmab's answer is to become a commercial drug company in its own right. The 8.0 billion dollar purchase of Merus, completed in December 2025 and funded with 5.5 billion dollars of new debt, brought the wholly owned head and neck cancer antibody petosemtamab; alongside sit the wholly owned ovarian cancer medicine Rina-S and the lymphoma medicine Epkinly, sold with AbbVie.

A plausible investment thesis

The story is a dated race, and the market has priced the loser. Bulls and bears on the card agree that the share price values the legacy royalties and net cash and gives the late-stage pipeline almost nothing. Three trial results due in the fourth quarter of 2026 could change that: Epkinly in front-line diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, petosemtamab with Keytruda in first-line head and neck cancer, and Rina-S in platinum-resistant ovarian cancer. Management has raised its peak sales ambition for the three franchises to over 8.5 billion dollars combined, about three times today's Darzalex royalty.

The route to earnings per share runs through revenue, not margin. Consensus already has earnings growing about 58 percent a year, while operating margins are projected to fall from 31 percent in 2025 to 27 percent by 2028 as wholly owned launches replace near-costless royalties. Positive readouts turn that consensus into firm numbers and give the pipeline a terminal value the market now denies.

The drivers to track are few: the three readouts, the petosemtamab monotherapy trial due in the first quarter of 2027, and operating cash flow, which fell to 54 million dollars in the first half of 2026 from 349 million a year earlier because spending has run ahead of proof. The business itself is going well: second-quarter 2026 revenue rose 25 percent to 1,155 million dollars, beat consensus by 5 percent, and full-year guidance was raised to 4,325 to 4,525 million dollars.

Case Riskiness and simplicity

The case is not simple. The case depends on three independent binary events in one quarter, and working assumptions of 70 percent for Epkinly, 50 percent for petosemtamab and 75 percent for Rina-S give a joint clean-sweep probability of about 26 percent. One miss would undo the chart; Jefferies puts a petosemtamab failure at about 700 Danish kroner a share and a front-line Epkinly failure at about 260. Petosemtamab matters most, carrying the largest positive weight at the lowest probability, in a crowded field where rivals from Bicara and Johnson & Johnson have posted competitive response rates.

What needs to go right is at least two of the three, with petosemtamab among them, while the balance sheet holds: operating costs of 2.81 to 2.95 billion dollars, 3,119 staff and 5.5 billion dollars of debt were committed before the trials resolved. The sell-side's 93 percent Buy ratings beside a 15 to 51 dollar scenario ladder tell the same story: a wide, lumpy distribution, not a smooth expectation. In plain words, Genmab is an event-driven case, not a low-risk one.

Business quality

The platform is excellent and the economics show it. Gross margins run near 94 percent, capital spending was 1 percent of sales in 2025, and free cash flow reached 1,257 million dollars that year. The antibody platforms are embedded in partners' regulatory filings, so switching away would take years of revalidation; Novartis's Kesimpta and Johnson & Johnson's Darzalex are the proof.

The moat and the cliff are the same asset. The switching costs protect each molecule, not the company, and expire with the molecules' patents. The adjusted margin of earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation has already fallen from 52 percent in 2019 to 31 percent in 2025, and return on invested capital dropped from 40 percent in 2024 to 12 percent in 2025 after the Merus deal. Management is a conservative tracker on financial guidance, with three years of over-delivery, so the guidance can be trusted; the clinical record is mixed, so the trial odds cannot be.

Value chain and thematics: the pros and the cons

The pros: Epkinly demand accelerated in United States community practices after the 24-hour hospital monitoring requirement was removed from its label; rival bispecifics and cell therapies have hit regulatory setbacks; and the pipeline is insulated from Inflation Reduction Act price negotiation through its biologic status. The weekly Thematic Fit/Momentum rating of 30 October is B, and my Sector Digest of 22 October named Genmab's three trials as a single-company story to look at, in a Sector whose median stock trades at the 22nd percentile of its ten-year range.

The cons: head and neck cancer is crowded; Roche's Polivy already holds 39 percent of United States front-line lymphoma and Roche's Lunsumio competes abroad; European reimbursement is slow; supply relies on single-site contract manufacturing; and the Sector's net thematic force on 23 October was C, weak, with failed trials the main listed threat.
