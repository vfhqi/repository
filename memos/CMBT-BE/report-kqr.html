Section 1 – The Key Question and Why It Matters

Section summary
  1. Geopolitical disruptions and shipyard capacity constraints will dictate the multi-year earnings trajectory for major European shipping operators.
1.
Investigating shipping dynamics: "For crude tankers and dry bulk carriers, how much of 2026 freight rates comes from rerouting around the Red Sea and the Strait of Hormuz and from sanctions, and what rates do brokers model for 2027 and 2028 if ships return to the Suez route? With 73% of crude tanker orders delivering in 2028 or later and a dry bulk order book of 14.5% of the fleet, how many old or sanctioned ships are expected to be scrapped, and at what rates would owners order new ships while shipyards are full until 2029?"

earnings sensitivity and risks

2.
Cash generation potential: Geopolitical disruptions in the Middle East have significantly elevated spot earnings for crude tankers. According to CMB.TECH's Q2 2026 earnings report, Middle East disruptions drove spot VLCC earnings to historically high levels averaging USD 140,029 per day. This windfall supports high shareholder distributions, but threatens long-term earnings stability once transport routes eventually reopen. This means that a sudden normalization of routes would quickly compress the company's spot earnings.
3.
Shifting operator strategy: Dry bulk and product tanker operators must navigate extremely volatile freight rate environments. Dampskibsselskabet NORDEN's CEO Jan Rindbo noted in the Q4 2025 earnings call that shipyard order books are full until 2029. This capacity constraint means NORDEN must optimize its existing fleet to protect its margins, unless global demand declines sharply. Consequently, the timing of shipyard slot availability is critical for NORDEN's long-term asset-light strategy.
4.
Container network changes: Major container carriers face severe S/D imbalances due to massive fleet expansions. Bernstein's Q4 2026 logistics report indicates that Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd face a twenty percent net fleet growth over 2027 and 2028. This supply surge is highly likely to depress container freight rates, unless Red Sea routing disruptions persist indefinitely. As a result, both companies remain highly vulnerable to rate normalization over the next three years.

broader shipping sector implications

5.
Restructured shipping routes: Related questions focus on how quickly carrier networks can normalize if the Suez route stabilizes. Morgan Stanley's report on July 6 2026 indicates that Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd began gradually returning selected services to the Red Sea. A full return would eliminate the route deviation around Africa, so this would release effective capacity and place severe downward pressure on global container shipping rates.
6.
Enforced vessel sanctions: Sanction enforcement on shadow fleets represents another critical variable for investor modeling. DNB Carnegie highlights that older, non-compliant shadow vessels are clear candidates for scrapping if global sanctions are successfully enforced. The removal of these vessels would tighten the compliant fleet supply, but would elevate freight rates for compliant mainstream operators.
7.
Shipyard slot constraints: Investors must also evaluate how full shipyards will affect fleet replacement and carbon regulations. DNB Carnegie's report on September 9 2026 notes that forward shipyard cover has risen to over four years. High newbuilding prices may prevent speculative orders, which means operators must keep older vessels in service longer than previously modeled.
8.
Other side of the story: Investors may underestimate the structural benefits of the new carrier alliances on asset utilization. Jefferies argues that the Gemini Cooperation between Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd has lifted schedule reliability above ninety percent. This improved efficiency can protect profit margins, so it can support earnings even if freight rates normalize back to historical averages.

Section 2 – Route Disruptions and Sanctions Impact on 2026 Rates

Section summary
  1. Geopolitical route disruptions and sanctions have severely restricted vessel capacity, pushing 2026 freight rates to record highs.
  2. Rapidly normalizing Suez transits and shadow fleet sanctions easing represent significant downside risks to the 2026 rate premium.

transit deviations and cargo rerouting

1.
Suezmax route alterations: Geopolitical conflicts have forced long-haul rerouting around Africa, which consumes vessel capacity. CMB.TECH's interim report on August 27 2026 notes that these disruptions have forced shipowners to use longer alternative routes via the Cape of Good Hope. Consequently, these longer voyages and extra tonne-miles drive up daily spot rates for Suezmax and VLCC tankers, which directly enhances the company's spot earnings per share.
2.
Escalating transport costs: Rerouting around the Cape of Good Hope has created substantial cost headwinds for container shipping lines. Hapag-Lloyd's press release on August 14 2026 reports that the blockage of the Strait of Hormuz resulted in additional costs for bunker, insurance, and storage. This means that although freight rates remain high, these elevated operational costs will weigh on near-term profitability unless routing conditions normalize.
3.
Dry bulk route standardization: Dry bulk carriers are also standardizing routes around the Cape of Good Hope as Red Sea disruptions persist. Jadroplov's annual report on September 11 2026 reveals that this shift effectively reduces available fleet capacity because vessels must spend more time at sea. Consequently, dry bulk operators like NORDEN will see prolonged rate support, which should protect their asset-light margins from a steep near-term decline.
4.
Vessel repositioning costs: Ballast voyage inefficiencies have further reduced the global tanker fleet's productivity. Morgan Stanley's report on September 22 2026 highlights that repeated vessel repositioning between the Atlantic and Asia consumes critical ballast days and restricts fleet flexibility. This means that effective supply is tighter than nominal fleet growth suggest, which will support high spot rates until trade routes settle.

supply chain tightness and capacity constraints

5.
Inefficient shipping operations: Logistical inefficiencies have further reduced the productivity of the global tanker fleet. Morgan Stanley's report on September 22 2026 argues that increased ship-to-ship transfers and longer waiting times are consuming massive vessel capacity in the Middle East. This means that even as Middle East crude exports recover, the structural capacity tightness will keep spot rates elevated, unless vessel turnaround times improve significantly.
6.
Container capacity absorption: Robust consumer demand has absorbed the massive influx of new container ship deliveries. Jefferies Research's report on September 18 2026 indicates that supply chain disruptions and port congestion have successfully absorbed the projected oversupply of container vessels. Consequently, these factors will support Hapag-Lloyd's and Maersk's earnings throughout 2026, but the threat of future oversupply remains clear once normal routes reopen.
7.
Suez transit recovery: Contrary evidence: A rapid return of container liners to the Suez Canal is already releasing effective market capacity. Morgan Stanley's tracker on October 5 2026 reports that containership transits surged to fifty-four percent of pre-disruption levels. This rapid normalisation is highly likely to exert severe downward pressure on Asia-Europe freight rates, which will ultimately compress container shipping earnings over the coming quarters.

historical precedents and peer comparisons

8.
Panama canal precedents: Historical weather disruptions illustrate how quickly shipping capacity can recover once environmental constraints ease. CMB.TECH's presentation on August 27 2026 recalls that the 2023 El Niño drought restricted Panama Canal transits and doubled or tripled charter rates within six months. This suggests that current Middle East geopolitical disruptions provide a temporary earnings cushion, but rates will quickly revert to historical averages once maritime corridors reopen.
9.
Suezmax spot outperformance: Spot crude tanker rates have reached historic highs compared to past averages. CMB.TECH's press release on August 27 2026 shows that Suezmax spot earnings averaged USD 146,567 per day in Q2 2026. This is significantly higher than the ten-year historical average of USD 30,946 per day, which means that the current geopolitical premium is the primary driver of the company's record earnings.
10.
Container freight comparisons: Container freight indices remain volatile but historically elevated compared to pre- crisis levels. Bernstein Research's report on October 9 2026 notes that the Shanghai Containerized Freight Index is currently up 177 percent versus pre-conflict levels. This elevated level will support Hapag- Lloyd's upgraded 2026 EBITDA guidance, although the benefit will fade as Suez transits continue to accelerate.
11.
VLCC rate premium: The current crude tanker spot rate premium exceeds past historical shocks. ING Wholesale Banking's report on October 7 2026 states that this year's crude tanker earnings spike is beyond levels ever seen before. This means that current rates are highly unsustainable, so investors must prepare for a rapid EPS decline once the Middle East conflict is resolved.

regulatory sanctions and shadow fleet impacts

12.
Sanctioned tanker fleet: Sanctions on non-compliant vessels have structurally restricted the supply of available tankers. Morgan Stanley's oil manual on October 13 2026 states that Clarksons counts 982 tankers under sanctions, representing seventeen percent of fleet deadweight tonnage. This means the compliant market has very little spare capacity, so any further sanctions on the shadow fleet will keep crude tanker rates elevated.

Shadow fleet integration: Easing sanctions on the shadow fleet represents a major downside risk for compliant shipping operators. ING Wholesale Banking's report on October 16 2026 notes that the United States is considering easing sanctions on Russian tankers to reduce global fuel costs. This initiative would release significant tanker capacity back into the mainstream market, which would severely depress spot freight rates and undermine CMB.TECH's crude division earnings.

US naval protection: Military escorts for non-compliant transits have unexpectedly improved crude flows. Deutsche Bank Research's report on September 21 2026 indicates that US Centcom is providing security to dark transits along the Omani coastline. This means that more crude is bypassing the blockade via ship-toship transfers, which could cap the geopolitical rate premium sooner than the market expects.

Section 3 – Broker Rate Forecasts for 2027 and 2028 Upon Route Reopening

Section summary
  1. Broker rate models diverge sharply on the pace of shipping rate normalization in 2027 and 2028 if routes reopen.
  2. Underwriting normalisation assumptions suggests significant downside risk to consensus EPS for asset-heavy operators.

diverging tanker rate normalization forecasts

1.
Elevated baseline projections: Morgan Stanley’s note of September 22 2026 assumes market-level VLCC TCEs of USD 200,000 per day for 2027 and USD 150,000 per day for 2028. These base case forecasts sit significantly above historical ten-year averages because persistent structural disruptions are expected to linger. Easing Middle East tensions would reset sentiment, but long-haul crude diversification should support these premium levels. Consequently, these robust rates will preserve the sector's elevated earnings power.
2.
Moderate rate assumptions: DNB Carnegie’s report of August 28 2026 takes a more cautious stance on long-term tanker rates. The broker raised its 2027 adjusted EBITDA estimate for CMB.TECH by only 1% while lowering its 2028 forecast by 2%. This cautious modeling places DNB Carnegie 13% below Bloomberg consensus EBITDA for 2027 and 12% below for 2028. Unless OPEC+ rapidly increases volumes, fading geopolitical tailwinds will compress future earnings.
3.
Contrary evidence: Morgan Stanley’s shipping update of October 13 2026 suggests that a peace deal in the Middle East would accelerate tanker rate normalization far faster than the consensus expects. The broker maintains a bear case where slower global economic growth and refined product restocking pull rates down to historical averages. This means that a rapid resumption of Strait of Hormuz transits would severely threaten the multi-year bull story.
4.
Sanctions release risks: ING Wholesale Banking’s report of October 16 2026 indicates that policy shifts present immediate downside risks. The broker highlights that the potential easing of US sanctions on Russian tankers would release significant non-compliant capacity. This supply injection would quickly deflate spot rates, so it would undermine compliant fleet earnings. As a result, any political breakthrough would trigger a rapid downward revision in broker models.

dry bulk normalization expectations

5.
Optimistic dry bulk outlook: Jefferies Research's report of December 4 2025 forecasts highly favorable dry bulk supply and demand dynamics. The broker raised its Capesize rate forecasts to USD 30,000 per day for 2027 and USD 32,500 per day for 2028. These projections reflect the impact of long-haul bauxite demand and the Simandou project. These structural tailwinds mean that dry bulk operators will see consistent earnings growth even if routes normalize.
6.
Conservative dry bulk estimates: DNB Carnegie’s note of February 18 2026 models a much flatter dry bulk rate trajectory. The broker expects Capesize rates of USD 22,500 per day in 2026, which is lower than Jefferies' USD 27,500 projection. These sideways rate movements mean that current secondhand asset values are unsustainable. Consequently, the broker expects substantial downside risk to vessel valuations if trade routes normalize.
7.
Handymax rate baseline: Morgan Stanley’s report of October 16 2026 models Handymax realized rates averaging USD 17,200 per day in 2027. This base case assumes that dry bulk shipping rates stay strong in response to constrained supply. However, the broker's bear case models a 10% rate decline under a weaker global economy. This means that easing Middle East tensions could offset the bauxite-driven tonne-mile gains.

earnings impact on cmbtech and norden

8.
CMB.TECH earnings degradation: DNB Carnegie’s report of June 22 2026 highlights the negative trajectory of CMB.TECH’s projected earnings. The broker forecasts adjusted EPS of USD 2.19 for 2026, dropping to USD 0.92 for 2027 and USD 0.28 for 2028. This rapid decline reflects the normalisation of the company's tanker earnings. This means that the company's heavy dry bulk capital expenditures will drag on cash generation as rates soften.
9.
NORDEN portfolio positioning: Dampskibsselskabet NORDEN’s annual report of February 4 2026 outlines its forward fleet coverage strategy. The company maintains a fairly large open position in dry bulk for 2027 onwards to capture rising markets. This asset-light operator has over thirty newbuildings delivering from 2027 to optimize returns. This means that NORDEN’s EPS remains highly sensitive to how fast Capesize rates normalize.
10.
Pan Ocean structural growth: NH Investment & Securities' note of October 12 2026 indicates that Pan Ocean is successfully diversifying into tankers. The broker expects the tanker division to account for 34% of total operating profit in 2027. Long-term VLCC contracts are expected to generate over one hundred billion won in annual operating profit. This stable contracted revenue means Pan Ocean can grow its EPS even if dry bulk rates normalize.
11.
Other side of the story: Some analysts argue that shipyard capacity constraints will support shipping earnings even if routes normalize. DNB Carnegie's report of September 9 2026 notes that shipyard forward cover has risen to 4.3 years. This extreme backlog means that the earliest possible delivery slot for larger vessels is 2030. Consequently, this prolonged supply constraint will prevent a complete rate collapse, which supports long-term EPS.

Section 4 – Fleet Demographics and Scrapping Expectations

Section summary
  1. The global shipping fleet faces multi-decade high average ages, but extremely strong freight rates and the shadow fleet's absorption of older vessels have kept scrapping at historical lows.
  2. We have high confidence that structural fleet renewal needs will prevent a multi-year rate collapse, supported by shipyard slots being fully booked through 2029.

crude tanker supply and demographics

1.
Aging crude tankers: The global crude tanker fleet is experiencing multi-decade high average ages due to muted demolition activity. According to the report by ING Wholesale Banking on October 7 2026, the average age of the crude fleet has reached over 13 years. This means that structural replacement demand is rising rapidly, which will support elevated secondhand asset values unless shipowners delay retirement. Consequently, this demographic pressure will bolster long-term tanker segment profitability as old vessels face obsolescence.
2.
VLCC replacement deficit: Significant long-term vessel replacement is required to offset the aging VLCC fleet despite the recent influx of new orders. DNB Carnegie’s sector note of September 9 2026 highlights that 114 million deadweight tons of VLCCs will be 20 years or older by 2030. This aging capacity represents 33% of the current fleet, whereas the global orderbook stands at only 95 million deadweight tons. Therefore, the persistent replacement deficit will support newbuild prices, which means higher asset values for companies with modern fleets.
3.
Orderbook delivery schedules: The supply pressure from the expanding tanker orderbook is concentrated heavily in the outer years. Deutsche Bank Research’s report of October 5 2026 indicates that the crude tanker orderbookto-fleet ratio has recovered to 28.2%. However, close to 73% of this current orderbook is scheduled to deliver in 2028 or beyond. This delayed delivery schedule means that near-term tanker capacity will remain exceptionally tight, preserving high spot freight rates and boosting operators' earnings until those ships arrive.

dry bulk fleet and replacement needs

4.
Dry bulk orderbook: Dry bulk carriers display the most attractive supply fundamentals across the entire maritime shipping sector. Deutsche Bank Research's update of September 23 2026 states that the dry bulk orderbook-tofleet ratio stands at just 14.4%. While this ratio is trending slightly above its trailing five-year average, over half of the current orders will deliver in 2028 or later. This highly disciplined supply growth means that dry bulk rates should remain highly resilient, shielding listed owners from oversupply unless global demand collapses.
5.
Deteriorating bulk demographics: The global dry bulk fleet is aging rapidly because sustained strong freight markets have discouraged shipowners from scrapping older vessels. Deutsche Bank Research's report of July 17 2026 notes that the average dry bulk fleet age has climbed to 13 years. This represents the highest average fleet age since 2010, yet actual demolition activity remains extremely muted. Consequently, dry bulk operators must eventually undergo massive fleet renewal programs, which will drain corporate cash reserves but prevent sudden rate collapses.
6.
Scrapping model constraints: Historical evidence shows that dry bulk scrapping has shifted from a pure rate- driven decision to a regulatory compliance requirement. DNB Carnegie's report of March 6 2019 explains that ballast water treatment mandates forced massive compliance costs on vessels entering their special surveys. This regulatory tightening meant that owners of older vessels preferred recycling over paying for expensive retrofits. Similarly, upcoming carbon intensity regulations are expected to force inefficient vintage bulkers to scrap, which will tighten compliant vessel supply.

shadow fleet and scrapping candidates

7.
Shadow fleet build-up: The creation of a massive parallel shadow fleet has fundamentally altered global tanker demographics and delayed normal ship recycling. DNB Carnegie's initiating report on September 9 2026 reveals that shadow vessels account for 21% of total tanker capacity. These non-compliant vessels have an average age of 21 years compared to just 13 years for compliant tankers. This massive age gap means that the shadow fleet represents an unprecedented backlog of scrapping candidates, which will distort normal fleet attrition until sanctions tighten.
8.
Sanctions enforcement leverage: Successful enforcement of maritime sanctions represents the most powerful catalyst to accelerate the scrapping of older tankers. Morgan Stanley's report of August 17 2026 notes that tanker scrapping has remained near historical lows because old ships are absorbed into illicit trades. However, tighter global sanctions would marginalize these older vessels and push them directly toward scrap yards. This recycling surge would substantially reduce global tanker supply, which would drive mainstream compliant freight rates and operator earnings higher.
9.
Contrary evidence: The potential return of the shadow fleet to mainstream compliant trade could ease global capacity constraints and delay expected vessel scrapping. Morgan Stanley's report of October 15 2026 indicates that the United States is considering allowing the use of shadow fleet tankers. Such a regulatory shift would allow older non-compliant vessels to reintegrate into the white fleet rather than being retired. This reintegration would severely depress spot freight rates, which would undermine the compliant fleet's earnings.

Section 5 – Newbuilding Prices and Shipyard Capacity Constraints

Section summary
  1. Severe shipyard capacity constraints will restrict vessel supply and sustain elevated asset values through 2029.
  2. Elevated vessel prices require historically high daily charter rates to justify new orders, discouraging speculative capacity additions.
  3. Regulatory uncertainty and high capital premiums are pushing shipowners toward more flexible, lower-capex dual-fuel ready vessel designs.

shipyard capacity and delivery schedules

1.
Constrained shipyard slots: Global shipbuilding capacity remains exceptionally tight, stretching forward delivery lead times into 2028 and beyond. According to a Deutsche Bank Research report on September 4 2024, shipyard slot availability is heavily constrained because ordering in other segments keeps slots elusive. This capacity bottleneck means that dry bulk operators face delayed fleet renewal, which will support elevated charter rates for several quarters unless global trade demand falls.
2.
Shipyard slot scarcity: Extreme shipyard utilization has significantly increased the bargaining power of shipbuilders over shipowners. A former senior supply chain consultancy advisor at Shell stated on March 13 2026 that slot scarcity can push newbuilding prices up, adding a premium of twenty to fifty percent for immediate slots. This means that operators must plan and book capacity up to eighteen months in advance, or they will face escalating capital costs that weigh heavily on long-term earnings.
3.
Divergent fleet expansion: Different ordering strategies among listed peers highlight how operators are navigating the tight shipyard slot environment. EuroDry announced on October 14 2026 that it ordered its third modern Kamsarmax bulk carrier for thirty-seven million dollars with delivery scheduled for late 2029. Meanwhile, CMB.TECH’s annual report on April 21 2026 stated it had forty-one vessels under construction to accelerate its green transition. This means CMB.TECH will grow its nearterm earnings per share faster than slower-moving peers like EuroDry.
4.
Contrary evidence: Chinese shipyard expansions present a medium-term threat of oversupply that could eventually undermine pricing power. NH Investment & Securities reported on October 12 2026 that Chinese shipbuilders, led by Hengli Shipyard, are pursuing aggressive capacity expansion which has lifted projected deliveries for 2028 to ninety-eight million gross tons. This capacity surge will eventually depress global vessel asset values, unless the industry maintains strict newbuilding ordering discipline.

vessel pricing and charter parity thresholds

5.
Escalating asset values: Strong ordering across maritime segments has pushed global newbuilding prices to historical cycle highs. Huatai Research noted on September 28 2026 that newbuild prices have held at cycle highs, while much of the active fleet was contracted years ago at far lower prices. This elevated asset pricing means that operators must generate substantial spot earnings to justify new orders, discouraging a rush of speculative contracting.
6.
Tanker parity thresholds: Shipowners face exceptionally high charter rate requirements to justify ordering new tankers at current prices. A DNB Carnegie report on May 7 2026 calculated that a VLCC newbuild requires an equilibrium charter rate of fifty-four thousand dollars per day to yield a ten percent unlevered return. This high threshold means that crude tanker earnings per share will remain highly sensitive to spot market volatility, unless operators secure lucrative long-term contracts.
7.
Substantial resale premiums: Massive demand for immediately available vessels has pushed secondhand resale prices far above newbuilding costs. Morgan Stanley reported on October 5 2026 that a resale VLCC is valued at a record two hundred and five million dollars, representing a seventy-four million dollar premium to a newbuild. This extreme resale premium means that operators can generate near-term windfall profits by selling older tonnage, but it raises the capital barrier for fleet expansion.
8.
Historical price stagnation: Historical shipping cycles show how newbuilding prices can flatten when seasonal buying activity cools down. Jefferies Research reported on September 3 2017 that the Clarkson newbuilding price index had been stagnant since May due to a weak season for orders. This past case suggests that current elevated newbuilding prices could flatten or decrease modestly if current geopolitical disruptions ease, which would reduce the capital expenditure pressure on operators.

alternative propulsion and fuel premiums

9.
Decarbonization premium costs: Adopting alternative fuel propulsion systems adds massive capital expenditure requirements to standard newbuilding contracts. Jefferies Research reported on October 20 2025 that ordering an alternative fuel alternative fuel capable vessel adds ten to fifteen million dollars to the construction cost. This capital premium will drag on net profit margins for asset-heavy operators, unless global regulators enforce a carbon tax.
10.
Shifting propulsion strategies: Shipowners are downgrading their vessel specifications to mitigate high capital costs following regulatory delays. Jefferies Research noted on October 20 2025 that many shipowners are shifting dual-fuel capable orders to dual-fuel ready designs to lower upfront capex by nearly fifteen million dollars. This strategic pivot means that operators can preserve cash, but they risk future compliance penalties if carbon regulations tighten rapidly.
11.
Pioneering green bulkers: Leading European listed operators are actively deploying zero-emission designs to secure long-term commercial advantages. CMB.TECH announced on October 9 2026 the delivery of the Mineral Europa, which is the world's first ammonia-powered dry bulk vessel. This strategic investment means that CMB.TECH will establish early cost leadership in low-carbon logistics, while less proactive peers will struggle with rising emissions compliance costs.
12.
Stable gas contracting: Shipowners are securing long-term charters for alternative fuel gas carriers to insulate their cash flows from spot market volatility. DNB Carnegie noted on February 19 2026 that ordering a newbuild LNG carrier for two hundred and forty-eight million dollars and fixing it on a seven-year charter yields an attractive return. This structured approach means that operators can lock in predictable earnings, whereas uncontracted vessel acquisitions remain highly speculative.
13.
Other side of the story: Easing newbuilding demand could quickly erode shipyard pricing power and lower vessel replacement costs. DNB Carnegie argued on May 7 2026 that high asset prices could eventually dissuade shipowners from placing new orders, which would force shipyards to narrow their profit margins to secure backlogs. This means that a sudden drop in global trade growth would deflate newbuild prices, allowing operators to renew their fleets at much more attractive rates.
