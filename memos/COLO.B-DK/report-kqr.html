Section 1 – The Key Question and Why It Matters

Section summary
  1. The looming 2028 Medicare competitive bidding round and Coloplast's strategic reset under a new chief executive officer represent pivotal binary catalysts.
  2. These events will directly dictate mid-term organic growth, margin expansion, and valuation multiples for leading European medical consumables companies.

Defining the Key Question

1.
Defined inquiry: "For Coloplast and Convatec, how large a price cut do brokers and United States distribution experts now expect from the national Medicare competitive bidding round for ostomy and urological supplies that starts on 1 January 2028, how much of that cut would fall on the manufacturers rather than the distributors, and is it already in consensus earnings estimates for 2028? Separately, what medium-term targets do brokers expect Coloplast's new chief executive to set at the November 2026 reset: a low bar that later results can beat, or a further cut to growth and margin?"

Valuation and Reimbursement Risks

2.
Regulatory overhang: The threat of national competitive bidding in the United States represents the single largest regulatory hurdle for chronic care manufacturers. Morgan Stanley highlights that while the potential for competitive bidding has historically depressed valuation multiples, any actual price cuts starting in 2028 could structurally alter long-term margins. These regulatory developments will directly shape long-term earnings per share trajectories for both Coloplast and Convatec.
3.
Consensus deficits: Broker consensus only partially captures the potential headwind from the competitive bidding program. Morgan Stanley notes that if the program is implemented as scheduled, it could result in a material headwind to earnings estimates. Consequently, the exact split of price cuts between manufacturers and distributors remains a critical unknown for investors.
4.
Valuation pressure: Competitive bidding has driven chronic care valuation premiums down to historic lows. Jefferies emphasizes that until contracts are awarded and prices are published, companies cannot precisely estimate the financial impact. However, the clearing of this regulatory hurdle remains the primary catalyst for a significant sector re-rating.

Leadership Resets and Transition Risks

5.
Strategic realignment: Coloplast's upcoming strategic update represents a major clearing event for the stock. Morgan Stanley expects new Chief Executive Officer Gavin Wood to prioritize growth over margin when he announces his updated strategy. However, any significant margin reset to fund this expansion could lead to near-term earnings downgrades.
6.
EBITDA pressure: Many market participants expect Coloplast to lower its mid-term targets. Deutsche Bank reports that investors are heavily focused on how the new leadership will reallocate existing resources to accelerate growth in the United States. Funding these investments through resource reallocation should ease concerns about near-term margin pressure.
7.
Performance concerns: Coloplast's historical execution was a lesson in shareholder value creation but recent performance has lagged. Rothschild & Co Redburn highlights that Coloplast's financial performance has consistently tracked below its long-term organic growth targets. Therefore, establishing a credible growth algorithm is necessary to restore investor credibility and rebuild valuation premiums.

Medical Distribution Dynamics

8.
Consolidation trends: The continuous consolidation of United States medical distribution channels shifts the balance of power. A Field Vice President at McKesson Corporation notes that major distributors are expanding their logistics capabilities to deliver products directly to patients. This consolidation trend increases execution risks for European consumables companies that rely on distributor relationships.
9.
Operational hurdles: Smith & Nephew faces unique operational challenges in its orthopedics and wound biologics segments. Morgan Stanley notes that underperformance in key assets continues to weigh heavily on longterm operating profit growth. Unless the company executes a major guidance rebase, valuation discounts relative to European medtech peers are likely to persist.
10.
Other side of the story: A bipartisan United States bill to delay competitive bidding for five years could remove regulatory overhangs completely. Morgan Stanley suggests that Coloplast's low direct sales exposure to Medicare means its underlying businesses are mostly immune. If the legislative delay succeeds, these chronic care assets could experience a sharp re-rating irrespective of leadership transition concerns.

Section 2 – Expected Impact and Price Cuts from the 2028 Medicare Competitive

Section summary
  1. Consensus expectations suggest national Medicare competitive bidding will introduce steep price cuts for ostomy and urological supplies in 2028.
  2. The financial impact will vary significantly because manufacturer exposure and distributor channel dynamics dictate how much pricing pressure is passed upstream.

Bidding Round

Projected Scale of Medicare Cuts

1.
Sweeping pricing pressure: Broker expectations point to significant retail price declines across the US chronic care segment. Bernstein's note of 28 January 2026 models a -30% retail price decline for ostomy and continence supplies under the competitive bidding program. This pricing compression is driven by CMS efforts to align Medicare rates with lower private payer levels, which historically paid 18% to 57% less than Medicare for similar medical supplies. Consequently, this shift will compress gross margins across the industry unless manufacturers can find substantial cost offsets or volume gains.
2.
Corporate revenue guidelines: Convatec has provided explicit estimates regarding the financial impact of the upcoming competitive bidding program. Convatec's disclosures cited in Morgan Stanley's report of 13 October 2026 project a 1-2% reduction in group sales during the implementation year. This guidance assumes that the program takes effect on 1 January 2028 as scheduled. Consequently, this headwind represents a key hurdle for the company's mid-term targets, although the ultimate impact depends on how much pricing pressure is passed upstream.
3.
Substantial earnings headwinds: Morgan Stanley has quantified the potential earnings headwind for Convatec from the competitive bidding program. Morgan Stanley's note of 13 October 2026 estimates a 3-8% headwind to consensus 2028 earnings per share for Convatec. This calculation assumes a sales headwind of $30 million to $60 million and a high operating profit drop-through of 70% to 90%. Therefore, failure to delay the program poses a material risk to baseline earnings expectations, which could lead to near-term earnings downgrades.
4.
Insulated direct exposure: Coloplast appears to be relatively insulated from direct Medicare bidding risks. Jefferies argues in its note of 18 August 2026 that Coloplast is widely immune to the program because it has only 6% direct Medicare sales exposure. This limited exposure stems from its low level of direct US distribution activity compared to peers. Nevertheless, Coloplast cannot provide a precise financial estimate until CMS publishes final contract terms, which means some regulatory uncertainty will persist.

Company Specific Impact Scenarios

5.
Limited sales disruption: Bernstein's proprietary analysis supports the view that Coloplast faces minor revenue disruption. Bernstein's report of 29 August 2025 calculates that Coloplast's US chronic care exposure represents only 12% of total group sales. Of this exposure, half is related to Medicare while the rest belongs to private payers. This minor exposure means any price cuts will have a negligible impact on Coloplast's overall growth algorithm, unless pricing cuts spill over to private payers.
6.
Divergent regulatory exposure: Smith & Nephew faces a different set of reimbursement dynamics than its chronic care peers. Bernstein's report of 4 June 2026 explains that Smith & Nephew's primary exposure lies in US wound biologic skin substitutes rather than ostomy or urological supplies. While Medicare has proposed steep flat-rate payment cuts for skin substitutes, these are separate from the competitive bidding program. This means Smith & Nephew is unaffected by the urological bidding rounds starting in 2028, though it faces its own pricing cuts.
7.
Capped pricing structures: Scenario modeling in related medtech categories shows how Medicare caps can squeeze manufacturers. Deutsche Bank's report of 14 October 2026 models a 15% pricing cut for DexCom continuous glucose monitors under competitive bidding. This model assumes that Medicare Advantage plans will cap their reimbursement at these new fee-for-service levels. Consequently, this spillover effect would amplify the overall pricing pressure felt by manufacturers across all Medicare channels, creating downside risk to long-term earnings per share.

This bill aims to give CMS five years to study the access impact on highly personalized products. Consequently, the legislative process creates a major binary catalyst that will shape investor sentiment over the next 12 months.

Rothschild & Co Redburn's report of 19 August 2026 notes that average Medicare spending on CPAP devices fell by 45% after competitive bidding was introduced. This severe pricing erosion drove massive distributor consolidation during the 2010s. Therefore, historical precedents suggest that unmitigated bidding represents a substantial structural threat to medtech margins unless clinical factors can insulate pricing.
11.
Partial pricing insulation: CPAP manufacturers managed to shield their gross margins during prior competitive bidding rounds. Rothschild & Co Redburn's note of 19 August 2026 points out that CPAP manufacturers did not experience this price dilution in full. Instead, much of the reimbursement decline was absorbed by the medical equipment distributor channel. This past case suggests that strong manufacturer market share can help defend realized prices, meaning direct earnings impact may be less than retail cuts imply.

Timing and Implementation Timelines

12.
Impending regulatory milestones: The regulatory timeline for the 2028 competitive bidding program is rapidly approaching. Regulatory news from WTWH Media on 14 October 2026 indicates that bidder registration opens 1 December 2026, with the bidding window launching on 5 January 2027. These operational milestones mean manufacturers must quickly prepare their pricing strategies. Unless the Murphy delay bill passes, these deadlines will lock in pricing terms for multiple years.

Clinical customization concerns: Patient advocacy groups argue that ostomy and urological supplies are poorly suited for competitive bidding. The Wound, Ostomy, and Continence Nurses Society urges CMS to preserve clinical choice to prevent patient complications. Therefore, these clinical concerns are the primary force driving the bipartisan legislative pushback.
14.
Operational execution focus: Analysts believe that regulatory overhangs have already been largely discounted by the market. Jefferies Research emphasizes on 18 October 2026 that the equity story has pivoted toward a scalable operating platform focused on higher organic growth and margin expansion. While the market remains cautious about competitive bidding, scale advantages should help leaders navigate these changes. This shift means operational execution will drive earnings growth regardless of short-term regulatory adjustments.
15.
Valuation multiple compression: Regulatory uncertainty has historically depressed valuation multiples across the medtech consumables peer group. The company trades at a 15% discount to the wider sector due to these ongoing uncertainties. Consequently, resolving these regulatory issues could act as a significant clearing event to re-rate the stock.
16.
Other side of the story: While many investors fear severe margin contraction, distributors rather than manufacturers may absorb the bulk of the 2028 Medicare price cuts. Jefferies reports on 10 July 2025 that industry experts expect no negative impact on manufacturers from competitive bidding. Because manufacturers hold high market shares in specialized categories, distributors will likely accept lower margins to maintain product access. If this thesis holds, Coloplast and Convatec will sustain their mid-term growth profiles.

Section 3 – Distribution of Bidding Cuts Between Manufacturers and Distributors

Section summary
  1. Distributors are expected to absorb the vast majority of the 2028 Medicare competitive bidding price cuts, leaving manufacturers largely insulated from direct margin compression.
  2. Strong manufacturer pricing power driven by high market concentration and GPO alignment prevents distributors from passing retail reimbursement cuts upstream.

Projected Split of Bidding Cuts

1.
Asymmetrical margin pressure: The financial impact of the 2028 Medicare Competitive Bidding Program (CBP) is projected to be highly asymmetric between supply chain participants. Bernstein's report of 4 June 2026 calculates that the upcoming CBP changes could force a step -30% retail price cut for ostomy and continence products at the distributor level. By contrast, manufacturers are modeled to face a much lower -15% wholesale price cut on their corresponding sales. This division of pricing pressure demonstrates that while retail channels face severe top-line headwinds, manufacturers are significantly shielded from the full volume of the CMS reset. Distributor-borne pricing pressure: Long-term market evidence indicates that medical equipment distributors act as the primary financial shock absorbers during national competitive bidding rounds. Jefferies' thematic report of 17 July 2025 emphasizes that the majority of competitive bidding pricing pressure is absorbed by DMEs and distributors rather than medical device manufacturers. During historical competitive bidding rounds for CPAP equipment, ResMed experienced only a minor 1% increase in like-for-like pricing pressure despite retail reimbursement falling precipitously. This past case suggests that distributors are forced to compress their own margins to maintain access to key brands, keeping manufacturer ASPs relatively stable. Manufacturer pricing insulation: Direct expert feedback from the durable medical equipment (DME) channel reinforces the view that manufacturers will maintain pricing integrity. Jefferies' report of 10 July 2025 summarizing an expert call with a seasoned DME operator concludes that competitive bidding will have no negative impact on manufacturer pricing. Because manufacturers control highly specialized product designs, distributors lack the leverage to demand matching wholesale price cuts. Consequently, the operational burden and financial squeeze of the CMS reimbursement reset are expected to remain localized within the distributor channel.

Manufacturer Pricing Power and GPO Alignment

2.
Contractual access barriers: Manufacturer pricing power is heavily reinforced by long-term alignment with Group Purchasing Organizations (GPOs) that control access to hospital systems. A former Territory Manager at Coloplast A/S explains that sales representatives cannot penetrate large healthcare systems unless their products are officially aligned on GPO contracts. These GPO contracts typically require a high 70% to 80% commitment of market share within the contracted system. This lock-in effect means that once a manufacturer secures a major GPO contract, it is highly insulated from ad-hoc pricing pressure from distributors.
3.
Consolidated market shares: High concentration among chronic care manufacturers limits the ability of distributors to swap out premium brands. Deutsche Bank's report of 21 October 2024 notes that the global ostomy market is highly consolidated, with Coloplast holding 35% to 40% global market share and Hollister acting as the second-largest player. Similarly, Coloplast holds a dominant 40% to 45% global market share in the Continence Care space. This tight oligopoly means that major distributors such as McKesson or Cardinal Health cannot easily bypass Coloplast or Convatec without facing severe patient and clinical backlash. Clinical choice and brand loyalty: The highly personalized nature of ostomy and continence care creates strong clinical barriers that preserve manufacturer pricing. A former Regional Sales Manager at Coloplast A/S notes that the company's ostomy products drive intense emotional loyalty among patients and clinicians due to their advanced design and usability. Because these products are clinically managed prosthetic devices rather than commoditized items, clinicians actively resist distributor attempts to substitute brands. This clinical preference prevents distributors from shifting volume to cheaper alternatives, preserving manufacturer gross margins.

GPO Dynamics and Distribution Channels

4.
Hospital purchasing complexity: Hospital purchasing decisions are heavily institutionalized, shielding manufacturers from direct distributor negotiations. A former Regional Sales Director at Coloplast A/S explains that purchasing involves a complex web of clinicians, procurement officers, supply chain managers, and formal GPO contracts. Because hospital systems utilize Request for Proposals (RFPs) to lock in multi-year pricing, distributors must deliver products under pre-negotiated GPO rates. This institutional structure prevents distributors from unilaterally passing down downstream Medicare cuts to the wholesale price of hospital-acquired supplies. Portfolio bundling strategies: Major distributors rely on comprehensive product bundling to maintain profit margins across thin-margin categories. A former Head of Marketing at Bioventus notes that Cardinal Health utilizes "total spend discounting" to bundle surgical and medical supplies together. This broad portfolio strategy allows major distributors to offer aggressive wholesale pricing to acute care systems by offsetting thin margins on certain items with higher-volume consumables. These bundled contract dynamics make it very difficult for niche manufacturers to compete on distribution alone. Kitting as prime vendor leverage: Capturing specialized service categories like product kitting allows distributors to secure exclusive prime vendor status. An Executive VP at Valor Elite Supply explains that once a distributor captures a hospital's kitting business, all other consumables flow through that prime vendor GPO price list. This service-based lock-in shifts the distributor's focus toward volume execution rather than squeezing individual manufacturer pricing. Therefore, distributors are highly incentivized to maintain positive relationships with top manufacturers like Coloplast and Convatec to protect their prime vendor status.

Private Label Expansion and Distributor Margins

5.
Private label margin defense: Distributors are actively expanding their own private label portfolios to offset reimbursement cuts in post-acute care. A National System Director at CommonSpirit highlights a significant shift in private label spend as healthcare systems look to capture cost savings. For highly commoditized product categories, clinicians find distributor private labels to be clinically acceptable nine times out of ten. This private label trend allows distributors to protect their margins by swapping out high-cost national brands where clinical differentiation is low. Distributor private label variance: The private label capabilities of major medical distributors vary significantly, dictating their exposure to margin compression. A former Director at Medline Industries notes that Medline's private label strategy successfully beats its direct competitors due to its deep focus on manufacturing and cost efficiency. Conversely, Cardinal Health is recognized for logistics and digital capabilities but has a less robust private label offering, leaving its medical device margins highly compressed. This variance suggests that vertically integrated distributors are much better positioned to absorb the 2028 Medicare price cuts. Declining distributor profitability: Post-acute distribution margins are facing structural downward pressure, accelerating the need for private label substitution. A Field Vice President at McKesson Corporation notes that distributor margins are largely flat to slightly pressured down due to Medicaid cuts and inflation. To mitigate this structural pressure, distributors are shifting post-acute and home care patients toward private label consumables. However, this substitution is highly restricted in the ostomy and continent segments, where premium design and patient choice limit private label penetration.

Bernstein's report of 28 January 2026 notes that Coloplast's US sales are primarily wholesales, which will see less headwind compared to direct-to-consumer retail models.
