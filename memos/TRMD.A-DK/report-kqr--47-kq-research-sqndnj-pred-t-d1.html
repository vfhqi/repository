Executive Summary

Section summary
  1. The evidence shows medium confidence that TORM plc's historical commercial premiums and integrated platform can sustain profitability during a market normalization, but the company's spot-heavy model and rising cost base create substantial earnings volatility and dividend compression risk when geopolitical disruptions ease and newbuilding deliveries accelerate through 2029.

TORM plc has demonstrated a consistent realized rate premium over public peers across multiple market cycles, with Medium Range vessels achieving USD 32,600 per day since 2022 compared to the peer average of USD 29,800 per day according to Jefferies September 2025 research, translating to over USD 200m in cumulative outperformance between 2023 and 2025.

This commercial advantage held during historical downturns including 2018 and 2021, though absolute premiums compressed to less than USD 1,500 per day during severe cyclical troughs.

The One TORM integrated platform has consistently outperformed peer averages in 13 out of 14 quarters leading into mid-2018, demonstrating that the company's technical and commercial systems deliver measurable value even when market conditions deteriorate.

Financial performance during historical downturns reveals severe cash flow sensitivity to spot rate compression, with EBITDA falling from USD 272m in 2020 to USD 137m in 2021 and net losses of USD 40.8m during the pandemic trough when fleet-wide TCE rates bottomed at USD 13,703 per day.

The company's formulaic dividend policy automatically adjusts to preserve liquidity, with total distributions slashing from USD 485m in 2024 to USD 212m in 2025 as spot rates normalized from geopolitical peaks.

While management demonstrated velocity in cutting shareholder payouts and deferring capital outlays during past downturns, administrative expenses surged 19% to USD 113.8m in 2025 despite falling revenues, indicating that the corporate overhead structure remains highly rigid and does not scale down during rate normalizations.

TORM executed counter-cyclical fleet acquisitions during historical market troughs, purchasing modern Medium Range resale vessels in 2017-2018 for USD 259m funded through a balanced USD 100m equity raise and senior loan facilities, and completing defensive sale-and-leaseback transactions in 2021 that released USD 73.7m in net liquidity while maintaining operational control.

These opportunistic asset plays positioned the company to capture substantial spot earnings during subsequent upcycles, contrasting with peers like Scorpio Tankers that were forced to liquidate 14 vessels at large book losses in January 2022 to address critical balance sheet leverage.

TORM's capital allocation track record demonstrates management's ability to deploy surplus cash during cyclical lows, though the company's 2018 expansion forced a complete suspension of dividend distributions to protect minimum liquidity covenants.

The company faces substantial cost inflation headwinds, with daily vessel operating expenses rising to USD 7,894 per day in Q3 2026 compared to USD 7,268 per day in the prior year, driven by higher crew change expenses and consumable costs.

This persistent upward cost trajectory increases TORM's cash breakeven rate and threatens to compress net income margins during normalizations, particularly when compared to Hafnia's highly efficient pool platform that achieves G&A expenses of approximately USD 1,200 per day versus the peer average range of USD 1,400 to USD 2,100 per day.

Historical precedents show that specialized operators struggle to reverse cost inflation without compromising vetting status, as demonstrated by Ardmore Shipping's forced upward revision of daily opex expectations during the 2021 downturn despite aggressive cost-saving campaigns.

TORM reported USD 608.8m in capital commitments as of September 30, 2026, including a 50/50 joint venture for eight LR2 newbuildings delivering in 2029-2030, which exposes the company to substantial overcapacity risk as the global product tanker orderbook reached 20.4% of the existing fleet by November 2026.

Historical precedents demonstrate severe financial distress for operators that committed to massive newbuildings near cyclical peaks, with Scorpio Tankers suffering negative cash flows and forced asset sales when its 91-ship orderbook delivered into the 2015-2016 downturn.

However, the global product tanker fleet faces severe aging with approximately 51% of vessels aged 15 years or older, and structural contraction of global shipbuilding capacity from over 1,000 active yards in 2008 to fewer than 500 by 2022 may help absorb the incoming delivery wave and stabilize long-term freight rates above previous trough levels.
