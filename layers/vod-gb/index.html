Vodafone Group (VOD-GB): answers to the first set of questions, 2026-10-27

Reading conventions for the Consumer

  • Fiscal years end 31 March. FY27 is the year to 31 March 2027; H1 FY27 results are due on 10 November 2026. Money is in euros unless stated; the share price is in pence (118.25p on 26 October 2026; 52-week range 85.2p to 132.1p).
  • Profit measures. The company's headline profit line is adjusted EBITDAaL (after leases): EUR 11,351m in FY26, a 28.1 percent margin. FactSet's "brokers' basis" EBITDA (EUR 11,367m in FY26) is the like-for-like base for consensus; FactSet's operating EBITDA (EUR 16,337m) is before leases and must not be mixed with it. Adjusted free cash flow (EUR 2,621m in FY26) excludes spectrum (EUR 404m) and restructuring and integration cash (EUR 422m); statutory free cash flow was EUR 1,795m.

The twelve visible brokers' FY27 EPS forecasts run from EUR 0.080 to EUR 0.150, a near two-to-one spread that is largely definitional. The Consumer should build EPS bottom-up from EBITDAaL and should not rely on the consensus EPS line without reconciling definitions.
  • Two corrections to earlier internal work. (1) The 1 October memo stated a group currency translation drag of 5.4 percentage points in FY26; the 5.4 points is Deutsche Bank's figure for Vodacom alone. (2) The memo's FY26 brokers'-basis EPS of EUR 0.093 has been superseded: the 27 October financials block shows EUR 0.107 with the basis now aligned, which makes consensus FY27 EPS (EUR 0.107) flat on FY26, not up 14 percent.

The external forces do not drive the investment case; they set its boundaries. The tailwinds (a closed UK merger, a regulator that now permits consolidation and extends spectrum, African growth, and a new activist-minded owner) are real and structural, but most are already visible to the market. The headwinds are narrower and sharper: German fixed-line disruption by fibre, a court ruling that removes Vodafone's back-book pricing lever in its largest market, a UK price fight that will last until tariff caps lapse in 2028, emerging-market currency exposure that is the largest among European peers, and a market regime that penalises leveraged yield stocks. For the Consumer, the external picture argues for a base case in which European revenue is flat to slightly down, African revenue grows in local currency but loses part of it in translation, and all of the group's margin improvement must come from internal cost and synergy actions rather than from the environment.

C-01

To what extent is end-market demand changing, structurally or cyclically, and with how much visibility?

C50

Question asked To what extent is end-market demand changing, structurally or cyclically, and with how much visibility? (A cyclical swing counts when it has two-plus years of visibility.)

Judgement. End-market demand is not changing at group level in a way that drives the case; it is a stable, contractual demand base with a mix shift underneath. European consumer connectivity demand is flat in value and structurally shifting in form (cable to fibre in Germany, network brands to discount brands and virtual operators in the UK); African data and payments demand and European enterprise digital demand are growing fast. None of this is a cyclical swing with two-plus years of visibility; it is structural, and it is highly visible because about four-fifths of revenue is monthly subscription.

Reasoning and critical evidence.

  • Europe is flat: European organic service revenue grew 0.1 percent in FY26; in Q1 FY27 Germany grew 1.2 percent (held up by temporary 1&1 wholesale revenue) and the UK 0.6 percent. Europe is about two-thirds of service revenue.
  • The growth sits in segments: Africa organic service revenue +12.6 percent in Q1 FY27 (from 10.9 percent in Q4 FY26), with Egypt +32.9 percent on regulator-permitted price rises, M-Pesa +23.6 percent and South African data use +29.5 percent per user; Vodafone Business +5.0 percent organic with digital services (cloud, security, software) +18.8 percent and now 28 percent of business service revenue.
  • The structural shift that matters most for the model is in German fixed: broadband base -202,000 in FY26 and -98,000 in Q1 FY27 as households move to fibre (Deutsche Telekom, alternative networks) and away from linear TV. This is a migration of demand away from Vodafone's asset, not a fall in demand for connectivity.
  • The consumer backdrop is a mild headwind and probably worsening: energy-led inflation (Brent at USD 111, euro-area energy inflation +10 percent in July 2026), squeezed lower-income households trading down to discount virtual operators (Bernstein), and the UK virtual-operator share forecast to rise from 24 to 30 percent of consumer mobile by 2031. Enterprise budgets are optimising (business connectivity under price pressure at renewal; the EU recovery money for digital is disbursing slowly).
  • Peer contrast: Deutsche Telekom's German broadband volumes are near flat (-3,000 in Q1 2026, -20,000 in Q2) while Vodafone's fall about 95,000 a quarter, so the issue is share of demand, not demand.

What it means for the model. Model European demand as flat in volume and value, with German consumer fixed volumes declining at the current run-rate unless evidence changes; model growth from Africa (local currency, inflation-linked) and from enterprise digital at mid-to-high teens on a small base. Visibility is high on the next twelve months and the downside skew comes from the consumer squeeze and down-trading, not from a demand collapse.

C-02

To what extent, and in which direction, is competitive intensity changing?

❌ D42

Judgement. Competitive intensity is diverging by market: easing in German pricing behaviour, but not in Vodafone's German volumes, and with Vodafone's own pricing toolset now narrowed by the court; intensifying in the UK since the merger; persistently high in Portugal and South African prepaid. Net, intensity facing Vodafone is slightly worse over the last six months than over the prior twelve to eighteen, because the UK has turned into a price fight and the German back-book price lever has been legally weakened.

Reasoning and critical evidence.

  • Germany, easing in tariffs: Deutsche Telekom raised broadband prices EUR 2 to 3 a month on roughly 40 to 50 percent of its base in April 2026 with "manageable, temporary churn"; 1&1 withdrew sub-EUR 7 tariffs and lost 150,000 mobile contracts in the June quarter; Telefónica Deutschland set a EUR 30 minimum on new bundles. This is a value-over-volume industry turn and it gives Vodafone a price umbrella.
  • Germany, not easing for Vodafone: Vodafone's broadband losses worsened (-90,000 to -98,000) and mobile contract losses rose (-77,000 to -85,000) quarter on quarter; Telefónica's O2 Plus bundles (EUR 15 discount with broadband, launched 10 June 2026) and Vodafone's own CHECK24 subsidies show front-book promotion persists. The European Court ruling of 10 September 2026 removes the ability to raise prices on existing contracts at the operator's discretion; Vodafone must reverse the 2023 EUR 5 a month rise on 2.5 million customers. Clauses written into contracts at signing appear to survive (sector digest), so future price rises must be built into new contracts, which shifts pricing power toward the network the customer most wants, which is Deutsche Telekom's.
  • UK, intensifying: since the merger, Virgin Media O2 cut 1.1Gbps broadband to GBP 26.99 and giffgaff unlimited data to GBP 14; Sky Mobile has a GBP 1 plan; fintech virtual operators (Revolut, Klarna on VodafoneThree's own network) target high-value users; Deutsche Bank calls the UK a "street-fight" with no evidence of reduced pricing pressure post-merger. Two moderating facts: Virgin Media O2's owners carry GBP 22bn of debt with falling bond prices, which limits how long they can discount; BT's purchase of TalkTalk out of administration removes a distressed discounter. The CMA caps selected Vodafone retail tariffs for three years from May 2025, so UK pricing repair is a 2028 question.
  • Elsewhere: Digi's entry in Portugal cut Vodafone Portugal's H2 FY26 EBITDAaL by 14 percent; in South Africa Telkom grew data subscribers 15.5 percent while Vodacom's fell 5.7 percent.

What it means for the model. Do not model German back-book price rises; model German pricing gains only through new-contract (front-book) tariffs with continuing volume losses. Model UK ARPU flat to slightly down in real terms until the tariff caps lapse in 2028, with revenue growth from wholesale, fixed wireless and broadband cross-sell rather than from mobile pricing.

C-03

To what extent is a disruption force strengthening or being disproved, and in which direction does it act on the company?

❌ D36

Judgement. The disruption force that matters, fibre overbuild of Vodafone's German cable network, is strengthening and acts against the company; it is the single largest structural negative in the case. Satellite direct-to-device disruption is being disproved for European operators on an eighteen-month view. Artificial intelligence acts in the company's favour on cost but carries a slower-moving revenue risk (automated switching agents) that is real and not yet visible.

Reasoning and critical evidence.

  • Fibre over cable: Deutsche Telekom has passed 13.6 million German homes with its own fibre (2.2 million active, 17.5 percent penetration) and adds about 2.5 million homes passed a year; alternative networks have passed 14.5 million homes; both Vodafone and Deutsche Telekom lost about a point of infrastructure share to alternative networks in FY25. Vodafone's answer is capital-light: the OXG joint venture (840,000 homes passed, 1.2 million targeted by end-FY27), wholesale access over Deutsche GigaNetz (500,000 homes), and DOCSIS 3.1 and later 4.0 upgrades of a 25 million-home cable footprint at about 20 percent of the cost of full fibre. Deutsche Telekom charges a 40 to 51 percent premium on 1Gbps plans, which tells us where customers place the quality. Dell'Oro's view is that fibre into homes is eventually required. The OXG partner swap (Société Générale replacing Altice) means Vodafone forgoes up to EUR 1.1bn of deferred payments and the build has been slow (about 1 million homes connected by September 2026).
  • Satellite: being disproved as a near-term threat in Europe. Terrestrial networks carry about 99 percent of traffic; Europe is reserving the 2 GHz band and Vodafone, Deutsche Telekom, Orange and Telefónica are exploring a joint satellite spectrum bid (October 2026); Vodafone owns 3.8 percent of AST SpaceMobile and 50 percent of the European distribution company, and Ofcom approved VodafoneThree's direct-to-device service in April 2026. The sector digest judges the satellite worry is clearing for network operators.
  • Artificial intelligence: a cost tailwind (Ask Once care reduced detractors 20 percent; bots raised first-contact resolution 50 percent; AI in procurement and code generation), available equally to all operators, so no relative edge. The revenue risk is automated tariff-switching agents, which Morgan Stanley flags for operators with high revenue per customer; Vodafone is a mid-market value brand in Germany, so it is less exposed than Deutsche Telekom on this vector but more exposed to the fibre vector.

What it means for the model. Treat German fixed as a structurally declining volume business with a terminal-value question: model continued broadband losses, rising wholesale and joint-venture access costs to reach fibre customers, and capital intensity that cannot fall in Germany. Satellite needs no line in an eighteen-month model.

C-04

To what extent is political or regulatory change under way, and how does it bear on the company?

C57

Judgement. Regulatory change is substantial and, net, mildly favourable: the direction of travel on consolidation and spectrum is the most supportive in a decade, and the UK merger is closed and cannot now be undone. Against it, consumer-protection rulings are narrowing pricing levers (Germany now, the UK on indexation) and political scrutiny is delaying the new shareholder's influence.

Reasoning and critical evidence.

  • Supportive: the UK CMA cleared the Three merger on behavioural remedies (a GBP 11bn network investment overseen by Ofcom; selected tariffs capped for three years; wholesale reference terms) rather than divestments; the European Commission's draft merger guidelines (30 April 2026; final version aimed for 1 November 2026) weigh investment and scale; the proposed Digital Networks Act supports licence auto-renewal; Germany extended 800, 1800 and 2600 MHz rights to 2030 (a near-term capex saving Deutsche Bank calls major); Italy is moving 2029 renewals to annual fees; Egypt and Tanzania allow price rises and set price floors. UK spectrum is perpetual, so Vodafone's second market carries no renewal auctions.
  • Adverse: the European Court ruling of 10 September 2026 on discretionary mid-contract price changes (German EBITDAaL -1.5 percent in FY27 per Morgan Stanley; an initial EUR 20m provision; EUR 100m to 200m refund exposure reported; about 99,200 to 115,100 customers registered for the collective action). Ofcom's ban on inflation-linked mid-contract rises pushes UK increases to fixed pounds-and-pence. The Kenyan High Court ruled against the government's sale of its 15 percent Safaricom stake (Vodacom is appealing). UK National Security and Investment Act review could delay Vega's board representation until summer 2027. Bundeskartellamt objections on tower delivery to 1&1. Bernstein flags that if Germany classifies the radio network as critical infrastructure, a forced replacement of Chinese equipment could cost the German industry over EUR 2.5bn (Vodafone Germany is exposed); the current agreed timetable (cores by end-2026, wider network by 2029) is manageable.
  • Compliance: NIS2 fines up to 2 percent of turnover; a record EUR 45m German data-privacy fine (June 2025).

What it means for the model. Keep German spectrum costs low to 2030 and UK spectrum renewal cost at zero; take the ECJ hit in FY27 (about EUR 60m of EBITDAaL plus a one-off refund provision of up to EUR 200m in cash, outside adjusted measures); model UK pricing as capped until mid-2028. The final EU merger guidelines are a sentiment event, not a cash event, for Vodafone.

C-05

How are input costs, input availability and supplier concentration changing, and is the net effect a headwind or a tailwind?

❌ D45

Judgement. A modest headwind building over the next six to eighteen months, contained in the near term by hedges and long contracts. The largest swing cost, energy, is hedged for now; the costs rising on the market (optical components, AI-capable radio, tower leases linked to inflation, wages in Germany) are smaller but persistent. Supplier concentration is high and stable.

Reasoning and critical evidence.

  • Energy: Vodafone hedged its main European markets and Deutsche Bank and Morgan Stanley credit the hedges for part of the FY27 guidance raise; before the oil spike, falling energy prices were worth about EUR 200m of opex relief. With Brent at USD 111 and euro-area energy inflation at 10 percent, the risk is that the relief turns into a headwind as hedges roll; Egypt faces a 2-point EBITDA margin headwind from fuel in FY27 (Morgan Stanley).
  • Network equipment: long contracts lock in prices (Ericsson eight years for the UK core and 10,000 base stations; Nokia eight years for 7,000 base stations; Ericsson as sole radio supplier in several markets); procurement is centralised (EUR 25bn a year across about 8,700 suppliers, 70 percent through Luxembourg). Optical components are up about 30 percent; AI-RAN costs USD 120,000 a site against USD 75,000 for a conventional site, so it will be deployed slowly.
  • Towers: the Vantage master services agreement with Vodafone Germany carries inflation escalators (capped at 3 percent, applying 85 percent of German CPI); the lease commitment to Oak Holdings is EUR 3.19bn. The tower-lease repricing wave (Zegona demanding EUR 75m off its Vantage payments; Swisscom and TIM serving notice on INWIT) cuts both ways for Vodafone: it is a large tenant and it owns 50 percent of Oak Holdings (about EUR 6.4bn).
  • Labour: German wage settlements are running high (Deutsche Telekom cites 9 percent salary inflation in Germany); Vodafone offsets with headcount cuts (German average workforce from 15,115 in FY24 to 13,568 in FY26).

What it means for the model. Hold capital intensity at about 18 percent of revenue (guided) with no deflation benefit; assume energy costs flat to slightly up in FY28 as hedges roll; model tower-lease inflation of about 2 to 3 percent a year in Germany; model the net European opex programme as the offset.

C-06

Where is the company's own revenue and demand cycle, and to what extent is it turning?

C60

Judgement. The company's own cycle turned up in FY26 after five years of decline and is now on a plateau of about 5 percent organic service revenue growth and 4 to 6 percent organic EBITDAaL growth. Over the next six months the group rate is likely to hold or ease slightly, while the European part (Germany) turns down again as temporary supports lapse. The turn is real but the next leg of improvement depends on UK synergies and cost, not on revenue.

Reasoning and critical evidence.

  • The turn: organic service revenue +5.5, +5.8, +5.4, +5.1 and +5.2 percent over the last five quarters; organic adjusted EBITDAaL +4.5 percent in FY26 and +6.2 percent in Q1 FY27 with a 0.6-point margin gain; adjusted free cash flow EUR 2,621m in FY26, the first growth in four years; FY27 guidance raised at the first quarter.
  • The plateau, and why: group growth is carried by Africa (inflation-assisted pricing in Egypt and Turkey, data and M-Pesa) and by business digital; Europe is about zero. In Germany, the 1&1 national roaming migration reached a full EUR 100m a quarter run-rate in Q4 FY26 and laps from Q2 FY27 (Deutsche Bank expects German mobile service revenue growth to fall from 2.7 percent to about 0.1 percent); the court-ordered reversal of the EUR 5 price rise lowers German fixed revenue from H1 FY27; a prior-year service-provider true-up makes Q2 tougher. Africa's local-currency growth is partly inflation (Turkey's CPI about 29 percent; Egypt's price rises), so as inflation eases the nominal growth rate eases.
  • The EBITDAaL cycle should stay positive because cost programmes, UK synergies and Safaricom's consolidation (EUR 1.1bn of EBITDAaL in FY27) add to it mechanically.

What it means for the model. Model group organic service revenue at about 4 to 5 percent in FY27 easing toward 3 to 4 percent in FY28 as African inflation and the German wholesale tailwind fade; German service revenue about -1 to 0 percent in H2 FY27 and FY28; group organic EBITDAaL growth of 4 to 5 percent supported by cost.

C-07

To what extent is a macro force acting on the company, on its sector and on its industry, and at which level does it bite hardest?

❌ D40

Judgement. Macro bites hardest at the company level through emerging-market currency translation, because Vodafone has the largest emerging-market exposure among European telecoms (South African rand, Egyptian pound and Turkish lira about 29 percent of revenue; Africa about a third of EBITDAaL after Safaricom). At the sector level the force is the discount rate: telecoms trade as yield stocks, and the current risk-off, rising-yield regime is a valuation headwind regardless of fundamentals. At the industry level the force is the consumer squeeze, which shows up as down-trading rather than lost connections.

Reasoning and critical evidence.

  • Currency, company level: a 41 percent fall in the Egyptian pound is a EUR 224m hit to profit before tax; a 38 percent move in the lira EUR 118m; a 9 percent move in the rand EUR 114m; a 20 percent fall in the emerging-market basket is about EUR 667m of adjusted EBITDAaL (about 5 percent) on the risks research's calculation. African operations have a natural hedge ratio below 0.75 because capex is bought in dollars. Currently the three currencies have depreciated at their slowest pace in three years and guidance uses rebased rates (EUR 1 = ZAR 19.60, GBP 0.87); Deutsche Bank estimates the guidance rates understate a spot tailwind by about EUR 60m while Morgan Stanley sees about EUR 100m of emerging-market weakness against guidance. Middle East escalation (oil at USD 111) is the tail risk for the Egyptian pound.
  • Rates, sector level: the US 10-year yield is near 5.10 percent with a December rate rise priced; the fitness-for-fighting digest rates telecoms Neutral (defensive earnings wanted, leverage penalised as yields rise) and has moved Momentum to Underweight for the whole market. Vodafone's own interest cost is insulated (substantially all debt fixed, average bond maturity 13 years, cost of debt about 3 percent, a 100 basis point rise costs about EUR 9m of profit before tax), so the hit is to the multiple, not the earnings.
  • Consumer, industry level: falling consumer confidence and energy inflation drive migration to discount brands and slower enterprise spending; it has not yet shown in group revenue.

What it means for the model. Run explicit currency scenarios on the African and Turkish lines (translation reduces reported growth by several points a year in those segments in most years; FY26 was an exception at group level because sterling strengthened). Keep interest cost close to flat in percentage terms; do not assume equity multiple expansion while the regime is risk-off.

C-08

To what extent is a key driver at the industry or sector level accelerating the company's demand?

C50

Judgement. No industry or sector driver is accelerating demand for the group as a whole. The sector's live drivers, in-market consolidation, falling capital spending on owned networks, owner-forced cost cuts and contractual price rises, act on costs, structure and cash rather than on demand. Demand acceleration exists only in segments: African data and mobile money, and enterprise cloud and security.

Reasoning and critical evidence.

  • The sector digest's five stories (closed mergers, falling capex on owned networks, new owners forcing costs out, contractual price rises, satellite) are all supply-side or pricing stories. Vodafone participates in the first and third strongly (UK closed; Vega), in the fourth weakly (the ECJ ruling hurt it), and not in the second (Vodafone's capex stays at about 18 percent of revenue and its German fibre is rented, so "add the rent back" applies).
  • Segment accelerators: Africa organic service revenue accelerated to 12.6 percent; M-Pesa active customers +12.5 percent; Vodafone Business accelerated from 3.2 to 5.0 percent with digital services at 28 percent of business revenue; the EU's EUR 150.9bn digital allocation is a slow-disbursing support (few governments above 30 percent of planned investment). Fixed wireless access in the UK (3.7 million premises; 23,000 net additions in Q1 FY27) is a small new demand line.

What it means for the model. Do not model a demand-led acceleration; model segment mix shift (Africa and enterprise digital growing faster than Europe consumer) as the mechanical source of group growth.

C-09

What other material external dynamic, positive or negative, is at work?

C56

Judgement. Three further external dynamics are material: the arrival of Xavier Niel's Vega as the largest shareholder (positive, and the most important new force, though delayed to mid-2027); a Europe-wide repricing of tower leases (two-sided for Vodafone); and the threat of German mobile consolidation between Telefónica and 1&1 (negative for Vodafone's high-margin wholesale income).

Reasoning and critical evidence.

  • Vega: bought e&'s 16.2 percent at 112.5p on 10 July 2026 (a 14.8 percent premium) and built to 19.9 percent; at Tele2, Niel's Freya removed 15 percent of the workforce and delivered 11.4 percent organic EBITDAaL growth in 2025 on 2.3 percent revenue growth, before slowing to 4 percent in Q2 2026. Morgan Stanley argues Vega could push Vodafone to double its European cost target and extend it to the EUR 10bn emerging-market cost base; doubling the European target to EUR 2bn would lift FY30 free cash flow by about 26 percent. Timing: UK security review may delay board seats until summer 2027; part of the stake is held by banks in block trades pending approval, so Vega's outright ownership is not settled.
  • Tower leases: tenants are attacking anchor contracts (Zegona: EUR 75m off about EUR 150m of Spanish lease payments; Swisscom and TIM against INWIT; European towers trading at a 20 to 30 percent discount to US peers). For Vodafone, cheaper leases help Vodafone Germany's costs and hurt the value of its 50 percent Oak Holdings stake; the net is unknown.
  • Germany wholesale: 1&1's own network covers about 29 to 34 percent of households and it depends on Vodafone's roaming; Telefónica has been negotiating to buy 1&1 for EUR 4.5bn to 5.0bn. A combination, or faster 1&1 self-sufficiency, would put at risk about EUR 400m a year of high-margin wholesale revenue by FY28.
  • Market regime: risk-off and the momentum tilt to Underweight (fitness-for-fighting digest) work against a stock up 33 percent in twelve months.

What it means for the model. Model the Vega effect as a FY28 to FY30 upside scenario (a larger cost programme announced mid-2027), not in the base case; model the 1&1 wholesale income as at risk from FY28 in a downside scenario.

C-10

Taking each sector and industry thematic driver in turn, and then all of the above together: how strong are the external headwinds and how strong are the tailwinds at value-chain, industry and sector level, and what is the overall external change picture?

❌ D46

Judgement. The external picture is close to balanced, tilted slightly negative for the next twelve months: the tailwinds are mostly structural and already visible in the price (closed UK merger, permissive regulation, Africa), while the headwinds are concentrated in the market that sets the group's value (Germany) and in the market regime (yields, risk-off).

Thematic drivers, one by one.

  • Closed in-market mergers (sector rates Big): a clear tailwind for Vodafone, the largest single example in Europe; synergies arrive on management's schedule, now raised to GBP 1.0bn by FY32.
  • Capital spending falling on owned networks (Big): not a tailwind for Vodafone; its capex stays at about 18 percent and German fibre is rented through joint ventures and wholesale.
  • New owners forcing costs out (Early): a tailwind from mid-2027 via Vega; the largest prize in the sector on the digest's own judgement.

  • EBITDAaL: Germany -2.5 to -4 percent a year against UK about +8 percent and Africa about +8 to 10 percent local; group organic about +3 to 4 percent.

  • Per share: the capital loop adds about 4 percent a year net, and it is the loop most sensitive to the others.

C60

Judgement. The expected change, as our base case defines it, rests about four-fifths on strengths Vodafone has demonstrated and one-fifth on a strength it has not: the net drop-through of gross savings to reported EBITDAaL. It does not rely on the two strengths the business would most need to acquire, German retail retention and real European pricing power, because the base case assumes neither. That is the main reason the base case is plausible. The same fact cuts the other way for the upside: the things that would make Vodafone beat our numbers (German stabilisation, UK pricing after May 2028, a Vega-forced programme reaching the accounts) are precisely the undemonstrated strengths, so the bull case is less plausible than the base and bear cases, which is why the skew of outcomes around our base is balanced and the skew around consensus is negative.

Strengths demonstrated, and the parts of the change that rest on them.

  • Centrally controlled cost removal and deal execution. 11,000 roles against a 10,000 target; more than 1,200 roles in Q1 FY27; EUR 2.0bn of net European opex removed since 2018; three country transactions in twelve months at good prices; the UK minority bought at a GBP 1.3bn discount; guidance met in FY24 and FY25 and delivered at the upper end in FY26. The plausibility report rates the guidance record "high" confidence. The base case's EUR 0.65bn of net programme savings reaching EBITDAaL by FY30 and its FY27 landing inside guidance rest on this.
  • Closed-merger synergy capture, at peers and now early at VodafoneThree. MasOrange EUR 356m against EUR 300m in year one; Swisscom EUR 166m in H1 2026 against EUR 300m for the year; Telefónica Deutschland EUR 820m of EUR 900m from E-Plus; VodafoneThree's organisational and retail synergies "already running ahead of schedule" (Deutsche Bank, 9 October 2026), network sharing ahead of plan, first-year opex synergies above GBP 100m. The base case's UK EBITDAaL path (EUR 1.95bn to 2.65bn by FY31, about 8 percent a year against management's "mid-to-high single digit") rests on this, with the IT-stack decommissioning (end-2028) and the MBNL unwind (to 2030) as the untested steps; the Virgin Media O2 precedent (30 percent of target captured by late 2022) is the caution, and the second set's phasing already back-loads the benefit.
  • African growth with pricing pass-through. Egypt +32.9 percent in local currency after regulator-approved rises; M-Pesa +13.4 percent; Vodacom's record of adjusting prices to currency; Safaricom's 50 percent margins. The base case's 7 to 10 percent local-currency growth rests on this. Minority dilution and translation are structural, not execution, risks.
  • Capital return capacity. EUR 4.0bn of buybacks executed between May 2024 and May 2026; leverage guided back to the lower half of the range by end-FY27; Deutsche Bank calls FY28 resumption "highly plausible". The base case's EUR 2bn a year from FY28 rests on this plus debt capacity, which is a choice, not a strength.

The strength the base case needs but the business has not shown: net drop-through.

  • Morgan Stanley (16 July and 2 September 2026): less than half of Vodafone's gross savings have historically reached profit, and the pace of net cuts slowed by about 45 percent (EUR 1.3bn in 2018 to 2022, EUR 0.7bn in 2022 to 2026) while group EBITDAaL stayed flat. Peer conversion rates: Proximus 38 percent of gross across three programmes; Telia expects 70 percent; Orange's EUR 600m of gross efficiencies are fully absorbed by EUR 800m of copper and wholesale headwinds; Deutsche Telekom's German opex rose 3 percent in 2026 despite falling headcount. Vodafone's own definition excludes energy and exceptional wage inflation, so the target can be "met" while reported EBITDAaL does not show it.
  • The base case credits EUR 0.65bn of the EUR 1.0bn net target, about 33 percent of gross, close to Proximus's 38 percent and well below the 50 percent the company implies. That is a reasonable middle, but it is still a bet on better conversion than Vodafone's own 2022 to 2026 record (when the net saving coincided with flat EBITDAaL), justified by three things that differ now: a closed merger inside the programme, a fixed portfolio, and an owner who will measure management against Tele2.

Strengths the business would need to acquire, and whether the thesis needs them.

  • German retail retention: not demonstrated (losses of about 95,000 broadband lines a quarter for six quarters; network quality last of three; the ECJ ruling has given 2.5 million customers a free right of termination). The base case does not need it: it assumes 380,000 losses in FY27 and a German EBITDAaL decline every year to FY31. The bear case needs the losses to accelerate; the bull case needs them to halve.
  • European pricing power: not demonstrated (Deutsche Telekom's EUR 2 to 3 back-book rise held with low churn; Vodafone's was reversed by a court). The base case assumes price is neutral to margin and nothing real until May 2028.
  • An owner-forced second programme: plausible (Tele2: 15 percent of headcount, +11.4 percent organic EBITDAaL in 2025) but 18 to 24 months from decision to accounts, with Vega's board representation possibly delayed to summer 2027. Not in the base case.
  • Stabilising German broadband while losing 90,000 to 98,000 lines a quarter has no precedent among overbuilt cable operators without full-fibre investment; Vodafone's capital-light answer (OXG at about 100,000 homes a quarter, wholesale access, DOCSIS 3.1) is the strategy of a company that has chosen cash over position, which makes the German decline more predictable, not less.

The record in the conditions that now apply. The plausibility report's history lesson is that Vodafone missed when energy and wage inflation spiked (2022: energy costs of EUR 760m facing a EUR 400m rise as hedges expired) and when a regulatory change removed high-margin revenue (the TV law: EUR 1.1bn of German EBITDAaL lost against an initial EUR 0.4bn estimate). Both conditions are present in milder form (Brent near USD 111 with hedges rolling in FY27 and FY28; the ECJ ruling worth about EUR 60m a year plus refunds). The base case has provision for both; the history says the provision is more likely to prove too small than too large.

D-03

In relation to the differences between the forecasts over the next 18 months to four years, vs. the last four to six years' financials, how diffuse or concentrated are the key drivers that generate the differences?

❌ D42

Judgement. The drivers are concentrated at every level of the account, and more concentrated the further down the account one goes. At EBITDAaL, three drivers (the Safaricom consolidation, the UK merger with its synergies, and African organic growth) supply about nine-tenths of the EUR 3.7bn increase from FY26 to FY31, and one of them, Safaricom, supplies about two-fifths on its own. At earnings per share, a single financial driver, the share count, supplies about two-thirds of the growth, and it depends on the operating drivers delivering the debt capacity that funds it. The negative drivers are concentrated too, in one market (Germany) and in one customer (1&1). This is a narrower set of drivers than in FY24 to FY26, when EPS rose on disposals, lower depreciation, tax, interest income and buybacks while EBITDAaL stayed flat; but it is a more mechanical set, which is why the forecasts are more predictable in their first 18 months than the breadth of the risk list suggests, and less predictable from FY29, when the concentrated negatives (1&1, refinancing, trade-tax losses) arrive.

The decomposition (our base case; EUR bn; FY26 to FY31).

  • EBITDAaL from 11.37 to 15.04: Safaricom consolidation about +1.55 (nine months in FY27, EUR 1.5bn to 1.6bn a year after); UK about +0.9 (two extra months of Three plus synergies, EUR 1.95bn to 2.65bn); Africa excluding Safaricom about +0.9 (7 to 10 percent local growth less 3 to 4 points of translation on a base of about EUR 3.3bn); the net European programme outside the UK about +0.3; Other Europe and Turkey about +0.3; Germany about −0.5 (EUR 4.15bn to 3.65bn). Three items are about 90 percent of the gross positives; Safaricom is 42 percent of the net change and reaches Vodafone's shareholders at about 36 cents in the euro.
  • From EBITDAaL to attributable net income (revised): EBITDAaL +EUR 3.7bn becomes attributable net income +EUR 0.3bn (about EUR 2.58bn to 2.88bn), because depreciation rises about EUR 1.1bn from the FY26 level implied by the component evidence (about EUR 7.2bn to 8.35bn), net financing cost rises about EUR 0.9bn from the flattered FY26 base (EUR 819m to 1.73bn) and minorities rise about EUR 0.65bn (EUR 194m to 850m). Three below-the-line lines absorb about 70 percent of the EBITDAaL growth.
  • From net income to EPS: the average share count falls from 24.0bn (FY26) to 18.4bn (FY31), −23 percent, worth about +31 percent on EPS; attributable net income adds about +12 percent. Of the roughly 45 percent EPS growth from FY26 to FY31, about two-thirds is the count and one-third the business.

Why this concentration matters for the next 18 months. In the next three half-years the operative drivers are even fewer: Safaricom's consolidation (certain, subject to the Kenyan appeal), the lap of the 1&1 boost and the ECJ reversal in Germany (mechanical), the first UK opex synergies (ahead of plan), and the below-the-line reset (investment income falling, minorities rising, which the first half will show). None of them needs a judgement about demand, competition or pricing; the 18-month forecast is therefore mostly arithmetic on announced facts. The diffuse elements that could move it (energy as hedges roll, German churn after the reversal letters, currency, the timing of restructuring cash) are each worth 1 to 3 percent of EBITDAaL or EPS, not more.

Why it matters for years two to four. Concentration makes the forecasts fragile where the single drivers have single failure modes: Safaricom (the Court of Appeal: de-consolidation would remove EUR 1.4bn of EBITDAaL and about EUR 0.2bn of minority dividends with an EPS effect of only about 1 to 2 percent, but would reverse the narrative of a simplified group); the UK (IT migration by end-2028; competitive reinvestment if BT or Virgin Media O2 cut prices); 1&1 (a Telefónica purchase or faster self-supply: EUR 300m to 400m of EBITDAaL, 9 to 12 percent of EPS, offset by EUR 100m to 200m of capital spending); the share count (a halving of the buyback to protect 2.5x costs about 3 percent of EPS a year). The EPS line is thus a chain of four links each depending on one event. The past four to six years were also concentrated (disposals, the TV law, the share count) but the events were in management's hands; the coming ones are in the hands of a Kenyan court, a German competitor, a British regulator and bond markets.

Peer contrast. Deutsche Telekom's growth is T-Mobile US (one driver, high quality, creeping ownership reduces leakage); Orange's is MasOrange (one driver, consolidation accretive to cash per share by about 5 percent in year one); BT's is falling fibre capital spending (one driver, in its own hands). Vodafone has three operating drivers but owns only a third of the largest, which is the unusual feature: its EBITDAaL concentration is lower than peers', its attributable concentration higher.

D-07

To what extent do the share price and the multiple move with sector and industry peers rather than idiosyncratically (on a rolling six-month basis), and so how far is the multiple company-driven rather than industry-driven?

C50

Judgement. The share price has become a high-beta, mostly idiosyncratic stock within its sector: over the latest six months the equal-weighted peer basket explains 35 percent of Vodafone's daily variance (correlation 0.59, beta 1.50), leaving 65 percent to company-specific movement, and the stock returned +0.5 percent while the basket fell 4.7 percent. The beta of 1.50 is the highest in the 24-month record (it was 0.71 to 0.76 from November 2025 to January 2026 and 1.44 to 1.47 in late 2024), and it has risen every month since January 2026 as the shares re-rated on Vega, the UK and Africa.   • The six-month window (27 April to 26 October 2026): correlation 0.59; beta 1.50; R-squared 35 percent.
