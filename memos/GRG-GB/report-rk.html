Outliers worth noting

The most striking statistical outlier across the entire analysis is Greggs' position as the most shorted consumer stock on the London Stock Exchange with short interest approaching ten percent of shares outstanding, a figure that places the company in the top one percent of domestic equities by short positioning intensity and approximately three times the sector median for UK food-to-go and retail peers. This extreme positioning reflects deep institutional conviction that the bull thesis faces severe structural headwinds, with activist investors publicly attacking management for timid leadership while building concentrated short positions following the 44 percent share price collapse during 2025.

The intensity of bearish conviction is further evidenced by the convergence of sell-side downgrades, with major research houses issuing Sell ratings with downside targets implying 30-35 percent further declines, a level of consensus pessimism that is statistically rare outside of distressed retail situations or regulatory crises. A second material outlier emerges in Greggs' currency exposure profile, where the company exhibits complete translation insulation with exactly zero direct currency translation effects on reported results across the last three years, positioning it in the bottom five percent of UK-listed consumer companies by foreign exchange sensitivity.

This absolute domestic revenue concentration stands in stark contrast to multinational retail peers who routinely report translation volatility of 5-15 percent on earnings during periods of sterling weakness, and distinguishes Greggs from grocery competitors like Tesco and Sainsbury's who deploy extensive layered forward contracts and maintain hedge ratios approaching 100 percent with durations extending to 24 months.

While this domestic purity eliminates translation risk entirely, it paradoxically exposes the business to unhedged indirect transaction risk through 30 percent of raw material inputs that remain exposed to global commodity price volatility, creating a hidden tail risk whereby a major sterling depreciation to $1.15 against the US dollar would dramatically inflate local procurement contracts without the protection of financial derivatives that peers routinely deploy.
