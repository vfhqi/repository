Section 1 – Brief company description and history

Section summary
  1. AddLife is a decentralised European Life Science distributor recovering from the post-pandemic and leverage setback, with the business quality and balance sheet improving, but its valuation still requires successful acquisition-led compounding rather than merely stable healthcare demand. European niche compounder: AddLife owns, develops and acquires specialised companies supplying equipment, instruments, reagents, medical devices, consumables, advice, training and technical support from research through medical care, with roughly 85 operating subsidiaries across 30 European countries and approximately 2,300 employees.
1.
Two complementary divisions: Medtech represented 62% of 2025 sales at SEK 6,495m and serves hospitals, homecare and social care, while Labtech represented 38% at SEK 3,953m and serves diagnostics, biomedical research and laboratory analysis.
2.
Distribution-led economics: AddLife mainly distributes products from external suppliers, although approximately 10% of sales come from own products, while services, training and specialist support increase customer value and make advanced products less substitutable than simple consumables.
3.
Defensive, but not non-cyclical: Management positions the group as relatively resilient because healthcare is largely publicly financed and prioritised during downturns, while Redeye notes that Labtech’s instruments and research exposure can still experience weaker activity, destocking and timing effects.
4.
Broad geographic footprint: AddLife’s revenue is concentrated in Europe, with 95% of sales generated there and more than 80% of purchased products sourced from European suppliers, reducing direct exposure to United States tariffs and long-distance supply disruption relative to more globally exposed distributors.
5.
Customer concentration is limited: Redeye reports that no individual customer contributes more than approximately 4% of revenue, the ten largest customers account for about 14%, and the largest supplier contributes approximately 5%, supporting the argument that AddLife’s decentralisation reduces singlecounterparty risk.
6.
Fragmented addressable market: DNB Carnegie identifies approximately 33,000 European medtech companies, around 95% of which AddLife considers potentially relevant acquisition targets, making the opportunity set materially broader than the company’s current 85-subsidiary base. Recovery from an abnormal period: AddLife’s 2023 sales reached SEK 9,685m, up 7%, but EBITA fell 7% to SEK 1,135m and EPS declined to SEK 1.56 from SEK 3.96 as COVID-related demand disappeared and one-off costs and write-downs affected earnings. 2024 marked operational repair: Revenue increased to SEK 10,286m in 2024 from SEK 9,685m in 2023, while EBITA rose to SEK 1,159m from SEK 1,135m and EBITA margin improved to 11.3% from 11.7% reported in 2023, although the 2023 comparison included unusually favourable and unfavourable non-recurring items. 2025 restored profitability: Sales rose 2% to SEK 10,442m, adjusted EBITA increased 8% to SEK 1,259m and adjusted EBITA margin expanded to 12.1% from 11.3%, while EPS rose to SEK 4.59 from SEK 2.06 and operating cash flow reached SEK 1,392m from SEK 1,095m.
7.
Nine-month 2026 confirmed the recovery: For January to September 2026, sales increased 3% to SEK 7,965m, adjusted EBITA rose 6% to SEK 972m, adjusted EBITA margin reached 12.2% from 11.9%, and EPS increased to SEK 3.15 from SEK 2.47. 37 21 Oct Interim Report Q3 2026 AddLife EPS progression through September 2026 versus prior periods Q3 was stronger than the public bar: The 21 October 2026 quarter delivered SEK 2,599m of sales versus the seven-contributor consensus of SEK 2,584m, adjusted EBITA of SEK 298m versus SEK 297m and EPS of SEK 1.05 versus SEK 0.86, meaning the principal surprise was below adjusted EBITA rather than at the operatingprofit level. Returns remain below elite peers: DNB Carnegie’s September comparison showed AddLife with 2025 adjusted EBITA margin of 12.1% and return on capital employed of approximately 11%, versus Asker’s 9.5% adjusted EBITA margin and 14.8% return on capital employed, while AddLife’s advantage was stronger cash EBITA margin and lower like-for-like balance-sheet risk.
8.
Balance-sheet repair reopened M&A: Financial net liabilities to EBITDA declined from 3.5x at year-end 2023 to 2.2x at year-end 2025 and 2.5x at September 2026, compared with AddLife’s stated ceiling of below 3.0x, allowing acquisitions to resume after only one deal in each of 2023 and 2024.
9.
Acquisition quality is central to the model: Unicam and Lehnen were presented as businesses with margins above 12% and purchase multiples below 7x enterprise value to EBITDA, while the four 2026 acquisitions together were expected to add approximately SEK 350m of annual sales, making acquisition execution the clearest route to EPS growth. Historical M&A explains the valuation discount: Redeye records 42 acquisitions since AddLife’s establishment, including 38 since the 2016 listing, but highlights that the larger 2021 transactions produced mixed outcomes and contributed to the leverage and return concerns that depressed the stock after its late- 2021 peak. The share-price record is a boom-and-repair cycle: Redeye describes the stock rising from approximately SEK 25 at the 2016 spin-off to nearly SEK 390 in late 2021, then falling to roughly SEK 55–60 in late 2023 before recovering to around SEK 160–163 in September 2026, although the available evidence does not provide verified three-, six- and twelve-month returns against the OMX Stockholm index. The six-month expectations bar rose progressively: DNB Carnegie moved from a SEK 177 target price in April 2026 to SEK 185 in July and SEK 186 in September, maintained a Buy rating, and raised its 2027–28 adjusted EPS forecasts by 2% before Q3 for Unicam and foreign-exchange changes. DNB Carnegie expected a solid but not transformational quarter: Rolder and Redin forecast Q3 adjusted EBITA of SEK 300m, an 11.6% margin and net debt to adjusted EBITDA of 2.8x, with Q4 cash generation expected to reduce leverage to 2.3x, setting a higher operating and balance-sheet bar than the company’s unquantified positive commentary alone. Redeye set the more ambitious medium-term framework: Knutsson modelled approximately 4% organic growth, a 13% EBITA margin through 2026–28 and continued acquisition acceleration, compared with FactSet’s approximately 12% margin expectation for 2026 before reaching 13% in 2027, so the market entered Q3 expecting gradual margin expansion but differed on its pace. Valuation was no longer distressed: Redeye valued AddLife at approximately 17.1x 2026 estimated EV/EBITA versus a 17.0x niche-acquirer peer median and assigned fair value of SEK 185, while DNB Carnegie’s SEK 186 target implied that further upside required delivery on M&A and margin expansion rather than simple re-rating from balance-sheet repair. The central positioning remains incomplete: AddLife is now a credible recovery and compounding candidate, but its 12.2% adjusted EBITA margin and approximately 11% return on capital employed remain below the quality profile of proven serial acquirers, leaving the expectations bar dependent on sustained cash-funded acquisitions and better returns, not merely another quarterly EPS beat.

Section 2 – Sell-side and market focus prior to these recent earnings

Section summary
  1. The pre-earnings bar was not a simple revenue beat: investors wanted proof that AddLife could sustain roughly 4% organic growth, expand margins toward 12% to 13%, convert earnings into cash and restart disciplined acquisitions without repeating the leverage and return mistakes of 2021–23. Organic growth bar, moderately demanding: DNB Carnegie’s Elvin Rolder and Robert Redin expected approximately 4% Q3 organic growth after adjusting for the UK endoscopy divestment, compared with AddLife’s reported 4% adjusted outcome, so the market was looking for confirmation of recovery rather than a dramatic acceleration. Underlying growth quality, closely tested: Redeye’s Stefan Knutsson also modelled approximately 4% organic growth in Q3 and annually thereafter, but treated that level as the minimum needed to support the longer-term compounder case, because AddLife’s historical median quarterly organic growth was only about 4% to 5% and its long-term ambition required stronger earnings productivity. Acquired growth, increasingly important: DNB Carnegie expected acquisition activity to contribute materially to Q3 revenue and EBITA, with Unicam included in its model and four 2026 acquisitions ultimately supporting the prospect of 6 to 8 annual deals, making deal flow a central part of the pre-results earnings expectation rather than an optional upside factor. Organic versus acquired growth, key market distinction: Redeye viewed AddLife primarily as a serial acquirer because acquisitions had historically supplied much of reported growth, so investors were assessing whether the renewed M&A programme would supplement genuine demand or obscure a merely modest underlying organic recovery. Labtech, expected to lead margins: DNB Carnegie expected stable diagnostic volumes, improving capitalequipment contribution and prior tender wins to produce approximately 3% Labtech organic growth, while high-margin acquisitions were expected to lift the segment’s adjusted EBITA margin by about 60 basis points year on year to 11.9%. Labtech, higher private bar: Redeye’s framework was more ambitious than DNB Carnegie’s quarterly estimate because it assumed a sustained 13% group EBITA margin through 2026–28, implying that investors were not only watching whether Labtech improved, but whether acquired businesses could raise the group toward a structurally higher profitability level. Medtech, resilient demand but operational friction: DNB Carnegie expected solid underlying Medtech demand and approximately 4% adjusted organic growth, but explicitly flagged continuing doctors’ strikes in

Spain as a drag and required improvement in homecare and eye surgery to offset that disruption. Elective surgery, an important upside driver: DNB Carnegie’s pre-earnings case assumed high growth in elective surgical procedures, continued recovery in eye surgery and homecare improvement, so the market was looking for evidence that waiting lists and deferred procedures were translating into sustained volumes rather than merely temporary quarterly timing.

Pricing power, inferred rather than proven: Management had previously linked margin improvement to active pricing and a shift toward advanced products, while Redeye expected portfolio optimisation and pricing discipline to support gradual organic operating leverage, but neither the company nor the available preearnings research quantified a distinct price contribution that could be cleanly separated from mix and acquisitions.

Advanced products, central quality test: Redeye focused on AddLife’s deliberate migration away from lowermargin, high-volume consumables toward advanced products, because the shift could improve margins and pricing power but also suppress reported volume growth, creating a pre-earnings tension between higherquality revenue and headline sales expansion. Gross margin, expected to continue improving: DNB Carnegie’s earlier 2026 work treated margin expansion as a core earnings driver and expected AddLife’s operating improvements, product mix and acquisitions to support more than 15% adjusted EBITA growth, materially above what revenue growth alone could deliver.

Group margin, the valuation hurdle: FactSet consensus cited by Redeye assumed approximately a 12% EBITA margin in 2026 before reaching 13% in 2027, whereas Knutsson modelled 13% throughout 2026–28, creating a visible public-consensus gap and a possible private bar above the published quarterly consensus. Cash conversion, the decisive credibility test: DNB Carnegie repeatedly identified second-half cash generation as the condition for AddLife to execute its M&A agenda, with its pre-results model expecting leverage to remain around 2.8x after Q3 before falling to approximately 2.3x at year-end when seasonal cash collection arrived.

Cash quality, not just reported profit: Redeye expected 2026 cash conversion of approximately 73%, below the unusually strong 83% recorded in 2025, and warned that investors should verify whether earnings converted into free cash flow sufficiently to fund deals while keeping net debt below AddLife’s 3.0x target. Leverage, acceptable but not comfortable: DNB Carnegie regarded leverage near the 3.0x ceiling as manageable because Q4 was historically AddLife’s principal free-cash-flow collection period, but still treated a failure to delever as a direct threat to acquisition capacity and therefore to medium-term EPS growth.

1.
Acquisition discipline, shaped by past failures: Redeye’s analysis highlighted the mixed results of AddLife’s larger 2021 acquisitions and the slower turnarounds at AddVision and Biomedica, so the pre-earnings market was watching whether the new smaller-ticket strategy restored returns rather than simply increasing transaction volume. Purchase multiples, required to stay below historical risk levels: Redeye noted that AddLife’s historical initial acquisition multiples averaged roughly 6x to 7x EBITA, while total consideration including contingent payments averaged approximately 8x, making the stated sub-7x EBITDA multiples for Unicam and Lehnen important evidence against a repeat of past overpayment concerns.
2.
Integration, deliberately light touch: Redeye expected AddLife to preserve acquired companies’ brands, management and entrepreneurial autonomy, with integration mainly involving financial governance, culture and code-of-conduct processes, so investors were focused on whether decentralisation preserved growth without weakening post-acquisition control. Synergies, a missing quantified element: AddLife’s pre-earnings materials described margin-accretive acquisitions and strengthened acquisition resources, but did not provide quantified synergy targets or milestones for Unicam and Lehnen, leaving the market to value the deals primarily through their stated margins and purchase multiples rather than proven integration benefits. Management credibility, under renewed scrutiny: The company’s 2023–25 repair programme had restored margins and reduced leverage, but Redeye still described the historical organic-growth record as mixed and partly below ambition, meaning management needed to demonstrate that the improvement was operationally durable rather than a favourable recovery from the post-pandemic trough. Long-term earnings target, ambitious split: Management’s long-term framework called for approximately 15% EBITA growth, with roughly half expected organically and half from acquisitions, while Redeye’s model assumed only about 4% organic growth and required renewed M&A to bridge the gap, making acquisition execution more important than the headline target suggested. Capital allocation, subordinated to reinvestment: Redeye viewed AddLife’s relatively low 30% to 38% historical payout ratio favourably because retained cash could fund acquisitions and reduce leverage, so the market’s priority before Q3 was not a larger dividend or buyback but evidence that reinvested cash would earn acceptable returns. Valuation, no longer a balance-sheet-repair free option: Redeye’s September framework valued AddLife at approximately 17x 2026 EV/EBITA, broadly in line with a 17x niche-acquirer peer median, while its 2026 P/E of roughly 36x to 38x exceeded the peer median near 29x, meaning the market was already paying for improved growth and did not require merely an in-line quarter. AI economics, constrained in Europe: The chief clinical officer at Incepto Medical said European reimbursement is materially lower than in the United States and that radiology-AI use cases such as fracture detection face intensifying competition and commoditisation; this limits the probability that AddLife can rapidly monetise AI through high-margin per-case economics, even if AI increases demand for compatible equipment and workflow services. Pay-per-use, unlikely to transform EPS soon: A vice president at Siemens Healthineers said pay-per-use and similar new business models are unlikely to become common within five to ten years without changes to reimbursement systems, while hospitals are moving toward standardised single-vendor solutions; this reduces the immediate threat of an abrupt asset-light disruption to AddLife, but increases the importance of winning framework agreements before procurement becomes more consolidated. Digital sales, an existing distributor threat: AddLife’s 2025 annual report states that more non-European research suppliers are offering non-exclusive distribution agreements, encouraging manufacturers and multicountry distributors to sell directly through digital channels; this is more immediate than generative artificial intelligence because it can erode local distribution economics now, particularly in research products that require less installation, training and clinical support.

Value-based procurement, defensive but investment-intensive: AddLife is moving from product-and-price selling toward procedure-specific solutions, training, clinical support and documented efficiency outcomes, which can reduce substitutability and protect margins when procurement evaluates total value rather than unit price; however, the model requires application specialists and customer implementation resources, and management acknowledged that those costs are being incurred before related sales fully materialise.

Automation and workflow, the strategic battleground: AddLife’s advanced products are intended to let healthcare providers perform more procedures with existing staff, shorten procedures and reduce aftercare, matching the industry’s response to labour shortages; the EPS upside is plausible over three years, but it will appear only if customers convert productivity claims into funded tenders and if AddLife retains enough service margin after supporting implementation.
