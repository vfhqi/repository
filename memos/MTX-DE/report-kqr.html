Section 3 – OEM Reliability, Fleet Storage, and Recovery Trajectories

Section summary
  1. Next-generation engine reliability deficits and fleet groundings have extended legacy narrowbody utilization, creating a highly visible aftermarket revenue cycle.

Next-Gen Engine Reliability and Storage Rates

1.
Unprecedented aircraft groundings: Serious durability and manufacturing issues tied to powder metal contamination on Pratt & Whitney Geared Turbofan engines drove global narrowbody storage to record levels, leaving over 800 GTF-powered aircraft in storage by late 2025.
2.
Improving storage trajectories: Deutsche Bank Securities highlights that stored Geared Turbofan aircraft declined to 596 in August 2026, representing a reduction in the grounded proportion of the active fleet to 21% from 31% in October 2025.
3.
Extended recovery timelines: Bernstein Research notes that although grounded Geared Turbofan counts are down 15% year-to-date, a complete operational resolution is highly complex and the recovery should take considerable time through the end of 2027.
4.
Strategic airline adjustments: Rothschild & Co Redburn notes that to mitigate these disruptions, carriers like Wizz Air expanded spare engine holdings by attempting to triple its spare engine requirements from 30 to 40 units to nearly 100.

LEAP Deferred Profits and Business Models

5.
Conservative profit recognition: Rothschild & Co Redburn analyst Olivier Brochet highlights that Safran utilizes a highly cautious accounting model under which more than 80% of the margin on LEAP service contracts will be recognized after 2030.
6.
Long-term profit scaling: Rothschild & Co Redburn forecasts that as the installed base matures, Safran's LEAP aftermarket profits by 2040 are expected to grow to more than twice the CFM56 contribution recorded in 2024.
7.
Accelerated program profitability: Jefferies Research reports that the LEAP engine services program achieved annual breakeven in 2025, which was nine years post-entry-into-service, as aftermarket turned profitable in 2024 with the onset of performance restoration shop visits.

Legacy Narrowbody Extensions and Pricing Upward

8.
Extended platform utilization: Jefferies Research reports that legacy narrowbody engines remain indispensable for global capacity, with the YTD CFM56 retirement rate tracking at just 1.5% to 2% compared to historical expectations of 3% to 4% as airlines extend the lives of older narrowbodies.
9.
Sustained shop-visit plateaus: Bernstein Research estimates that delayed fleet retirement trends will keep CFM56 shop visits elevated at 2,300 to 2,400 annually through 2028, showing that the shop visits to remain elevated for longer than the OEM's historical peak-and-decline guidance.
10.
Surging overhaul pricing: Maintenance experts at Air France Industries highlight that because of tight MRO capacity and OEM-controlled parts supply, airlines face extremely steep maintenance pricing where LEAP shop visits exceed $10 million compared to mature CFM56 visits that typically sit in the $5 million to $6 million range.
11.
Other side of the story: Other side of the story: If elevated fuel prices persist, lessors and operators note that the high cost of maintaining older engines could instigate an accelerated retirement wave from 2027 onward, leading to a rapid retirement of legacy platforms and an oversupply of used serviceable material.

Section 4 – Upward Repricing Power and Contractual Structures

Section summary
  1. Tight capacity limits allow engine manufacturers to enforce uncapped contract escalation formulas and preserve outsized margins.
  2. Long-term service agreements delay immediate pricing capture but guarantee highly predictable long-term aftermarket revenue streams upon contract renewal.

Contractual Structures and Inflation Protection

1.
Strong contract protection: Rothschild & Co Redburn analyst Olivier Brochet outlines that engine aftermarket agreements typically run for 8 to 12 years with uncapped, built-in material and labor escalation clauses.
2.
Direct inflation pass-through: According to Rothschild & Co Redburn, these uncapped contractual formulas allow manufacturers to flow inflation straight to airline customers to protect long-term margins.
3.
Delayed price realization: Former Woodward executives note that pricing under long-term agreements is often tied to historical indices, creating a two-year lag behind general inflation before cost increases are fully recovered.
4.
Custom risk sharing: According to a former Group CPO at Rolls-Royce, supplier and customer contracts are heavily indexed to public market indices with specific mechanisms to adjust material pricing if costs exceed agreed boundaries.
5.
High switching barriers: Rothschild & Co Redburn emphasizes that airlines cannot easily bypass these terms because supply constraints and pilot retraining costs eliminate competitive switching options.

OEM Pricing Power and MRO Captive Dynamics

6.
Robust catalog increases: A former Senior Vice President at AAR Component Services notes that pricing increases for aftermarket parts typically remain steady at 3% to 4% annually.
7.
Monopolistic aftermarket grip: A former special advisor at Airbus explains that once an engine is selected, the airline is locked into a monopoly structure where the OEM controls high-margin parts supply.
8.
Captive MRO structures: A former Vice President at StandardAero highlights that independent MRO shops face strict margin compression because they are contractually required to purchase parts from OEMs at list prices.
9.
Value-add MRO focus: A former chief financial officer at MTU Aero Engines explains that commercial maintenance shops earn their entire operating profit on the 30% value-add from repairs while new parts are zero-margin pass-throughs.
10.
Cost-minimizing workscopes: Bernstein Research highlights that while 73% of airlines prioritize limiting shop visit costs, they have little power to negotiate lower pricing due to extreme part scarcity.

Pricing Window and Capacity Constraints

11.
Sustained pricing power: Jefferies Research expects that the persistent structural undersupply of narrowbody engines will support robust OEM pricing power and aftermarket demand.
12.
Long-term contractual catch-ups: In its H1 2026 financial statement, Rolls-Royce reported a £356 million operating profit boost driven by commercial contract renegotiations and LTSA catch-ups.
13.
Step-up renewal opportunities: Deutsche Bank Securities highlights that because 15% to 20% of long-term agreements renew annually, OEMs can capture persistent pricing step-ups over the next five years.
14.
Growing shop-visit backlogs: Bernstein Research reports that massive shop-visit backlogs will continue to insulate engine aftermarket providers from near-term macroeconomic volatility.
15.
High-cost service barriers: Bernstein Research notes that intense material scarcity has pushed LEAP shop- visit costs past $10 million, forcing airlines to accept extremely steep pricing due to lack of alternatives.

Sceptical Outlook and Downside Risks

16.
Other side of the story: If high jet fuel prices persist, Bernstein Research warns that financially strained airlines will accelerate legacy engine retirements, which would rapidly destroy the most profitable segment of the OEM aftermarket annuity.

Section 5 – Supply Chain Bottlenecks: Castings, Forgings, and M&A

Section summary
  1. High-tonnage castings and forgings act as the primary physical constraint capping commercial engine build rates through the end of the decade.
  2. Extreme component scarcity has triggered aggressive vertical integration and M&A as OEMs scramble to secure single-crystal airfoil capacity.

High-Tonnage Capacity and Forging Constraints

1.
Structural forging caps: Large hydraulic press capacity acts as a structural cap on aircraft build rates because these massive assets are extremely capital intensive and require up to five years to construct.
2.
Isothermal lead times: Lead times for high-end isothermal forgings have extended to more than two years as aerospace engine demand outstrips available capacity.
3.
Mammoth press barriers: Only two players in the United States operate mammoth presses in the 250,000-ton range, creating single-source vulnerabilities for the largest structural forgings.
4.
Goodwin order visibility: Goodwin mechanical engineering division leverages specialty casting capabilities to support aerospace and defense customers, extending visibility through its record £300 million order book.
5.
Tooling lifecycle limits: Die tooling dedicated to specific engine components limits sustained press output even when raw forging capacity is physically available.
