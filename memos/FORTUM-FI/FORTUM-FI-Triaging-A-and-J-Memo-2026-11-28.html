#0 The investment case

The six messages that matter most

Fortum's earnings are a lever on two numbers Fortum does not control: each EUR 1/MWh of Nordic power price is EUR 47m of EBIT (about 4% of EPS), and each 1 TWh of output is EUR 0.04 of EPS.
Fortum's share price implies a long-term Nordic power price of about EUR 58/MWh against a forward curve of EUR 41 beyond 2028, so the recovery, the higher curve and the Google contract are already in the price.
The Google power purchase agreement (PPA) reprices 9% of Fortum's output from 2028 (about EUR 125m of pre-tax profit); a second such contract, about 45% likely by mid-2027, is the one upgrade that would hold the multiple.
Fortum's FY2028 EPS of about EUR 1.21 only gets back to FY2023's comparable EPS of EUR 1.28: the earnings path is a recovery, not yet a step-up.
The eighteen-month return is (8)% at the guideline 13.87x multiple, +2% at Viewforth's 15.5x and +19% if 18.4x holds; weighted by probability it is +6%, so the multiple decides the outcome.
Three dated tests settle the case: the hedge book and nuclear guidance on 2 February 2027, a second long-term contract by late April 2027, and the 2027-2028 Nordic forward holding above EUR 52/MWh through the winter.

The case in seven steps (the topics below refer back to these steps)

  1. The asset is irreplaceable, but a market Fortum does not set prices its output.
  2. The change is real: a tightening Nordic market, one proven contract and mechanical self-help.
  3. The change reaches the financials as a recovery to FY2023 earnings by FY2028.
  4. The foundation (asset, balance sheet, management) can carry the recovery.

  6. The reward is symmetric at EUR 22, not positively skewed: the multiple decides it.

Step 1. The one idea: the moat is in the asset; the fragility is in the price of what the asset produces

Fortum is a Nordic power generator producing 47 TWh a year (52% nuclear, 44% hydro, 99% carbon-free) that sells almost all of its output at the Nordic wholesale price. On top of that price, Fortum earns an optimisation premium of EUR 8-10/MWh by moving hydro output into the hours that pay most. Generation is 88% of EBITDA, the fixed cost base is EUR 870m, and the balance sheet is nearly debt-free (net debt 1.4x EBITDA, rated BBB+).

Fortum's assets cannot be replicated (perpetual water rights; nuclear licensed to 2050), but the market, not Fortum, sets the price of their output. Fortum's earnings are therefore a lever on two numbers it does not control: the Nordic power price and the fleet's volume. The competitive position, the main risk and the earnings sensitivity all follow from that one fact.

Step 2. The change: three positive forces, one of them external and large, against specific and visible headwinds

  • The Nordic power balance is tightening for the first time in a decade. Wind additions fell below 500 MW in 2026 from a run-rate of 2 GW a year. Fingrid (the Finnish grid operator) had signed 5 GW of data-centre and 4 GW of storage connection agreements by August 2026, and Google has committed EUR 13bn to Finnish data centres for 2027-2028. A 15% Swedish reservoir deficit pushed the 2027 Nordic forward to about EUR 66/MWh in September, with 2028 at about EUR 53, against a curve of EUR 40-42 a year earlier.
  • Contracting at a premium has been proven once. The 22-year Google PPA (signed 9 September 2026) takes up to half of the Loviisa nuclear plant's output from 2028: about 4 TWh, or 9% of the fleet, at about EUR 30/MWh above the curve. When fully ramped, the Google PPA is worth about EUR 125m of pre-tax profit and 1.4 points of return on net assets (RONA). The Google PPA shows that Fortum can sell its output above the curve to the buyer with the deepest pockets in the market.
  • Self-help is real and mostly mechanical. In 2025 Fortum lost 3.9 TWh of nuclear output (availability 75% against a 90% target) and 1.5-2 TWh of hydro, so normal weather and a working Oskarshamn 3 reactor would on their own return EUR 150-250m of profit. The EUR 100m cost programme is complete. The Elmera acquisition (closed in Q4 2026, about EUR 450m) adds about 5% to EBITDA, with EUR 30m of synergies from 2028. Management targets EUR 330m more comparable EBIT by 2030.
The headwinds are specific and already visible. Batteries have collapsed Finnish reserve prices from EUR 10-30/MWh to below EUR 10, and management has guided the optimisation premium down from EUR 9.7/MWh (2025) to EUR 6-8 from 2027, a loss of EUR 80-160m of profit. Finland raised the electricity tax on data centres forty-fold to EUR 22.4/MWh in July 2026 and is drafting "additionality" rules that would stop hyperscalers contracting existing nuclear output (Microsoft already excludes existing nuclear). Industrial customers sign three-to-five-year contracts, not ten-year ones. The Finnish state owns 51.26% of Fortum and has a record of taking from the company when power prices are high.

Step 3. How the change reaches the financials: a recovery to FY2023 earnings by FY2028, not a step-up

Comparable EPS recovers to FY2023's level only in FY2028-FY2029. Comparable EPS fell from EUR 1.28 in FY2023 to EUR 0.82 in FY2025 on nuclear outages, dry weather and lower hedge prices. Viewforth forecasts a recovery to about EUR 0.95 in FY2026, EUR 1.09 in FY2027, EUR 1.21 in FY2028 and EUR 1.38 in FY2030, slightly below consensus throughout (chart below).

The earnings lift comes through two lines, achieved price and volume. On achieved price, 65% of 2027 output is hedged at EUR 41/MWh, so only the open 35% of 2027 and about 55% of 2028 see the higher curve; each EUR 2/MWh of achieved price is worth about EUR 94m of EBIT. On volume, about 2 TWh of normalisation arrives in 2027. Elmera and Google together add about EUR 0.15 of EPS by 2030. Fortum's FY2028 EPS therefore gets back to FY2023's level, not past it: the case is a recovery with a contracting option attached, not yet a step-up.

The sell-side has already moved, so the next estimate change depends on news not yet published. FY2027 consensus EPS is up 35% in twelve months and 16% in three, driven by the forward curve and the Google contract, and three large brokers moved from Sell or Hold to Hold or Buy within a fortnight of the Google contract. The next move in estimates depends on the Q4 2026 hedge book (2 February 2027), the 2027 nuclear guidance, and whether a second buyer signs.

Step 4. The foundation: first-rate asset, clean balance sheet, and a management team that delivers on costs and capital but not yet on volumes

The asset is first-rate and the financial profile is above average. Generation earns a 28% EBIT margin; capex is 9% of sales against 17% at Verbund and 86% at Orsted; and on Viewforth's numbers free cash flow covers the dividend from 2028.

Management is disciplined, long-tenured and finance-led, with a mixed delivery record. On the credit side, management exited Uniper and Russia at a cost of about EUR 9.6bn of capital, rebuilt the balance sheet, took EUR 100m of costs out ahead of plan, and signed Google. On the debit side, management ran nuclear availability at 75% against a 90% target, cut the 2026 nuclear volume guidance twice, missed FY2025 EBIT consensus by 3% and Q2 2026 EBIT consensus by 13%, and declines to confirm achieved prices or PPA premia on calls. Fortum's record is that it delivers on costs and capital, but not yet on volumes or on commercial pace.

The industry is fragmented but disciplined. The Nordic market has 350 generators but little new wind is being built, and the Nordics remain Europe's lowest-cost power market.

Step 5. The setup: Fortum fits 'Demand-driven EPS upgrades' best of Richard's six Target Setups, but only loosely

Fortum has most of the ingredients of a 'Demand-driven EPS upgrades' setup, but the new demand has not yet appeared in the consumption data, the upgrades so far have come from the forward curve rather than the company, and the starting multiple is high. 'Big Corporate Change-driven EPS upgrades' is the secondary fit: Google, Elmera, the coal exit and the cost programme are real changes, but together they lift EPS by only about 10-15%, mostly in 2028-2030. None of the three setups that depend on a capitulation and a low starting multiple (the CfC-clearing setups) applies, because there has been no capitulation and Fortum's multiple is high.

Demand-driven EPS upgrades: present

  • A dominant position in what the new demand needs: firm clean baseload power, with 5 GW of powered-land grid access
  • Fixed-cost operating leverage: EUR 47m of EBIT per EUR 1/MWh of price, on EUR 870m of fixed costs
  • A first contract (Google) that proves the pricing
  • A sell-side revision cycle already running

Demand-driven EPS upgrades: missing

  • The demand in the data: Nordic consumption flat at 395 TWh in 2025; industrial demand flat in H1 2026; green steel and hydrogen delayed
  • A record of company-driven upgrades (the upgrades so far are curve-driven)
  • A low starting multiple: 18.2x is the 77th percentile of Fortum's own history and, among peers, only Uniper and CEZ are higher

#1 Operating environment: a net tailwind, driven by supply before demand has arrived

Evidence. The external tailwinds are the strongest in Fortum's modern history, and they come mostly from the supply side of the Nordic power balance. The headwinds are equally specific.

Tailwinds: mostly on the supply side

  • Wind additions collapsed to below 500 MW in 2026 from 2 GW a year since 2020, because new wind is uneconomic without a PPA at Finland's 65% capture rate
  • A Swedish reservoir deficit of 15-32% year on year lifted the 2027 forward to about EUR 66/MWh
  • Nordic interconnector capacity is rising to 13 GW, exporting the Nordic surplus
  • The EU Emissions Trading System review is tighter than feared on supply (140 Mt fewer emission allowances in 2026-2031, Morgan Stanley), and allowances at EUR 80/t support the continental prices that Nordic exports price off
  • Sweden has a SEK 220bn state-loan framework for new nuclear
  • The demand pipeline is large on paper: Fingrid's 5 GW of data-centre connections, Google's EUR 13bn, a 600 MW Microsoft-linked site worth 5 TWh, and Finnish demand that could nearly double to 160 TWh by 2035

Headwinds: specific and visible

  • Nordic consumption was flat at 395 TWh in 2025, and industrial demand was flat in H1 2026
  • High interest rates have pushed green steel and hydrogen projects (LKAB, SSAB Oxelosund) to 2027 and beyond
  • About 2 GW of batteries in Finland are saturating the reserve market and compressing the optimisation premium
  • Finland's data-centre electricity tax rose to EUR 22.4/MWh, the compensating support package is still unapproved, and Google has paused two Finnish expansions pending clarity
  • Draft additionality rules would bar hyperscalers from contracting existing nuclear
  • The Finnish ten-year yield has risen from 2.8% to 3.4%, and any Ukraine settlement risks reopening cheap Russian power imports

Inference. The Nordic balance is tightening from the supply side before the demand side has shown up in consumption data. The tightening is a genuine tailwind for the 35-55% of 2027-2028 output that is unhedged, but it is weather-sensitive (the curve beyond 2028 is still EUR 40-42/MWh), and the demand growth that would make it structural is a 2028-2030 event with policy risk attached. The sector digest agrees: the market has stopped paying for AI capital spending on sentiment, but contracted firm power is one of the two big stories in integrated utilities, and the window to sign contracts at a premium is probably one to two years.

Where I differ from the market. The market, like the September sell-side upgrades, treats the move in the forward curve as the start of a structural re-pricing. I treat the 2027 move as mostly hydrology and the 2028-and-later curve as the real test. Consensus 2029 EPS of EUR 1.40 (seven brokers) looks to me like it assumes a curve sustained above EUR 55/MWh; I use EUR 50.

Counter-case and what would change my view. If Fingrid's connection agreements start converting into measured load in 2027 while wind additions stay below 1 GW a year, the Nordic balance tightens structurally and the EUR 58/MWh that the share price implies becomes conservative. Estimates of Finnish data-centre consumption have already been revised to 2,000 MW, 10% of national demand, by 2030. I would change my view on two consecutive prints of rising Finnish industrial consumption, or on the 2028-2029 forward holding above EUR 52/MWh.

Conclusion. The operating environment is a net tailwind over 12-24 months; the weather-driven part of the tailwind is already in estimates, and the policy headwinds are real but survivable. The environment supports step 2 of the case (the change is real) and leaves step 6 open, because the skew depends on whether the tightening is structural.

Score: 65, set by the Rater. A genuine net external tailwind that reaches most of profit (generation is 88% of EBITDA): wind build collapsing to below 500 MW, 5 GW of data-centre grid agreements and the 2027 Nordic forward at about EUR 66 against EUR 40-42 a year earlier. But 65% of 2027 is hedged at EUR 41, the 2027 leg is largely hydrology, the curve beyond 2028 is still EUR 40-42, consumption is flat at 395 TWh, and batteries (premium guided down EUR 80-160m), the forty-fold data-centre tax and additionality drafts are direct threats; consensus growth at the 33rd-42nd percentile confirms a partial rather than structural lift, so it sits just above Linea Directa's advanced, reversible pricing cycle and below Eckert & Ziegler's structural demand.

#2 Internal change forces: well-sequenced and credible, but narrow and back-loaded

Evidence. Fortum's internal changes are mostly positive, but the commercial record so far rests on a single contract.

Positive

  • The pivot from wholesale sales to bilateral contracts, proven once with Google: 22 years at about EUR 80/MWh, a 60% premium to the curve on Morgan Stanley's estimate and EUR 30 above it on Deutsche Bank's; EUR 125m pre-tax. A memorandum of understanding (MoU) on powered land, wind, solar and storage could make Fortum Google's energy portfolio manager in Finland.
  • Elmera closed: 24% of Norway's consumer electricity market and 684,000 deliveries, bought at 16.6x 2027 P/E, with EUR 30m of synergies from 2028; retail rises from 12% to about 20% of EBITDA.
  • Costs: the EUR 100m cost programme is complete and fixed costs are capped at EUR 870m.
  • New mid-term targets: 14% RONA (against 11.0% over the last twelve months) and EUR 330m of comparable EBIT uplift by 2030, two-thirds of it volume normalisation according to Bernstein.
  • Portfolio: the coal exit completes by March 2027 and the Meri-Pori site becomes a powered industrial zone; Loviisa is licensed to 2050, with 38 MW and 10 MW uprates from 2028; Fortum has applied for Swedish state aid for 1.2-3.4 GW of new nuclear at Oskarshamn; an 8 GW wind and solar permitting pipeline, bought for about EUR 50m upfront, should reach 1.2 GW ready-to-build by 2028; and Loviisa's fuel supply has switched to Westinghouse, with Framatome to follow.

Negative

  • Nuclear availability was 75% in 2025, and the 2026 nuclear guidance was cut twice.
  • For a second quarter, a 93% realised hedge ratio locked Fortum out of a EUR 53/MWh spot price.
  • Fixed costs rose EUR 11m year on year in Q2 2026.
  • Morgan Stanley has raised a CFO succession question: a Deputy CFO has been appointed, and the CFO is taking a Spanish chairmanship.
  • One contract in a year of trying is the whole commercial record so far.

#3 Value chain, business model, financial profile, management: a high-quality asset inside a medium-quality business

Evidence: value chain. Generation is the profit-favoured stage of the value chain (a 28% EBIT margin against 4% in retail), and Fortum has had no regulated network business since the 2015 sale of its Swedish distribution arm. The Nordic generation market is 442 TWh, led by Vattenfall and Statkraft, with Fortum third; it has 350 generators but is disciplined on new build. Customers are the weak link: they sign three-to-five-year tenors, hyperscalers demand additionality, and large users such as Norsk Hydro self-supply or sign with Statkraft. The supply side is now de-risked: Westinghouse fuel is loaded, and Framatome follows by the early 2030s.

Evidence: business model. Fortum holds perpetual water rights that cannot be replicated, 4.7 GW of hydro with hourly-to-seasonal storage and 3.3 GW of nuclear at 11-16 g CO2/kWh. The optimisation premium (EUR 9.7/MWh in 2025) is Fortum's one source of pricing power.

Evidence: financial profile. Fortum's EBITDA margin (25% on the brokers' basis) is below its peers', but Fortum needs far less capital and debt (table below). RONA is 11% against a 14% target. Free cash flow did not cover the dividend in FY2024-FY2025 (operating cash flow fell from EUR 1,392m to EUR 840m, against EUR 1,256m of dividends paid in 2025), but should cover it from 2028.

Evidence: management and governance. CEO Markus Rauramo (CEO since 2020; CFO 2012-2017 and 2017-2020) and CFO Tuomela (CFO since 2023, previously Uniper's CFO) are disciplined, finance-led and risk-averse. Hurdle rates are WACC plus 150-400bp; growth capex is capped at EUR 750m over five years, with EUR 2.5bn uncommitted. Shareholding requirements are met and CEO pay is below the peer median. The nine-member board is fully independent, but the Nomination Board is chaired by the Prime Minister's Office. Fortum's total shareholder return (TSR) has trailed European utilities by 47% since early 2022 because of the Uniper and Russia legacy. The culture is steady: staff turnover 7.8%, engagement 7.7, and a total recordable injury frequency of 2.4.

#4 Guidance conservatism and delivery record: honest rather than conservative, with no record of raises

Evidence. Fortum gives operational guidance only (volumes, premium, hedges, costs, tax and capex) and no profit guidance. The delivery record is in the table below. Two further facts complete it: H1 2026 delivered only 51% of full-year consensus net income against a 59% norm, and FY2026 consensus EPS fell 2% after the Q3 2026 results. The change-forces research calls management an "honest tracker": management adjusts volume guidance to reality rather than defending it, holds the premium guidance through volatile quarters, and meets capex and cost guidance exactly. Management does not manage consensus at all: Fortum does not confirm achieved prices or PPA pricing, and leaves the sell-side to model them.

Inference. Fortum's guidance is conservative on the premium (beaten by EUR 1.7/MWh in 2025) and realistic on costs, but has been optimistic on volumes for two years because nuclear availability has been structurally below target. Fortum has no record of raising guidance: the 2026 upgrade cycle came from the forward curve and Google, not from company numbers. The mechanical "guide low, beat, raise" engine that Richard values is therefore absent. What exists instead is a published hedge book that lets the market recompute Fortum's earnings itself as the curve moves.

Where I differ from the market. The market treats the September upgrades as fundamental momentum; I classify most of them as curve-driven and therefore reversible. The FY2026 cut after Q3 and the (0.9)% one-month move in FY2028 consensus EPS are the first signs of that reversal. I agree with the market that nobody expects Fortum to give profit guidance.

Counter-case and what would change my view. The 2026 nuclear guidance now looks achievable after the July restart of Oskarshamn 3, and the 2026 premium should print within its range (it was double-digit in Q1). A 2027 nuclear guide of 24.5 TWh, with the Olkiluoto 2 rotor fixed in the 2027 outage, would be the first volume upgrade in three years. #5 Operating leverage, pricing power and feedback loops: high and clean, but two-way and set from outside

Evidence: operating leverage. Fortum's operating leverage is almost purely price and volume on a fixed cost base. Fixed costs are EUR 870m, and the marginal cost of output is near zero on hydro and low on nuclear, so EUR 1/MWh of achieved price is EUR 47m of EBIT (100bp of margin), 1 TWh of volume is EUR 45-50m, and 50bp of EBIT margin is EUR 25m. Most of the cost-out scope is used (EUR 100m done; fixed costs capped).

Evidence: pricing power. Fortum's pricing power sits in one place, the optimisation premium (EUR 9.7/MWh in 2025, guided EUR 6-8 from 2027), which batteries are eroding. Fortum is rebuilding pricing power in a second place, bilateral contract premia (Google at EUR 30/MWh above the curve).

Evidence: financial leverage and feedback loops. Financial leverage is deliberately low (1.4x net debt to EBITDA; EUR 6.5bn of liquidity), so there is no balance-sheet multiplier and no refinancing fragility. The hedge book is pro-cyclical: a high hedge ratio turns a volume shortfall into a buyback at spot, as happened in Q2 2026 at a 93% hedge ratio. The Finnish price zone is the most volatile in Europe, which helps the premium in normal years and hurts when volumes fail.

Inference. Revenue growth does lead to outsized profit growth: on Viewforth's numbers EBITDA rises 13% in FY2027 on about 3% underlying (excluding Elmera) revenue growth, and 8% in FY2028 on 1% revenue growth. But the lever works in both directions and is set from outside: a EUR 5/MWh fall in the 2028 spot price on 25 TWh of open volume takes 9% off EPS. The reinforcing loop that would make Fortum a compounder, contracting the fleet at premia so that the base is protected while the open volume rides the curve, has started (9% of output) and is the thing to watch.

Where I differ from the market. The market prices the optimisation premium at EUR 7-7.5/MWh and I use EUR 7, so there is no material difference. I give less weight than management to Elmera as an "internal hedge" (retail margins widen when wholesale prices fall): at a 4% EBIT margin and 20% of EBITDA, Elmera offsets perhaps EUR 1-2/MWh of generation price.

Counter-case and what would change my view. Rising wind volatility is lifting the premium even as batteries compress reserve income: very-high-price hours exceeded 14% of all hours in the Swedish SE2 price area in Q2 2026, and management held its EUR 8-10 premium guidance for 2026. A 2026 premium of EUR 9 or above, with the 2027 guidance held at EUR 6-8, would make me lift my EUR 7 to EUR 7.5 (+EUR 0.02 of EPS).

Conclusion. Fortum's operating leverage is high and clean but two-way and mostly set from outside, and its pricing power is narrow, under attack from batteries and only beginning to be rebuilt through contracts: a C. Operating leverage supports step 3 of the case (the mechanics of the recovery) and is the reason the range of outcomes in step 6 is wide.

Fortum's business is sound and its direction is right; the problem is the price of the contracting option, and the next three to five months will show whether the option is worth paying for.

Fortum is a first-rate clean Nordic generator run by a disciplined, risk-averse operator. Fortum is recovering from an outage-and-weather trough into a tightening Nordic market, and one 22-year contract proves that its fleet can be sold above the forward curve. The case is a recovery with a contracting option attached. At 18.2x, the 77th percentile of its own history, the market has already paid for the recovery, the September curve, the Google contract and part of a second contract. A re-rating from here needs the second contract; at a price near EUR 18 with the thesis intact, the case needs nothing more than today's business.
