Section 1 – The Key Question and Why It Matters

Section summary
  1. Understanding the historical overcapacity dynamics of the 2015-2016 container supply wave provides critical valuation framework benchmarks for evaluating the current normalization cycle.

Core Question Analysis

1.
Critical inquiry: "For Kuehne + Nagel International (KNIN-CH) and its peers DSV, Panalpina, Expeditors and DHL Global Forwarding, what did sea and air gross profit per unit, volumes and segment conversion rates do quarter by quarter through the 2015-2016 container supply wave (orderbook deliveries, Clarksons fleet and demand growth by year, scrapping, the Shanghai Containerized Freight Index path, and the Hanjin collapse of August 2016), separating the effect of abundant capacity in 2015 and the first half of 2016 from the effect of the carrier rate spike in the second half of 2016; how long did any decline in unit gross profit last and what ended it; how did the 2015-2016 experience compare with the 2023-2024 normalisation; and what do Clarksons, Drewry, Xeneta, Sea-Intelligence and the carriers (Maersk, Hapag-Lloyd, HMM, CMA CGM) now publish for container deliveries, scrapping, blank sailings, Suez transit shares and schedule reliability for 2027 and 2028, and for the expected timing of contract repricing in the Asia-Europe (January to April) and transpacific (May) tender seasons of 2027?"
2.
Decoupled earnings: Kuehne+Nagel and its peers do not see their earnings directly driven by carrier rates because their gross profits depend primarily on supply chain complexity. Shippers regularly pay forwarders for value-added logistics services rather than simple port-to-port capacity brokerage. Consequently, the development of earnings per share over the next three years will be determined by how well these companies manage labor productivity and technology adoption.
3.
Discrepant valuation: DSV trades at a forward price-to-earnings multiple of 17.1x, which represents a modest 8% premium to its global freight forwarding peer group. This current premium sits significantly below the historical average premium of 13% that the company has commanded since 2010. In contrast, DSV has recently traded at a substantial 24% discount to its closest listed peer Kuehne+Nagel.

Structural Risks and Historical Precedents

4.
Challenging integration: DSV faces significant downside risks to its valuation if the integration of its newly acquired DB Schenker business experiences delays or higher costs. Analysts expect that any failure to realize the targeted synergies will depress the company's conversion ratio below the historical 45% threshold. Such operational disruptions in the road logistics segment could severely impair forward earnings per share expectations.
5.
Cyclical benchmarks: Freight forwarders experienced intense yield pressure during the 2015-2016 supply wave due to rampant vessel oversupply. However, the subsequent 2023-2024 post-pandemic normalization demonstrated that unit gross profits can stabilize well above historical baselines because of increased supply chain disruption. Understanding these contrasting cycles is vital for investors trying to model normalized earnings power in the upcoming 2027 contract repricing seasons.
6.
Contrary evidence: Jefferies research reports a clear correlation between changing ocean freight rates and forwarding gross profit yields, although the relationship is not perfectly one-for-one. Their analysis indicates that falling carrier rates will act as a direct headwind to sea freight yields with a typical lag of several months. This correlation suggests that the upcoming 2027 capacity normalisation could trigger more severe earnings pressure than asset-light management teams expect.
7.
Resilient outlook: The forwarders are highly likely to protect their earnings per share because their asset-light business models allow them to quickly adjust their variable cost structures during downcycles. While ocean carrier profitability remains highly vulnerable to vessel oversupply, the forwarders will benefit from purchasing cheaper capacity from those carriers. This operational flexibility should allow leading players to sustain superior returns compared to asset-heavy liners.
8.
Other side of the story: While asset-light models offer flexibility, the freight forwarders still face substantial downside risks if a severe global macroeconomic slowdown destroys aggregate trade volumes. Furthermore, if shippers aggressively shift from contract agreements to cheaper spot rates, forwarder margins will compress. If these volume declines coincide with rapid air and sea capacity growth, normalized earnings per share will fall below current consensus estimates.

Section 2 – Sea and Air Forwarder Metrics in the 2015-2016 Supply Wave

Section summary
  1. Asset-light freight forwarders achieved elevated unit gross profits during the 2015 overcapacity phase because carrier buy rates collapsed rapidly.
  2. The severe carrier rate spike in late 2016 severely compressed forwarder yields because contract repricing lagged behind spot market increases.

Industry Context and Research Baseline

1.
Robust database: This comprehensive forwarding industry analysis is constructed from 25 relevant sources covering five major companies and two comparable precedents, with no named entities omitted from the study. The global containership fleet expanded by 8.6% in 2015, which far outpaced the sluggish global trade demand growth of only 2.5% during the same year. This severe market imbalance pushed spot container freight rates into a deep cyclical trough.
2.
Depressed benchmarks: The Shanghai Containerized Freight Index hit a historic low of 400 points in March 2016 as vessel oversupply intensified. During this period of abundant capacity, ocean carriers were completely unable to sustain general rate increases. Consequently, asset-light logistics providers benefited from exceptionally favorable spot market buy rates during the first half of 2016.
3.
Sudden restructuring: The dramatic bankruptcy of Hanjin Shipping in August 2016 removed approximately 3% of global container capacity overnight. This unprecedented disruption acted as a major catalyst that forced shippers to prioritize financial strength. As slot capacity tightened, spot rates out of China more than doubled from their spring lows by the end of 2016.

Kuehne + Nagel Performance

4.
Profitable selection: Kuehne + Nagel defended its margins in 2015 by strategically walking away from 144,000 TEUs of low-margin volume. This targeted portfolio optimization successfully increased sea gross profit per TEU by 5.2% to CHF 356. By focusing on complex pharmaceutical solutions, the segment conversion rate reached an impressive 33.2% for the full year.
5.
Lagging recovery: Kuehne + Nagel suffered a sudden CHF 16 per TEU gross profit drop in the third quarter of 2016. The company was caught off guard by the rapid post-Hanjin capacity tightening in September. This unexpected procurement pressure dragged down fourth-quarter sea EBIT by 15% to CHF 105 mm.
6.
Contrary evidence: Jefferies reported that Kuehne + Nagel experienced a 10% lower sea unit gross profit in late 2016, whereas some peers maintained relatively stable yields. This temporary yield divergence occurred because the company struggled to immediately pass higher carrier rates to its large retail shippers. The yield pressure ultimately compressed its sea freight conversion ratio by 456 basis points to 30.2% in the fourth quarter.

This yield compression was driven by rising carrier rates and a higher mix of large global accounts. Management reported that procurement pressure squeezed air margins by 2% and sea margins by 1% during the quarter.

Contractual losses: Panalpina saw its ocean freight segment turn unprofitable in the fourth quarter of 2016 with an EBIT loss of CHF 5 mm. The company suffered from severe contract mismatches as carrier spot rates spiked. This operational disruption pushed its ocean conversion ratio down to negative 5.1%.
10.
Procurement benefits: Expeditors International achieved a substantial 26.9% airfreight net revenue expansion during the second quarter of 2015. This impressive financial performance was driven by falling carrier spot buy rates as global capacity surged. Section 3 – Comparison of 2015-2016 Dynamics with 2023-2024 Normalisation

Section summary
  1. Global freight forwarders achieved substantial gross profit yields during both normalization cycles because carrier rates collapsed faster than customer contract adjustments.
  2. Coordinated carrier capacity management and major geopolitical disruptions allowed liners to defend pricing far more effectively in 2024 than during the 2015 overcapacity phase.
  3. Forwarder conversion ratios and margins remained resilient above historical averages throughout the post-pandemic normalization due to cost restructuring and process automation.

Section 4 – Carrier Supply, Transits, and Reliability Outlook for 2027-2028

Section summary
  1. Rapidly accelerating global container vessel deliveries in 2027 and 2028 threaten to create severe structural overcapacity.
  2. Geopolitical transit normalisation through the Suez Canal is releasing significant effective fleet capacity.
  3. Asset-light freight forwarders are well positioned to purchase cheap capacity from oversupplied ocean carriers.

Global Fleet Growth and Deliveries

1.
Accelerating fleet additions: Deutsche Bank Research reported in November 2026 that Clarksons expects nominal global containership capacity to expand by 9.5% in 2027 and 12.7% in 2028. This rapid supply growth is set to vastly outstrip projected global trade demand growth of only 4% in each of those years. Consequently, the container market is highly likely to face severe structural oversupply over the medium term. This supply pressure will compress carrier rates and reduce ship utilization.
2.
Challenging capacity glut: Bernstein Research projected in October 2026 that net industry supply growth will accelerate to 7.4% in 2027 and 13.2% in 2028. This surge follows a relatively benign fleet growth rate of 4.6% in 2026. Shippers and carriers will navigate an in-service fleet that is some sixty-three percent larger than its pre-pandemic baseline. Such expansion will weigh heavily on shipping line profitability and rate sustainability.
3.
Impending supply pressure: DNB Carnegie reported in November 2026 that containership deliveries will accelerate to a 10% rate in 2027. This delivery pace will expand even further to 16% in 2028 as new orders continue to arrive. The massive orderbook currently stands at 44% of the active global fleet. Such structural additions will materially erode long-term carrier pricing power as capacity outpaces demand.
4.
Massive shipyard backlogs: Jefferies Research reported in October 2026 that containership deliveries are projected to reach 3.29 million TEU in 2027. This annual volume is expected to rise further to a record 5.23 million TEU in 2028. These additions will drive annual average fleet capacity growth of 7% in 2027 and 10.6% in 2028. The incoming supply will prolong downward pressure on freight rates and asset values.

Scrapping Rates and Capacity Mitigation

5.
Minimal technical scrapping: Jefferies Research reported in September 2026 that container ship scrapping will remain extremely low until 2027 and 2028. This delay occurs because carriers locked in multi-year vessel charters during the Suez crisis. However, a substantial wave of economically driven scrapping is expected to emerge in 2028. This retirement wave is anticipated as a large portion of the global fleet exceeds twentyfive years of age.
6.
Growth-oriented carrier networks: Morgan Stanley reported in November 2026 that Maersk has shifted its strategy from fleet restriction back to active fleet growth by ordering 26 new dual-fuel container vessels of 18,600 TEU each. These vessels are scheduled for delivery through 2029 and 2030. This expansion increases capital expenditure commitments to 19.5 billion dollars. This ordering trend suggests that long-term oversupply remains a primary concern for ocean carriers.
7.
Ineffective capacity discipline: Bernstein Research reported in October 2026 that ocean carriers are unlikely to implement voluntary capacity discipline. Most shipping lines maintain net cash balance sheets, which incentivises them to defend market share. Carriers will likely engage in a game of chicken rather than scrap vessels. This competitive dynamic will collectively prolong the industry-wide margin pain as companies burn cash.
8.
Accelerated fleet decarbonisation: CMA CGM announced in October 2026 that the carrier is actively modernizing its fleet with dual-fuel vessels. The line expects to operate approximately 200 LNG and methanol-powered vessels by 2031. This transition includes the recent delivery of ten new 24,212 TEU series containerships. These capital-intensive additions will further expand global slot capacity during the transition.

Section 5 – Contract Repricing and 2027 Tender Season Timings

Section summary
  1. Shippers are holding off on signing long-term ocean contracts in anticipation of rate declines.
  2. Major ocean carriers are offering spot discounts while maintaining balanced contract-to-spot exposure splits. Net read: Freight forwarders use pricing lags and flexible procurement models to protect unit gross profits.

Tender Season Dynamics and

1.
Pricing Expectations Sluggish negotiations: Rabobank reported in March 2026 that ocean freight rates remain on a downward trend because shippers are holding off on signing 2026/27 service contracts. Shippers are deliberately delaying commitments in anticipation of further spot market declines. This tactical postponement will delay the typical Asia-Europe contract repricing window into late spring, which directly affects forwarder margin predictability.
2.
Gradual rate convergence: Maybank Investment Banking Group reported in January 2026 that Xeneta projects global average spot rates could fall by up to 25% under a normalization scenario. This normalization would bring rates to within 5% of pre-pandemic averages. This projected decline encourages shippers to demand substantial rate concessions from carriers during the upcoming 2027 tender seasons, which will compress direct operating costs.
3.
Severe pricing pressure: DNB Carnegie reported in February 2026 that industry sources expect ocean contract renewals to experience double-digit declines of up to 30% compared to 2025. This downward repricing is driven by persistent overcapacity concerns and a potential Red Sea reopening. If these contract rate reductions materialize, they will significantly compress ocean carrier profit margins throughout the outer years.

Carrier Strategies and Gemini Alliance Positioning

4.
Strategic contract expansion: Morgan Stanley reported in January 2026 that Maersk intentionally increased its long-term contract mix to 75% in 2024 from 61% in 2020. This deliberate shift aims to reduce earnings volatility in its liner business. This high contract exposure will provide a partial cushion against spot rate declines, although customers may demand renegotiations if spot rates fall significantly.
5.
Balanced volume allocation: Maersk management stated during its Q1 2025 earnings call that the carrier expects a roughly equal split between long-term and short-term volumes. This volume allocation strategy helps Maersk navigate a continuously declining rate environment. This balanced exposure allows the company to stabilize average loaded freight rates while maintaining high vessel utilization levels across its East-West networks.
6.
Weak pricing leverage: Morgan Stanley reported in January 2026 that the Gemini Alliance between Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd seeks to deliver 90% schedule reliability. However, analysts believe that schedule integrity is a weak pricing lever when the broader ocean shipping market is soft. Under soft market conditions, intense rate competition dominates and compresses any service differentiation premium carriers attempt to charge.
7.
Balanced rate exposure: Deutsche Bank Research reported in June 2025 that CMA CGM maintains a balanced 50/50 spot and contract exposure across its global networks. This split allows the carrier to capture immediate spot rate spikes while maintaining baseline volume security. This commercial positioning will underpin carrier earnings resilience during the upcoming 2027 Asia-Europe tender season as spot rates begin to normalize.
8.
Expansive commercial agreements: CMA CGM announced in July 2026 that it entered into an agreement to acquire FedEx Supply Chain for 1.4 billion dollars. Under the accompanying commercial agreements, CMA CGM will become a preferred ocean carrier for FedEx. The two companies also plan to collaborate on an air cargo capacity agreement, which will improve long-haul aircraft utilization on key strategic routes.

Spread Dynamics and Repricing Lags

9.
Decoupled margin structures: Bernstein Research reported in October 2026 that freight forwarding gross profits are structurally independent of absolute carrier freight rates. Forwarding margins are driven instead by logistics complexity and value-added services. Freight rates are essentially passed through to customers with a temporary lag, meaning that rate declines do not permanently damage forwarder earnings power.
10.
Tactical rate arbitrage: Rothschild & Co Redburn reported in October 2024 that DHL Global Forwarding typically purchases a baseline capacity of 30% to 40%. The remaining 60% to 70% of its capacity fluctuates between contracted or spot capacity depending on rate expectations.
