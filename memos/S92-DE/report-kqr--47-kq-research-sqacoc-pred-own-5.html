Section 2 – Legal Status, Timetable, and Likely Outcome of the Appeal

Section summary
  1. The US government's appeal faces a highly skeptical Federal Circuit but has achieved a temporary stay of refund requirements.
  2. Alternative statutory authorities like Section 301 have already replaced the invalidated emergency measures to maintain tariff pressure.

The Judicial Precedents

1.
Significant invalidation: The legal landscape underwent a massive shift when the US Supreme Court struck down emergency tariffs in early 2026. Chief Justice Roberts wrote for the majority in Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump on February 20, 2026, that IEEPA does not authorize unilateral tariffs. This landmark 6-3 decision established that the authority to impose taxes and custom duties is exclusively vested in the Congress. Importers quickly began leveraging this high-court precedent to demand massive administrative refunds of duties paid.
2.
Subsequent expansion: The Court of International Trade pushed the precedent further by applying the ruling to all impacted importers on March 4, 2026. These judge-led orders directed the customs agency to prepare broad repayment mechanisms with interest. In response, Customs and Border Protection launched an electronic claims portal on April 20, 2026, to process the massive volume of filings. This rapid expansion demonstrated that the lower courts intended to enforce complete remediation for the unlawful collections.
3.
Unexpected setback: The administration's backup plan under Section 122 faced immediate legal challenges from several states. The Court of International Trade ruled in Oregon v. United States on May 7, 2026, that the temporary 10% global balance-of-payments surcharge was unlawful. The court ordered the government to stop collecting these fees from the named plaintiffs and refund their paid duties. This second legal defeat highlighted the judiciary's refusal to accept broad emergency justifications for executive trade actions.
4.
Contrary evidence: The Federal Circuit provided a critical lifeline to the government by halting the immediate refund order. The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit on June 11, 2026, paused the lower court's invalidation of Section 122 tariffs. This decisive stay prevented a massive flood of mandatory refunds while the appellate proceedings run their course. Therefore, the government's legal defense remains active despite losing twice in the lower courts.

Arguments of the Parties

5.
Broad interpretation: The government's appellate brief argues that emergency statutes must be interpreted flexibly to protect national economic interests. Federal attorneys argued before the Federal Circuit in June 2026 that international payment problems require expeditious executive trade interventions. The administration maintains that Section 122 grants the president explicit authority to manage import surcharges. This flexible interpretation is designed to bypass the strict constitutional limits set by the earlier Supreme Court decision.
6.
Strict limitation: Importers counter that the administration's reliance on Section 122 is an unconstitutional distortion of a narrow statutory tool. Plaintiffs argued in their July 2026 briefs that the provision was designed solely for fixed-rate currency exchange crises. They assert that using balance-of-payments authority to address general trade deficits violates the separation of powers. Consequently, private companies expect the appellate court to affirm the lower court's strict limits on executive power.
7.
Procedural defense: The government also relies on sovereign immunity and administrative exhaustion arguments to limit the scope of potential financial exposure. Federal attorneys asserted in June 2026 that courts cannot order broad automatic refunds for finally liquidated customs entries. They argue that each importer must file specific litigation rather than receiving automated relief. This procedural barrier could protect the federal treasury from billions in claims even if the government loses on the merits.

Timeline of the Appeal

8.
Accelerated schedule: The Federal Circuit is moving the appeal forward on an expedited basis to resolve the lingering trade uncertainty. Oral arguments are projected to occur in early winter of 2026. Importers are closely watching the court docket for any indications of an expedited ruling. This rapid schedule reflects the high economic stakes involving billions of dollars in held duties.
9.
Protracted litigation: Legal experts warn that the final resolution of these tariff disputes could still drag on for several years. Economist Jiménez reported on March 29, 2026, that the administrative and legal battles over these refunds are extremely complex. Even after the Federal Circuit issues its decision, the losing party is highly likely to petition for Supreme Court review. This multi-tiered appellate process means that importers must prepare for an extended period of balance sheet volatility.

Alternative Legal Channels

10.
Successful transition: The administration has successfully bypassed the judicial setbacks by transitioning its tariff strategy to more durable trade laws. When the temporary Section 122 tariffs expired on July 24, 2026, the President immediately implemented Section 301 forced labor tariffs. These new duties of 10% to 12.5% target 60 trading partners. Because Section 301 involves formal investigations and public comment periods, it stands on a much firmer legal footing.
11.
Sectoral durability: The government is also increasingly relying on Section 232 national security investigations to protect specific manufacturing sectors. The White House utilized Section 232 in early 2026 to target semiconductors and critical minerals. Courts traditionally show significant deference to executive determinations of national security under this statute. This high legal durability makes Section 232 the primary long-term tool for rebuilding the tariff wall.
12.
Unprecedented escalation: The administration has even deployed obscure, untested statutes to penalize specific trading partners. President Trump invoked Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930 to impose a 50% tariff on Canadian goods. This aggressive move was met with a lawsuit filed in the Court of International Trade on October 20, 2026. This unprecedented escalation highlights that trade policy continues to operate in a state of high friction regardless of individual court defeats.
13.
side of the story: The legal battlefield could shift dramatically if upcoming political changes lead to a legislative repeal of the tariff authorities. Morgan Stanley reported on September 28, 2026, that executive trade actions remain highly insulated from midterm election results. However, if the opposition wins control of Congress, they have already drafted bills to repeal the underlying tariff powers. Such a legislative overhaul would permanently dismantle the administration's tariff wall and render the ongoing appeals moot.

Section 4 – Interest Treatment and Accounting Mechanics of Tariff Clawbacks

Section summary
  1. Any successful government appeal will trigger clawbacks of IEEPA tariff refunds, potentially including retroactive interest under customs guidelines.
  2. Accounting treatments under IFRS and US GAAP dictate different timelines for recognizing these potential clawback obligations.

Customs Guidelines and Interest Calculations

1.
Potential repayment: Importers face a significant risk of refund clawbacks with interest if the Federal Circuit reverses the universal refund order. U.S. Customs and Border Protection is authorized under federal law to assess interest on unpaid duties or reversed liquidations. Under 19 U.S.C. Section 1505, any underpayment of duties or fees must be paid with computer-calculated statutory interest from the date of entry. This potential clawback would immediately erase the cash flows received by major importers during 2026. and Border Protection Retroactive accrual: Customs and Border Protection tracks duty payments and potential shortfalls through its electronic Automated Commercial Environment portal. Under 19 U.S.C. Section 1505(b), CBP is required to collect increased or additional duties with interest determined at liquidation or reliquidation. If the government wins the appeal, CBP will reliquidate entries and assess interest on the clawed-back refunds from the original import date. This mechanical process ensures that interest begins to accrue from the date the debt is due until the balance is fully paid. (U.S.) and Border Protection (U.S.)

IFRS and US GAAP Accounting Standards

4.
Strict provisions: SMA Solar prepares its financial reports under IFRS, which mandates a rigorous threshold for recognizing provisions. Under IAS 37, a provision must be recognized when an entity has a present obligation from a past event, an economic outflow is probable, and a reliable estimate can be made. If a government victory becomes probable, IFRS-compliant firms must immediately move their contingent liabilities into active provisions. This transition would trigger an immediate charge in the profit and loss statement, heavily depressing reported earnings.
5.
Divergent treatment: SolarEdge prepares its financial statements under U.S. GAAP, which utilizes a different threshold for legal and tariff contingencies. Under ASC 450, companies must record an estimated loss from a loss contingency when the loss is both probable and reasonably estimable. If the loss is only reasonably possible, the company must disclose the matter but refrain from recording a liability. This distinction allows U.S. GAAP firms to delay the earnings impact of the tariff appeal longer than IFRS peers. 117 120 10Q 118 119 10Q Separate classification: Accounting standards dictate that any interest assessed on tariff clawbacks must be classified separately from the principal duties. Under IAS 37, interest and penalties related to tax exposures do not meet the definition of income taxes and are recorded within other finance costs. Similarly, under U.S. GAAP, any interest associated with tariff recoveries is recognized separately, typically within other income or interest expense. This separate presentation prevents the interest expense from distorting operating gross margins.
6.
Complex timing: The timing of recognizing clawback obligations depends on when the legal appeal is resolved or when the risk shifts. Companies must continuously evaluate the probability of an economic outflow as additional information becomes available. If the Federal Circuit issues a ruling favoring the government, the contingency must immediately be reclassified as an active liability. This reclassification would force companies to recognize the full principal and accrued interest, severely impacting their quarterly earnings per share.
