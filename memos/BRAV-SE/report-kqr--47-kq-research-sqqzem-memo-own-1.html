Operating conversion: Q3 matters first because revenue growth must convert into EBITA rather than merely enlarge a low-margin project base. Section 4 – Management Commentary and Data-Centre Execution

Pricing and competitive intensity Bravida’s Q2 call indicated that pricing had not yet clearly improved across the conventional installation market, even though management expected conditions to vary by geography and end market. Management said Bravida was prioritising cost control, customer quality and financial counterparties that could pay, rather than pursuing volume at any price. This matters for EPS because data-centre growth can be accretive only if contract discipline offsets wage, material, subcontractor and delay risk. 116 - Earnings Calls Management drew a distinction between ordinary installation tenders and technically demanding infrastructure or data-centre projects, where it said competition and pricing were better because fewer contractors possessed the necessary competence.

The company had previously said that price pressure was greater in southern Sweden and Finland than in parts of Denmark and Sweden, while data-centre and major infrastructure pricing was already comparatively attractive. The inference is supportive for margins, but it remains dependent on whether the reported price advantage survives procurement, commissioning and subcontractor negotiations. 118 - Earnings Calls DNB Carnegie’s 28 September preview supported the distinction between end markets, stating that robust infrastructure and public-construction volumes coexisted with weaker private-construction demand and industrywide pricing pressure.

DNB Carnegie nevertheless expected Bravida’s Q3 adjusted EBITA margin to rise to 6.0% from 5.3% a year earlier, partly because Swedish restructuring was thought to have ended. Its estimate implies that the group could expand earnings before the major data-centre contracts materially enter the income statement, so Q3 margin performance would be an important test of whether pricing and internal efficiency are improving independently of project ramp-up.

The strongest competitive advantage identified in the evidence is customer trust rather than absolute cost leadership. DNB Carnegie argued that hyperscalers and other operators place substantial weight on delivery certainty, references and time-to-capacity because a commissioning failure can impose financial and reputational costs far exceeding a modest difference in tender price. If that procurement logic applies to Green Mountain, atNorth and EcoDataCenter, Bravida may defend margins more effectively than in conventional building work, although the evidence does not disclose contract-level gross margins. Green Mountain and DC Nordic The Green Mountain assignment is being delivered through DC Nordic, the joint venture between Bravida and Backe Industri, and Bravida’s announced share is approximately NOK4.5 billion.

The scope includes project management, design and all technical installations under a design-and-build arrangement, making Bravida responsible for a broader delivery chain than a specialist electrical or mechanical subcontractor. That scope can increase value capture and control over interfaces, but it also increases exposure to design errors, procurement delays, coordination failures and commissioning claims. Bravida’s Q2 report said the Norwegian assignment was expected to run until 2028 or 2029, while management indicated that execution would begin toward the end of 2026.

DNB Carnegie therefore expected limited Q3 profit-and-loss contribution but a more significant increase from Q4, modelling data-centre-related organic growth of 12% year on year. The most defensible reference path is adjusted EPS of SEK7.0 in FY2026, SEK8.9 in FY2027 and SEK9.7 in FY2028, with confidence highest for the direction of improvement and lower for the timing of data-centre conversion.

DNB Carnegie FY2026–FY2028 estimates and revisions for Bravida The base hypothesis is that Bravida broadly delivers DNB Carnegie’s pre-Q3 framework, with FY2026 EPS near SEK7.0 and EPS rising to approximately SEK8.9 in FY2027 and SEK9.7 in FY2028. This requires Q3 adjusted EBITA close to the broker’s SEK417 million estimate and a 6.0% margin, followed by a more significant data-centre contribution from Q4 onward. The base case therefore assumes that Sweden’s restructuring burden has largely ended, Denmark continues recovering, Norway mobilises Green Mountain gradually, and Finland improves without requiring immediate convergence with Instalco’s higher margin.

The base case also assumes that Bravida’s roughly 49% service mix continues to cushion installation volatility. This is strategically important because service revenue is generally less cyclical than installation revenue, while Bravida’s service exposure is higher than Instalco’s 37% of 2025 revenue. The implication is that EPS can grow even if conventional construction remains uneven, provided project margins do not deteriorate and service contracts maintain reasonable pricing.

Base EPS hypothesis The operating assumptions behind the base case are consistent with Bravida’s Q2 evidence, which showed 9% group organic growth, a reported 7.5% quarterly EBITA margin and a 21% year-on-year increase in order backlog to SEK20,372 million. The important adjustment is that Q2 reported EBITA included SEK118 million of net non- recurring benefit, so sustainable EPS should be assessed against the adjusted 5.9% quarterly margin rather than the headline margin.

Country normalization is the main base-case earnings lever outside data centres. Sweden’s reported Q2 EBITA margin was 5.7% after SEK40 million of restructuring costs, Denmark’s first-half margin was 5.1%, Norway’s Q2 margin was 6.1%, and Finland’s Q2 margin was only 3.2%. A modest improvement in Sweden and Finland, rather than a full group-wide return to more than 7%, is therefore sufficient to support the base path if revenue growth remains positive.

Working capital is a necessary condition for the base case to translate into cash-supported EPS quality. Bravida’s twelve-month cash conversion was 77% at the half-year, below the 80% prior-year level, while first-half working capital consumed SEK409 million because trade receivables increased and the net contract-asset position moved negatively. The base case therefore assumes that receivables and contract assets stabilise as milestones are invoiced and that contract liabilities or prepayments partly offset the funding needs of Green Mountain and atNorth.
