Executive Summary

Section summary
  1. PGE's business model carries extraordinarily high fixed-cost operating leverage, with a 10% movement in electricity prices translating into greater than 20% impact on earnings per share, and this vulnerability can be partially resolved over the next 18 months to four years through aggressive capital reallocation toward regulated networks, but meaningful incremental cost reductions in conventional operations remain severely constrained by rigid mining overheads, union agreements, and persistent wind-down liabilities. The investigation reveals that lignite mining constitutes a largely fixed-cost business model for integrated utilities like PGE, where internally sourced lignite operations do not scale down with lower electricity production volumes, directly inflating the unit cost of each megawatt-hour produced and amplifying financial downside as generation volumes decline.

Peer evidence from CEZ demonstrates this structural challenge vividly, with lignite cash extraction costs of EUR 17 per MWh ballooning to EUR 95 per MWh total marginal costs once carbon allowance expenses of EUR 78 per MWh are included, meaning that low utilization hours significantly increase average electricity costs across the conventional fleet. Decommissioning conventional fleets leaves substantial persistent liabilities that do not disappear with capacity closures, as evidenced by PGE's PLN 435 million rise in reclamation provisions during 2025 and a separate PLN 228 million restructuring provision for employee severance at the decommissioned Dolna Odra plant, indicating that reclamation costs remain highly rigid even as power generation volumes decline and threaten future earnings per share.

The utility faces additional inflexibility through its PLN 13.5 billion hard-coal contract with Polska Grupa Górnicza running through 2028, with these take-or-pay agreements preventing fuel expense reductions when conventional generation volumes fall and elevating unit costs during generation down-cycles.

PGE recognized monumental asset impairments totaling PLN 8,650 million during the first half of 2025, fully writing off the book value of the Bełchatów and Turów generation complexes.

This accounting event caused coal energy depreciation to plunge from PLN 173 million in Q1 2025 to just PLN 60 million in Q1 2026, directly boosting conventional EBIT despite weaker operational volumes.

Sell-side analysts generally strip out these noncash impairment and depreciation effects when valuing PGE, with Bernstein Research assigning a negative PLN 12.3 billion valuation to the conventional generation division in their sum-of-the-parts model and focusing primarily on underlying cash flows and the timing of the state-backed coal asset carve-out.

Outside coal operations, PGE's newly announced Efficiency Improvement Programme projects reducing forecasted EBITDA losses by 35% in 2030 and 53% in 2035 compared to previous forecasts, representing a key strategic mechanism to stabilize retail margins outside of coal operations and support cash flow resilience.

European precedents provide instructive benchmarks, with Public Power Corporation of Greece executing one of Europe's fastest utility transitions by planning complete decommissioning of remaining lignite-fired power plants by 2026 to dramatically reduce its carbon footprint, phasing out all lignite units while expanding the regulated grid asset base to ensure stable regional growth.

Section 1 – The Key Question and Why It Matters

Section summary
  1. The business model of PGE carries high fixed-cost operating leverage that creates massive earnings sensitivity to wholesale power prices.
  2. De-risking PGE's earnings per share requires shifting capital allocation toward regulated grid networks and executing disciplined structural cost reductions.
  3. The cost structure of lignite mining behaves as an highly inflexible fixed-cost business that amplifies financial downside as generation volumes decline.
  4. Unit electricity costs for thermal power plants escalate dramatically when falling load factors prevent the efficient absorption of fixed operational overheads.
  5. High carbon emission liabilities and stickier mine maintenance expenses squeeze conventional operating margins across Polish and Czech utility peers.

Operating leverage and strategic priorities

1.
Critical assessment: The investigation addresses "how much fixed-cost operating leverage does PGE's business model carry, how much scope is there for it to work over the next 18 months to four years, and, where it is relevant to the company's strategy, how feasible is cost elimination or reduction, both incremental and in material large cuts?"
2.
Broad baseline: The research identifies nine distinct peer companies and analyzes fifteen relevant documents to map the utility landscape. These entities include Polish state-controlled giants and international peers transitioning away from solid fuels. Understanding their cost structures helps determine how fixed costs influence earnings per share over the medium term as capacity regimes change.
3.
High volatility: The financial model of PGE exhibits substantial operating leverage where modest fluctuations in market power prices translate into outsized changes in profitability. Analysts from Morgan Stanley estimated that a 10% movement in electricity prices leads to a greater than 10% shift in group EBITDA and more than 20% impact on EPS for the utility. This high sensitivity underscores why cost-reduction flexibility remains a key determinant of future earnings growth.
4.
Strategic pivot: The management of PGE aims to mitigate its volatile merchant exposure by expanding regulated network and renewable investments. Bernstein Research reports that the company's updated strategy focuses on increasing stable cash flows from regulated grids and contracted capacity to reduce reliance on thermal generation. This capital allocation shift is critical to stabilize earnings per share and support the resumption of dividends.

Interlinked regulatory and transition risks

5.
Interlinked uncertainties: The resolution of PGE's operating leverage is deeply tied to broader questions of asset separation and national regulatory frameworks. Industry economists note that the pace of the Polish energy transition must accelerate to avoid stranded conventional assets and grid bottlenecks as coal capacity declines. Furthermore, resolving the backlog of solar and wind projects is vital to unlock real long-term clean energy investments.
6.
Comparable pressures: The peers of PGE such as Enea and Tauron face similar restructuring challenges and fixed-cost overhangs. Enea historically targeted a fixed-cost reduction of PLN 500m over a three-year period to offset declining wholesale margins. These peer comparisons reveal that workforce adjustments and grid tariff determinations are critical levers for stabilizing utility earnings across the Polish sector.
7.
Restored distributions: The resumption of dividends represents a key milestone for investor confidence and overall risk reassessment. PGE intends to distribute up to 5% yield starting from fiscal year 2026 earnings, depending on net profit and leverage development in its coal segment. This policy change reflects management's belief that growing regulated contributions will successfully offset the volatile conventional generation business.

Financial sensitivity and structural constraints

8.
Regulated cushions: The distribution business offers a vital hedge against merchant market volatility but remains sensitive to regulated return formulas. Regulated tariffs determine how grid investments convert into EBITDA, with recent periods showing downward pressure on nominal WACC due to interest rate shifts. This regulatory determination directly impacts the speed at which PGE can grow its asset base and de-risk its net profit.
9.
Contrary evidence: Some analysts warn that conventional generation assets cannot be easily run down without significant residual liabilities and negative free cash flows. Noble Securities projected that Tauron would face negative cash flows starting in 2026 despite brief operational improvements. This indicates that conventional operating leverage can quickly turn negative if fuel and emissions costs rise faster than power prices.
10.
Other side of the story: While transitioning to a low-carbon model is intended to reduce risks, the heavy capital expenditure required could strain balance sheets. PGE plans to spend PLN 210bn on investments through 2035, which could elevate leverage ratios if merchant margins deteriorate further. This capital intensity means any delay in grid commissioning or offshore wind returns will directly penalize earnings per share.

Lignite cost inflexibility and operating leverage

11.
Persistent rigidity: PGE's lignite mining activities carry exceptionally high fixed costs that do not scale down with lower electricity production. Bernstein Research reported on May 8, 2025, that while hard coal generators operate on variable fuel costs, internally sourced lignite constitutes a largely fixed-cost business model for integrated utilities. This cost structure means that any reduction in generation volumes directly inflates the unit cost of each megawatt-hour produced, hurting profitability. Consequently, the company's conventional earnings are highly sensitive to load factors.
12.
High exposure: Fitch Ratings noted on January 14, 2025, that PGE's operating margins face severe pressure from structural market shifts and priority renewable grid dispatch. The agency highlighted that the fixed cost base of lignite mines exacerbates the utility's margin deterioration when generation volumes decline. Unlike hard coal plants, these mining operations require ongoing maintenance, staffing, and regulatory spending regardless of short-term power output. Without regulatory mechanisms to cover these losses, conventional profitability is highly vulnerable.
13.
Significant overheads: The annual concession and exploitation costs reported by PGE reveal substantial baseline commitments that remain fixed annually. PGE's annual report published on April 16, 2026, showed that concession costs reached PLN 23 million in 2025, while exploitation and usufruct fees stood at PLN 137 million. These regulatory fees are accompanied by massive labor costs and site maintenance overheads across the Bełchatów and Turów complexes. Such rigid operational outlays prevent management from rapidly reducing segment costs when market demand weakens.
14.
Escalating liabilities: Lignite-based generation at CEZ demonstrates a similar high-operating-leverage cost structure tied directly to carbon and mining overheads. CEZ stated in an investor presentation on June 8, 2026, that its own lignite cash extraction costs stood at EUR 17 per MWh, but total marginal costs reached EUR 95 per MWh. This massive difference is driven by carbon allowance costs of EUR 78 per MWh, which dominate the marginal cost structure of conventional assets. As a result, low utilization hours significantly increase the average cost of electricity.

Conventional fuel variables and peer comparisons

15.
Variable dynamics: Hard coal generation stands in contrast to lignite by utilizing a more flexible, variable fuel purchase model. Bernstein Research observed on May 8, 2025, that PGE's hard coal plants purchase fuel from external mining companies, making their costs predominantly variable rather than fixed. This allows hard coal units to adjust their spending during down-cycles by simply reducing coal purchases. However, negative spreads can still force these units to operate at a loss or face contractual fines.
16.
Contractual rigidities: Enea's experience with long-term fuel procurement illustrates how contractual terms can transform variable fuel costs into rigid obligations. The CFO of Tauron explained during a first-quarter briefing on May 19, 2014, that the utility faced potential contractual fines because warmer weather prevented it from burning minimum required coal volumes. Similarly, Enea Generation signed a coal delivery contract with Bogdanka valued at PLN 755 million in 2013 to manage its fuel needs. These take-or-pay structures limit a utility's ability to lower costs during volume down-cycles.
17.
Declining profitability: Operating margins at conventional power plants deteriorate rapidly as high carbon prices intersect with falling utilization rates. Carbon Tracker reported in a study on December 8, 2017, that older conventional units commissioned decades ago carry much higher operating costs. The research showed that lignite assets have higher fixed costs due to the substantial annual outlays associated with running integrated mines. These fixed outlays cannot be easily mitigated when load factors fall, resulting in stranded value.
18.
Contrary evidence: Some conventional operations can maintain profit margins through volatile cycles by optimizing their supply structures and hedging electricity sales. CEZ reported a massive 88% year-on-year surge in 9M22 EBITDA to CZK 89.3 billion despite surging input costs. This exceptional performance was driven by a strong contribution from fossil fuels and trading optimization. It shows that conventional operating leverage can work highly in a utility's favor during pricing up-cycles when output is hedged.

Load factor degradation and peer performance

19.
Escalating unit costs: Unit costs per megawatt-hour rise dramatically as declining utilization rates force fixed costs to be spread over fewer operating hours. Carbon Tracker analyzed on April 8, 2020, how conventional generators face a persistent headwind from lower capacity factors. The analysis concluded that fixed operations and maintenance costs represent 8% of long-run marginal costs, and every percentage point drop in the capacity factor reduces operating cash flows by USD 0.42 per MWh. This relationship penalizes plants that run on low-load mid-merit cycles.
20.
Severe margin pressure: The rapid wind-down of ZE PAK's conventional fleet reveals the extreme operational deleveraging that occurs during a phase-out. ZE PAK's annual report for 2025 noted that the scale of reduction in fixed costs associated with coal operations is proportionally smaller than the scale of the operational reduction. This structural imbalance caused cost of sales in Q1 2026 to rise 18.26% year-on-year to PLN 316.31 million despite a smaller generation footprint. 21.
Inflexible operations: Technical limitations of coal-fired units prevent operators from rapidly cycling plants to avoid periods of unprofitable pricing. Morgan Stanley explained in a report on January 13, 2026, that coal plants have high minimum load requirements, ranging from 25% to 40% for hard coal and 50% to 60% for lignite. This operational inflexibility forces utilities to maintain minimum generation thresholds even during negative price events. Consequently, conventional operators must absorb substantial efficiency losses and restart costs.
22.
Structural shifts: The transition toward renewables has permanently altered the merit order and depressed conventional utilization rates. The World Bank noted on March 13, 2025, that Polish lignite plants have historically held must-run status to ensure they operate at at least 60% capacity, which is the cutoff for profitability. However, the rapid expansion of solar and wind has forced frequent curtailments of low-cost renewables to accommodate these inflexible coal units. This systemic conflict highlights how conventional assets struggle to remain competitive.

Structural cost control and peer strategies

23.
Targeted savings: Peer utility cost-reduction programs show that while some administrative overheads are cuttable, core operational costs remain highly stubborn. Enea Group announced an ambitious plan to reduce fixed costs by PLN 500 million over three years starting in 2014. The program focused on system power plants, shared service centers, and cutting grid losses. Despite these efforts, conventional operating costs remained heavily exposed to volatile fuel and carbon markets.
24.
Complex adjustments: Accounting adjustments and revised depreciation schedules can temporarily cushion conventional EBIT but do not alter cash flows. CEZ Group adjusted its depreciation method for coal assets on October 1, 2024, shifting from a linear basis to the sum-of-years' digits method. This accounting change targeted a total depreciable amount of CZK 73.2 billion to capture the decreasing economic benefits of conventional generation. While this reduces trailing EBIT paper losses, it leaves the underlying cash cost of carbon and mining untouched.
25.
Other side of the story: Lignite assets can maintain high profitability and strong cash generation during market up-cycles if the company benefits from fully integrated mine-to-plant operations. Morgan Stanley noted on January 18, 2013, that PGE covers approximately 70% of its fuel needs through internally sourced lignite. This high level of self-sufficiency shields the utility from international coal price spikes. Under these conditions, conventional generation can generate robust EBITDA margins when wholesale power prices remain elevated and carbon costs are hedged.

Section 3 – Persistent Liabilities and Wind-Down Costs of Fleet Decommissioning

Section summary
  1. Decommissioning conventional generation fleets leaves substantial persistent liabilities that do not scale down with capacity closures.
  2. Land reclamation provisions and long-term fuel contracts create heavy cash outflows even after electricity production ceases.

Environmental and reclamation provisions

1.
Growing liabilities: PGE faced rising long-term liabilities as environmental provisions and restructuring costs expanded during its phased fleet retirement. The utility reported that its reclamation provisions rose by PLN 435 million in 2025 as conventional generation operations contracted. Furthermore, PGE established a restructuring provision of PLN 228 million in 2025 to cover employee severance packages at the decommissioned Dolna Odra plant. These ongoing outlays indicate that reclamation costs remain highly rigid even as power generation volumes decline, which threatens future earnings per share.
2.
Sticky obligations: Tauron and Energa maintained substantial environmental provisions to manage conventional asset decommissioning. Tauron reported a total provision for dismantling fixed assets and reclaiming land of PLN 293 million at the end of 2025, which rose to PLN 299 million by mid-2026. At the same time, Energa carried land reclamation and liquidation provisions of PLN 201 million in late 2026. This persistent provision level shows that environmental liabilities linger for decades after the operational life of conventional plants ends, limiting the scope for immediate cost elimination.
3.
Extended timelines: CEZ demonstrated the long-term cash flow drag of mining liabilities, with site reclamation schedules extending decades beyond active mining operations. The Czech utility carried mining reclamation provisions of CZK 15.77 billion as of late 2025, with restricted funds of CZK 7.38 billion set aside in escrow. CEZ expects the highest utilization of these provisions to occur between 2031 and 2040 to handle residual mine pits. This elongated timeline confirms that conventional wind-down costs are deferred rather than eliminated, penalizing medium-term cash flows.
4.
Contrary evidence: ZE PAK reported that some decommissioning costs can be fully offset by selling salvaged materials from closed power plants. The private generator noted that experience from dismantling the Adamów plant indicated that bids for scrap, aggregate, and salvaged equipment could cover the entire cost of unit decommissioning. If salvage values remain high, decommissioning cash outflows will be lower than original balance sheet provisions, providing some upside for corporate earnings.

Rigid contractual and regulatory obligations

5.
Inflexible agreements: PGE's fuel procurement strategy is constrained by long-term take-or-pay agreements that convert variable fuel needs into rigid financial obligations. The utility remains committed to a PLN 13.5 billion hard-coal contract with Polska Grupa Górnicza signed on December 13, 2023, which runs through 2028. These contracts prevent the utility from reducing fuel expenses when conventional generation volumes fall. Consequently, take-or-pay contracts elevate unit costs during generation down-cycles and compress conventional profit margins.
6.
Unexpected surcharges: Polish utilities faced significant cash drains from retroactive regulatory interventions related to the national Price Difference Payout Fund. Energa-Obrót paid PLN 553 million to the fund in March 2026 following a dispute with the energy regulator, while Tauron recognized a related provision of PLN 277 million in late 2025. These payments do not represent ongoing operational costs but are non-refundable cash outflows. This regulatory regime shows how wind-down years remain highly vulnerable to policy-driven financial penalties.
7.
Rigid logistics: Tauron also struggled with rigid coal supply arrangements that restricted its ability to optimize conventional fuel costs during down-cycles. The group signed coal delivery agreements with PGG valued at PLN 520 million to cover its generation needs through early 2024. These agreements, combined with logistics disruptions, prevented Tauron from rapidly adjusting fuel intake as demand fluctuated. This operational rigidity indicates that fuel procurement costs remain fixed in the short term, dragging down conventional segment margins.

Workforce obligations and peer wind-down cases

8.
Stubborn overheads: ZE PAK illustrated that shutting down a conventional fleet does not immediately eliminate its fixed operational costs. The company completed its continuous coal mining operations in mid-2026 but faced ongoing reclamation and public levy expenses. ZE PAK's fixed cost reductions were proportionally smaller than its revenue declines, leading to trailing operating losses. This structural lag demonstrates that closing conventional units leaves trailing liabilities, which limits immediate profit recovery.
9.
Slow recovery: ZE PAK faced a lengthy and capitalintensive land restoration process, with only a fraction of exploited areas fully reclaimed. The company reported that only 38% of its land had been reclaimed as of early 2026, leaving 62% requiring extensive environmental treatment. These restoration projects must be funded from accumulated cash or asset sales after coal revenues cease. This funding gap confirms that reclamation represents a persistent cash drain long after generation stops.
10.
Structured exits: RWE managed its conventional wind-down liabilities through state compensation and strategic asset ring-fencing to protect its clean growth business. The German utility agreed to phase out lignite by 2030, supported by €2.6 billion in government compensation. RWE carried €5.4 billion in mining provisions in late 2025, which were covered by its 15% stake in E.ON. This funding mechanism ensures that mining liabilities do not disrupt capital allocated to renewables expansion.
11.
Other side of the story: Conventional fleet wind-down costs can be mitigated if state aid packages and labor protection agreements are fully secured by the utility. RWE received €1.3 billion of its €2.6 billion German state aid package by mid-2026, while ZE PAK employees transitioned smoothly under the Social Protection Act. If governments subsidize these social and environmental transition costs, utilities can avoid major EPS hits during the conventional wind-down phase.

Section 4 – EBITDA Volatility, Capacity Payments, and Coal Fleet Impairments

Section summary
  1. PGE conventional generation segment experienced severe EBITDA volatility from 2019 to 2026 due to electricity price and volume swings.
  2. Monumental asset impairment write-downs in 2025 significantly reduced conventional depreciation, boosting reported operating profits from 2026.
  3. PGE holds a distinct capacity market revenue advantage over local peers, stabilizing conventional revenues through 2028.

Historical EBITDA and volume trends

1.
Extreme volatility: PGE experienced substantial fluctuations in its conventional generation segment profitability during the macroeconomic cycles from 2019 to 2026. The research for this section identified five Polish energy companies and analyzed ten distinct regulatory and broker documents as relevant sources. This volatility demonstrates the high operating leverage of its fossil fuel operations.
2.
Pricing spikes: PGE capitalized on exceptionally high wholesale electricity prices during the European energy crisis in fiscal year 2022. The company achieved over PLN 7 billion in recurring group EBITDA in 2022 despite facing massive inflationary pressure and state regulatory interventions. However, surging carbon and coal fuel costs prevented the utility from fully translating these high spot prices into net margin gains.
3.
Margin compression: PGE faced an intense margin squeeze in its conventional power segment during 2024 and 2025 as wholesale prices returned to historical averages. The utility reported that lower electricity prices and falling generation volumes caused a significant decline in its conventional energy production margins. This downward cycle forced the company to suspend dividend payments to protect its balance sheet.
4.
Volume recovery: PGE recorded a notable operational rebound in the second quarter of 2026 when coal energy generation volumes increased significantly. The management reported in an interim presentation on September 16, 2026, that segment electricity production rose by 18% during the quarter. This temporary volume increase helped recover conventional segment EBITDA to PLN 763 million for the three months ended June 30, 2026.
5.
Structural shifts: PGE remains highly exposed to wholesale power price volatility because conventional power generation represents its largest merchant risk. Analysts from Morgan Stanley noted in November 2014 that a 10% decline in electricity prices would severely hurt PGE's conventional earnings. Therefore, the company's long-term profitability remains highly dependent on either power price stabilization or rapid asset separation.

Impairments and depreciation relief

6.
Massive impairments: PGE recognized monumental non-cash asset impairment write-downs in its coal energy segment during the first half of fiscal year 2025. The company's interim report published on September 16, 2026, confirmed that impairment write-downs of non-current assets totaled PLN 8,650 million. These write-downs fully wrote off the book value of the Bełchatów and Turów generation complexes.
7.
Depreciation relief: PGE saw its conventional segment depreciation charges drop precipitously from the start of fiscal year 2026 because of the 2025 impairments. The company's financial report showed that coal energy depreciation fell to PLN 60 million in the first quarter of 2026 from PLN 173 million in the first quarter of 2025. This accounting reduction directly boosted conventional EBIT despite weaker operational volumes.
8.
Sustained impact: PGE maintained this lower depreciation run-rate into the second quarter of 2026 to cushion its reported operating profit. The company's segment accounts for the three months ended June 30, 2026, showed coal energy depreciation of PLN 59 million compared to PLN 171 million in the prior year. This drop of over 65% in depreciation paper costs helped the segment report a positive EBIT of PLN 646 million.
9.
Contrary evidence: The downward trend in conventional asset book values did not trigger any further impairment write-downs during the first half of 2026 despite persistent market headwinds. The company reported on September 16, 2026, that its impairment tests as of May 31, 2026, concluded that no additional write-downs or reversals were necessary. This shows that the coal fleet's carrying value of PLN 3,966 million had stabilized.
10.
Analyst perspective: Sell-side analysts generally adjust their financial models to stripped out these non-cash impairment and depreciation effects when valuing PGE. Bernstein Research noted on May 8, 2025, that the conventional generation division was valued at negative PLN 12.3 billion in their sum-of-the-parts model. Analysts focus primarily on underlying cash flows and the eventual timing of the state-backed coal asset carve-out.

Capacity payments and peer dynamics

11.
Capacity revenues: PGE benefited from substantial capacity market revenues that provided a critical financial buffer for its conventional generation assets. The utility reported that its conventional generation segment earned PLN 2.2 billion from capacity contracts in 2023 and PLN 2.25 billion in 2024. These contracted revenues helped stabilize segment EBITDA during periods of clean-dark spread compression.
12.
Peer comparisons: PGE secured a significant competitive advantage over its local Polish utility peers through its proactive capacity contracting strategy. Bernstein Research reported on May 8, 2025, that peers like Enea, Energa, and Tauron face a capacity revenue cliff starting in 2026. However, PGE secured five-year capacity contracts in 2024 that ensure stable capacity payments for its old coal assets until the end of 2028.
13.
Resource challenges: Tauron experienced severe financial pressure on its generation segment earlier than PGE due to differences in asset age and capacity agreements. Financial analysts from ING Wholesale Banking noted that Tauron was forced to recognize PLN 240 million in generation asset write-offs as early as June 2013. These write-offs proved that conventional cash flows were highly sensitive to wholesale pricing and environmental compliance costs.
14.
Outperforming peers: PGE's integrated lignite operations historically yielded superior margins compared to peers that rely heavily on merchant hard coal. Broker research from ING Wholesale Banking highlighted that PGE and ZE PAK were the main beneficiaries of historical power price increases due to their self-sufficient lignite mines. This structural self-sufficiency protected PGE's earnings from international coal price spikes during pricing up-cycles.
15.
Other side of the story: While the massive depreciation reduction artificially inflates PGE's reported operating profit from 2026, it does not improve the underlying cash generation of the coal fleet. Bernstein Research emphasized on May 8, 2025, that clean-dark spreads remain deeply compressed, meaning conventional cash flows will continue to deteriorate. Therefore, the accounting relief from impairments will not prevent conventional operations from draining cash unless power prices rebound.

Section 5 – Polish Distribution Tariffs and Grid EBITDA Translation

Section summary
  1. Regulated grid tariffs in Poland translate growing capital investments into stable EBITDA through a formulaic regulatory framework.
  2. High interest rates and specific reinvestment incentives boosted distribution returns until a sharp drop in yields lowered the 2026 tariff WACC.

Regulatory asset base and EBITDA translation

1.
Predictable returns: Polish distribution companies operate as natural monopolies that convert capital investments into predictable earnings. The President of the Energy Regulatory Office sets the distribution tariffs to cover reasonable operating costs and ensure a justified return. This framework directly links regulatory asset base growth to EBITDA expansion.
2.
Growing assets: Capital expenditures in the network segment drive the expansion of the regulatory asset base. Polish distribution system operators signed the Charter of Effective Transition of Distribution Grids to align their investments with national transition targets. This coordinated effort is designed to accelerate network modernization through 2030 while securing stable returns.
3.
Incentivized expansion: The regulatory framework incorporates a reinvestment premium to encourage grid operators to fund critical transition projects. This premium is added to the base weighted average cost of capital depending on the investment priorities agreed with the regulator. For example, Enea reported that its WACC reinvestment premium in 2025 was higher than in 2024.
4.
Operational adjustments: Regulated revenues are adjusted annually through true-up mechanisms to account for volume fluctuations and balancing differences. These retroactive regulatory settlements can heavily impact segment EBITDA by correcting for past over-recoveries or underrecoveries. For instance, Enea Operator recorded a substantial drop in energy purchase costs to cover grid losses in 2025.

WACC mechanics and the 2026 contraction

5.
Formulaic sensitivity: The regulated cost of capital in Poland is highly sensitive to macroeconomic interest rate cycles. The base risk-free rate within the WACC formula is determined using the average yields of 10-year Polish government bonds. Consequently, periods of falling yields compress the allowed return on assets and penalize segment profitability.

Peak returns: Polish distribution companies achieved historically high regulated returns during the high interest rate environment of 2025. Tauron recorded a peak weighted average cost of capital of 10.8% in its 2025 tariff. This favorable rate combined with a growing asset base to boost segment EBITDA to record levels during the year.

Sharp contraction: The regulatory return environment deteriorated rapidly in 2026 as macroeconomic interest rates began to normalize. Tauron experienced a significant 1.34-percentage-point reduction in its 2026 tariff WACC compared to 2025. This downward adjustment squeezed margins and drove distribution EBITDA lower in the first half of 2026.
8.
Contrary evidence: Tauron's total Distribution segment EBITDA increased to PLN 4,788 million in 2025 from PLN 3,963 million in 2024 despite lower distributed volumes. This expansion shows that grid modernization and retroactive settlements can sometimes overcompensate for operational volume drops. Therefore, volume risks do not always translate into immediate cash flow deterioration.

Broker expectations and forward projections

9.
Expected normalisation: Financial analysts expect PGE to experience a multi-year contraction in regulated returns as inflation and bond yields decline. Bernstein Research forecasts PGE's distribution return to gradually normalize from 12.96% in 2025 to 8.0% by 2030. This normalization will act as a major drag on the utility's regulated earnings growth over the medium term.
10.
Gradual compression: Broker projections for peer utilities confirm a sector-wide downward trend in regulated returns through the late 2020s. Noble Securities expects Enea's distribution WACC to fall from 9.35% in 2026 to 7.5% in 2028. Analysts believe that a deeper cut would demotivate distribution companies from continuing their highly ambitious capital expenditure programs.
11.
Stable returns: Orlen's Energa segment also relies heavily on stable distribution tariffs to fund its capital expenditure programs. Morgan Stanley base estimates for Energa's network are anchored on a regulatory WACC of 10.48% on a regulatory asset base of PLN 15.52 billion. This substantial asset base provides reliable EBITDA that helps offset volatility in the group's conventional power generation assets.
12.
Regulatory stability: The local regulatory authority seeks to maintain a balanced framework that supports grid investments without overcompensating operators. The Energy Regulatory Office designed the distribution framework to avoid major penalties or excessive remuneration during the initial years of implementation. This cautious approach helps stabilize regulated cash flows for major utilities during periods of macroeconomic volatility.
13.
Sector comparisons: Polish utilities allocate a vast majority of their capital expenditure to regulated distribution networks to reduce merchant exposure. Tauron directed 68% of its total capital expenditure to its distribution network in 2025. 195 196 This concentrated investment strategy mirrors PGE's focus on growing its regulated share of group EBITDA over the decade.

Regulatory uncertainty and grid constraints

14.
Systemic bottlenecks: Electrification and renewable integration are creating severe capacity constraints across European distribution grids. Regulators face the difficult challenge of balancing grid connection incentives with customer affordability. This tension makes cost-recovery mechanisms and allowed returns a central source of dispute between utilities and state authorities.
15.
Other side of the story: Rapidly rising network tariffs designed to recover grid investments could trigger an unintended feedback loop that hurts utility cash flows. High tariffs incentivize commercial customers to invest in self-generation and behind-the-meter storage solutions to bypass the public grid. This defection leaves fewer users to bear fixed costs, placing further upward pressure on tariffs and complicating regulatory cost recovery.

Section 6 – Headcount Restructuring and Workforce Reduction Programmes

Section summary
  1. Polish utilities rely heavily on voluntary leave programs and natural retirements to manage headcount reductions during the coal phase-out.
  2. Rigid collective bargaining agreements and strong trade union representation limit the speed of labor cost reductions across the state-controlled sector.

Employment trends and labor rigidity

1.
Steady contraction: PGE recorded a decline in total headcount as its conventional energy assets began their phased transition. The utility reported that its headcount fell to 41,690 employees at the end of 2025 from 42,898 employees at the end of 2024. Management noted in its 2024 report that this 10.2% employee turnover was driven primarily by natural retirements and increased hiring in the renewables segment. These steady departures help reduce long-term personnel expenses without triggering active union disputes.
2.
Gradual downsizing: Tauron maintained stable average employment levels while preparing for structural changes in its core generation businesses. The group's management board reported that average employment in the first half of 2026 stood at 18,867 full-time equivalents, down slightly by 0.1% compared to 18,889 in 2025. This minor reduction shows that immediate labor cost flexibility remains limited due to ongoing operational requirements and regulatory grid commitments.
3.
Funded restructuring: Enea utilized strategic provisions to prepare for substantial future workforce reductions across its fossil fuel operations. The utility's consolidated financial statements for 2023 showed a persistent provision of PLN 454 million allocated for its Voluntary Leave Programme. This substantial funding suggests that Enea expects material cash outflows for severance as conventional generation units are gradually retired.
4.
Accelerated transition: ZE PAK executed rapid labor force downsizings as it completed the closure of its continuous lignite mining operations. The private generator reported that workforce restructuring affected 504 employees in the first half of 2026, with 307 receiving severance payments. These rapid cuts demonstrate that private operators can achieve faster fixed-cost labor reductions than state-controlled peers during decommissioning phases.
5.
Contrary evidence: PGE's overall employee benefit expenses increased significantly despite the reported reduction in total headcount. The company's annual financial report showed that total employee benefits expenses rose to PLN 8,163 million in 2024 from PLN 7,462 million in 2023. This wage inflation shows that rising average salaries can completely offset the savings achieved through voluntary redundancy schemes.

Transition agreements and social protection

6.
High protection: State-controlled utilities operate under heavy trade union influence that protects core labor lines from unilateral corporate downsizing. PGE disclosed in its 2025 annual report that trade union density remained at 67%, while 82% of its employees were covered by collective agreements. This high coverage means that any large-scale compulsory layoffs require extensive and costly negotiations with social partners.
7.
Legal shields: The Polish government established a statutory framework to subsidize labor transitions and protect coal sector workers. ZE PAK employees became the first beneficiaries of the Social Protection Act in December 2024, securing paid leaves at 80% of their salary. This state-backed mechanism allowed ZE PAK to reduce its direct salary costs during the rapid winddown of its mining complexes.
8.
Attempted modernization: Enea's management pursued structural changes to its labor agreements to improve operational agility and match modern market realities. The company proposed a modernized and more transparent collective bargaining agreement in late 2025 to replace legacy frameworks. This initiative indicates that utilities are actively trying to increase labor cost flexibility as transition pressures mount.
9.
Other side of the story: While voluntary leave schemes and natural retirements help utilities downsize, strong union agreements and rising wages limit overall savings. PGE and Tauron have concluded multiple pay agreements that increased basic remuneration, with Tauron signing three new wage agreements in 2025. These wage increases, combined with rigid collective agreements covering over 80% of staff, mean that labor cost reduction remains slow and capital-intensive.

Section 9 – If the Answer Differs from the Common View

Section summary
  1. Underestimating the stickiness of coal mining costs and the speed of carbon pricing reforms poses severe earnings risks for Polish utilities.
  2. Retroactive tariff interventions and trade union resistance represent the primary disruptions to the consensus conventional wind-down trajectory.

Macroeconomic risks and cost stickiness

1.
Severe pressure: PGE's financial performance faces substantial risks if the transition away from solid fuels occurs faster than consensus forecasts assume. Fitch Ratings highlighted that the utility's competitive position is weakening as renewable energy sources with priority grid dispatch reduce conventional generation volumes. This structural shift lowers conventional energy margins, creating a severe profitability threat that the fixed mining cost base of lignite operations further exacerbates.
2.
Escalating risks: Polish energy groups remain highly vulnerable to sudden regulatory changes in European carbon pricing. Analysts from ING Wholesale Banking pointed out that the scheduled entry into force of the ETS2 system in 2028 represents a significant political risk for national utilities. This carbon pricing mechanism will push fuel prices higher, potentially accelerating the financial decline of conventional assets before alternative low-emission capacity is built.
3.
Sharp corrections: Historical precedents demonstrate that rising carbon allowance prices can quickly derail utility valuations. Broker reports from ING Wholesale Banking documented that PGE, Energa, and Enea saw their share prices fall sharply in 2018 due to escalating carbon permit costs. Vulnerable earnings: Future electricity demand growth remains a key source of uncertainty for the viability of expensive baseload investments. Reports from the Polish Economic Institute indicate that the future of the country's energy-intensive industries is highly uncertain as they grapple with transition costs. Any major downturn in industrial power consumption would depress wholesale electricity prices, severely penalizing the operating margins of unhedged power plants.

Contrary evidence: State authorities are pushing back against rapid closures to ensure national energy security during transition periods. Polish State Assets Minister Balczun stated that the country does not need to phase out its mining complexes or conventional power stations at any cost. The minister argued that these existing coal assets can successfully stabilize the national power system for many years to come as nuclear energy sources remain far from completion.

Competitiveness and regulatory interventions

6.
Deteriorating competitiveness: High domestic generation costs are creating severe macroeconomic headwinds for Poland's industrial sectors. Financial analysts at ING Wholesale Banking warned that high carbon allowance prices keep Polish wholesale electricity prices significantly higher than those in Germany. This persistent pricing premium hurts the competitiveness of local manufacturers, which may force them to reduce domestic production or defect from the grid entirely.
7.
Intrusive intervention: Retail supply margins face direct threats from state-imposed price caps designed to shield households from market volatility. The Polish Ministry of Energy drafted legislation to guarantee maximum gas prices for protected customers in 2027 based on legacy tariffs. While this plan includes a compensation scheme for gas trading firms, such retroactive political interventions restrict the retail margin recovery of integrated utilities.
8.
Rigid delays: Strong political resistance from regional mining unions threatens to derail scheduled conventional asset decommissioning. Academic research shows that Śląskie Province maintains significant political influence by employing approximately 80,000 people in coal mining activities. This powerful regional lobbying helped secure a social contract that extends domestic mine operations to 2049, delaying the necessary energy transition and keeping expensive assets online.
9.
Transition barriers: Tight labor markets and structural shortages of skilled personnel could severely delay the expansion of clean energy networks. Analysts from ING Wholesale Banking emphasized that a shortage of construction workers and energy specialists acts as a primary bottleneck for grid upgrades. These supply chain frictions will likely extend the reliance on older coal units, elevating system balancing costs and dragging on the net profit of integrated operators.
10.
Other side of the story: Technological progress and market forces are driving a rapid transition away from coal despite institutional inertia. Forum Energii's yearbook documented that Poland's domestic steam coal production has plunged by 47% since 2016 as renewable generation expands. The rapid build-out of solar and wind capacity reached a key milestone when renewable generation exceeded coal production for the first time in June 2025.
