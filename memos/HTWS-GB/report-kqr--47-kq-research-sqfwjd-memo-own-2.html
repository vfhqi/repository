Section 2 – DRC Law No. 20/017 and Regulatory Interpretations

Section summary
  1. The Democratic Republic of Congo is enforcing Article 40 of Law No. 20/017, requiring telecommunications companies to transfer thirty percent of their capital to local interests by July 2027.
  2. The regulatory watchdog actively engages major mobile network operators, but the direct applicability of these provisions to passive infrastructure providers like Helios Towers remains a subject of ongoing legal debate.

Mandating Local Telecom Capital

1.
Accelerating compliance: The Democratic Republic of Congo has retroactively halved the ten-year moratorium for telecommunications ownership compliance, establishing a firm deadline of July 2027. Under Minister of Posts and Telecommunications José Mpanda's decree signed on February 27, 2026, foreign operators must open up 25% of their share capital to local investors. This rapid acceleration is designed to halve the ten-year moratorium and localize the economic benefits of the rapidly growing $2.09 billion telecom market. This regulatory shift threatens the long-term equity structures of foreign-backed players by mandating local participation.
2.
Mandated employee quotas: President Félix Tshisekedi ordered regulators to enforce the long-ignored employee ownership provisions of Article 40 of Law No. 20/017. This specific provision requires telecommunications operators to allocate a 5% equity stake to local Congolese workers within five months. The presidency described the delayed implementation as a legal and social anomaly that excludes local workers from profit participation. For foreign-owned firms, this means local workers will become equity shareholders eligible for annual dividend distributions.
3.
Contrary evidence: Minister of Posts and Telecommunications José Mpanda's decree signed on February 27, 2026, specifically names active mobile operators like Airtel, Africell, Vodacom, and Orange when discussing the accelerated July 2027 deadline. This focus on active mobile network operators suggests that the primary target of the ownership law is active licensees rather than passive infrastructure providers. If the regulator limits enforcement to active carriers, Helios Towers' DRC subsidiaries could avoid direct capital transfer requirements.

Regulatory Watchdog Enforcement Actions

4.
Regulator engagement: The Ministry of Posts and telecom watchdog ARPTC are actively engaging major foreign-backed operators to establish pathways for the equity transfer. These discussions are focused on defining financial and procedural structures to execute the 30% capital localization by July 2027. The regulator is pushing for strict adherence to ensure that local citizens and employees participate in the sector's financial gains. This direct intervention signals a high risk of forced restructuring or capital dilution for foreign telecom interests.
5.
Market scale comparisons: Vodacom Congo reported annual revenues of $715.2 million for its fiscal year ending March 31, 2025. This performance represents a substantial share of the wider DRC telecommunications sector, which reached overall revenues of $2.09 billion in 2024. The significant size of these operations explains why the presidency is targeting major foreign-controlled operators for local equity localization. Dilution of these revenues through local share transfers will directly reduce parent-level earnings per share.
6.
Diverse ownership structures: Foreign parent companies maintain highly centralized control of their DRC telecom subsidiaries compared to the mandated local ownership targets. While Orange RDC and Airtel Congo RDC are 100% foreign-owned, Vodacom Congo is 51% owned by Vodacom Group and 49% by Congo Wireless Network. These existing structures mean that some operators face a much larger compliance gap than others to reach the 30% Congolese ownership threshold. This uneven playing field may lead to varying strategies and timelines for regulatory compliance.
7.
Subscriber tracking reforms: The ARPTC reported that the DRC reached 73.28 million active mobile subscriptions by late September 2025. In tandem with capital localization, the regulator is implementing stricter user identification rules to ban pre-registered SIM cards. This dual push for regulatory compliance and user registration increases the operational overhead for all telecommunications operators. These strict compliance requirements could slow down the acquisition of new customers and impact carrier earnings.
8.
Mining sector precedent: The DRC government's parallel enforcement of local equity rules in the mining sector highlights a broad localization policy. Mines Minister Louis Watum directed mining firms to prove that 5% of their capital was held by local employees by July 31, 2026. However, operators continue to dispute the 10% national ownership rule due to disagreements over implementation and retroactive application. This ongoing dispute indicates that local ownership mandates face intense legal pushback and operational delays.

Impact on Tower Infrastructure Providers

9.
Tower license exposure: Helios Towers operates its critical DRC business through 100% indirectly owned local subsidiaries, including Helios Towers DRC S.A.R.L. Because these subsidiaries hold passive infrastructure licenses rather than active mobile network licenses, their exposure to Law No. 20/017 depends on regulatory interpretations. If the ARPTC defines passive tower operators as telecommunications operators, Helios Towers must divest 30% of its local equity by July 2027. This forced divestment would severely dilute consolidated earnings per share due to minority interest leakage.
10.
Rural infrastructure initiatives: Orange RDC and Vodacom Congo established a pioneering joint venture to build and operate solar-powered mobile base stations in rural DRC. This joint venture represents a new model of shared active and passive infrastructure where both operators act as anchor tenants. The joint venture intends to open its passive infrastructure to other mobile network operators to foster local competition. This partnership demonstrates that operators are increasingly looking at shared infrastructure models to optimize capital expenditure and regulatory alignment.
11.
Compliance risks quantified: The potential application of Law No. 20/017 to passive infrastructure players represents a major downside risk for Helios Towers. Because the DRC segment contributes approximately thirty percent of group earnings, a mandatory 30% local equity transfer would materially dilute earnings per share. Investors must monitor upcoming ARPTC decrees to determine if passive tower operators will receive a formal exemption. Unless a clear exemption is codified, the threat of equity dilution will continue to depress valuation multiples.
12.
Other side of the story: The strict enforcement of Law No. 20/017 may ultimately exclude passive tower operators because regulators are focusing their limited resources on active mobile carriers. Additionally, establishing local partnership structures could strengthen Helios Towers' regulatory relations and accelerate approvals for new site builds. By partnering with Congolese interests at fair valuations, the company could offset equity dilution through faster tenancy expansion. Thus, the mandate could serve as a catalyst for longterm growth rather than a pure financial headwind.
