Executive Summary

Section summary
  1. The evidence indicates a low-to-moderate risk of punitive regulatory intervention against OTE over 2026 to 2028. The most likely pressure is a managed wholesale repricing rather than a windfall tax or retail price cap. The report assigns high confidence to low intervention risk in Greece. That confidence rests on a supportive regulator, sector precedents that were confined to energy and banking, and a ruling party that leads the polls. Medium-confidence Western and Central and Eastern European (CEE) precedents, plus a fragmented-parliament risk in spring 2027, keep the tail risk from being negligible.
1.
Regulatory stance: EETT has proposed a 10% to 13% wholesale price decrease for FTTH broadband alongside a 4% to 5% increase for copper-based broadband to incentivize fiber migration. This is a steering tool, not a profit cap. An earlier copper adjustment did not hurt OTE's profits because the upsell to higher-priced fiber lines offset it. EETT's benchmarking approach, which weighs service quality and network investment alongside prices, suggests it is unlikely to impose arbitrary price caps. What broker figures already assume: The cited broker figures price in a modest, scheduled intervention, not a punitive one. Deutsche Bank Research expects a 10% fiber price cut as EETT reviews copper and fiber wholesale pricing. Morgan Stanley's sensitivity analysis shows new levies that are passed on to consumers could reduce consolidated EBITDA by 1-2%. Jefferies says bank windfall taxes are not part of the ruling government's agenda. The report does not identify any consensus figure that explicitly embeds a windfall tax, an inflationindexation ban or a retail price cap, so these remain outside the base case.
2.
Cash returns and spectrum: OTE raised its dividend 21.7% to €0.90214 per share in Q2 2026. The payout is supported by free cash flow of €570 mm to €580 mm, which unfavorable regulation would deplete. Near-term spectrum costs look contained. EETT has proposed 20-year or indefinite-duration permits at reserve prices of €23.3 mm and €15.5 mm. The final auction rules are not yet settled.
3.
Greek sector precedents: The costly Greek interventions hit energy, not telecoms. HELLENiQ ENERGY paid a €222.4 mm solidarity contribution, which lifted its effective tax rate to 81% for 2024. Fuel-price discounts equated to roughly 5% of estimated EBITDA and 10% of net income. The government has historically limited such measures to sectors tied to cost-of-living crises, such as refiners and power utilities. That limits readacross to telecoms.

Political landscape to 2028: New Democracy polls around 30% and may fall short of an outright majority, but remains in pole position to form a stable coalition. Opposition platforms focus on general cost-of-living issues such as housing, energy prices and welfare, not telecom operators. The main exception is on bank profits. Western European precedents: Interventions here targeted unilateral pricing and billing practices. The European Court of Justice ruled in September 2026 that operators cannot raise prices unilaterally, forcing Vodafone Germany to reverse a €5 monthly increase. Italian courts nullified a €113 million concession-fee surcharge claim against Telecom Italia in late 2025. Magyar Telekom's EBITDA AL then rose 16.1% in 2025. Bulgaria's proposed windfall-profits tax for 2027 would apply to telecoms, banks and retail chains. Levies can therefore be reintroduced when budgets come under strain.
7.
Competitive and structural backdrop: OTE's wholesale framework and incumbency cushion regulatory shocks. EETT approved FTTH volume discounts of up to 28% in mid-2024, and Vodafone Greece and Nova signed up. Deutsche Bank Research estimates Nova's operating free cash flow at negative €33 mm in FY24, which limits its capacity for a prolonged price war. Even under a negative regulatory scenario, OTE's cash generation is expected to stay insulated relative to more leveraged rivals.
8.
Downside scenarios: The main vulnerabilities are a deeper-than-expected wholesale cut, an indexation ban and bidding inflation. A deeper forced wholesale cut would pressure OTE's high-margin wholesale revenues. A ban on inflation indexation would neutralize OTE's pricing power. Strict price controls could lead OTE and its rivals to scale back capital expenditure. That in turn would hurt fiber-supplier volumes and Greece's national fiber coverage targets.
9.
Limitations and next steps: The thresholds that would trigger EETT pricing action are not publicly disclosed. The telecom proposals of rising opposition parties remain vague. Final spectrum auction rules and bidding intensity are unsettled. Closing these gaps requires EETT draft consultation documents and expert interviews. Early-2027 party manifestos should also clarify the political picture. Until then, the findings are a qualitative assessment rather than a forecast.

Threatened dividend stability: Hellenic Telecommunications Organization (OTE) could face a severe constraint on its capital return capabilities if regulatory intervention caps operating profits or enforces special sector levies. The company recently reinforced its capital allocation with a substantial 21.7% dividend increase to €0.90214 per share in Q2 2026, supported by robust cash generation. Section 2 – Regulatory Interventions and Political Platforms in Greece

Section summary
  1. Greece's regulatory environment for telecommunications remains constructive as the government focuses on digital infrastructure deployment rather than imposing punitive price controls.
  2. The ruling New Democracy party maintains a stable policy outlook despite upcoming spring 2027 elections, reducing the likelihood of sudden sector windfall taxes.

Political Landscape and Election Risks

1.
Stable political leadership: Greece's ruling New Democracy party maintains a solid lead in recent opinion polls ahead of the scheduled spring 2027 general elections. Although the party currently polls around 30% and may fall short of an outright parliamentary majority, Prime Minister Mitsotakis's reform-minded administration remains in pole position to form a stable coalition. This political continuity suggests that abrupt changes in corporate tax policy or severe regulatory interventions are highly unlikely to disrupt the telecom sector.
2.
Tempered opposition threats: Opposition parties such as PASOK-KINAL and SYRIZA have focused their political platforms on general cost-of-living concerns rather than targeting specific telecom operators. PASOK leader Nikos Androulakis has unveiled election proposals centered on housing costs, energy prices, and welfare support. Because these political campaigns do not propose telecom-specific windfall levies, Hellenic Telecommunications Organization faces low risk of political expropriation from the left.
3.
Favorable fiscal reforms: The Greek government's proposed 2027 budget includes positive corporate measures such as a phased repeal of the entrepreneurship duty and accelerated depreciation for business investments. The Ministry of National Economy and Finance has focused its tax hikes on non-EU property transfers and executive profit-sharing over €60,000 rather than corporate revenues. These reformist measures will help strengthen OTE's post-tax cash flows and support capital expenditure.
4.
Favorable profit growth: Sectoral developments in Greece since 2022 indicate that information and communication remains one of the most dynamic growth drivers of the domestic economy. A regulatory study on market power showed that the sector recorded high annual growth rates that exceeded the wider market average. This robust economic recovery cycle has given telecom operators greater room to adjust pricing packages without attracting punitive state interventions.
5.
Contrary evidence: Greek political risks could escalate if New Democracy fails to secure an outright parliamentary majority in spring 2027, leading to a protracted period of political uncertainty. Morgan Stanley reported on November 15, 2026, that New Democracy's projected vote share of 30% was insufficient for single-party rule, which could usher in a highly fragmented coalition government. This political transition could pressure any new administration to introduce telecom consumer tariff caps or windfall levies.

Regulatory Framework and EETT Benchmarking

6.
Advanced pricing benchmarking: EETT has partnered with Tarifica to deploy a customized version of the Telecom Pricing Intelligence Platform. This platform enables the Greek regulator to compare Greece's mobile service landscape with five European peers, including Belgium, Poland, and Portugal. By utilizing data-driven insights, EETT can objectively assess domestic pricing against European benchmarks to guide its regulatory decisions.
7.
Constructive regulatory oversight: EETT's benchmarking framework explicitly prioritizes service quality and long-term network investments alongside retail prices. EETT President Konstantinos Masselos stated that the regulator evaluates pricing alongside sustainable infrastructure development to ensure accurate policymaking. This comprehensive approach indicates that the regulator is unlikely to impose arbitrary price caps that could damage OTE's network deployment incentives.
8.
Favorable wholesale agreements: EETT approved OTE's wholesale Fiber-to-the-Home pricing framework in mid-2024, introducing volume discounts of up to 28% for retail rivals. Competitors Vodafone Greece and Nova signed up to this system, committing to long-term volumes in exchange for lower wholesale rates. This regulatory approval helps OTE secure predictable wholesale revenue streams from its fixed-line infrastructure.
9.
Mitigated overbuild risk: OTE's approved fiber pricing framework has successfully minimized the threat of alternative network overbuild by competitors. Because rivals Vodafone and Nova have strong economic incentives to utilize OTE's wholesale network rather than building their own, capital duplication in Greece remains low. This regulatory compromise secures OTE's role as the dominant national digital infrastructure provider through 2028.

Comparative Pricing Power and Peer Friction

10.
Severe utility interventions: Public Power Corporation has faced much more aggressive state and regulatory interventions compared to the relatively protected telecom sector. During the 2022 energy crisis, the Greek government imposed strict revenue caps and retail tariff mechanisms on electricity suppliers, which was not replicated in telecom. These historical precedents demonstrate that utilities face heightened exposure to government interventions during macroeconomic crises.
11.
Unsuccessful wholesale entry: Public Power Corporation has struggled to establish itself as a viable wholesale fiber competitor to OTE. Rival operator Nova publicly rejected PPC's wholesale fiber pricing terms as unacceptable, while Vodafone has accelerated its own infrastructure deployment. Consequently, PPC's telco venture is unlikely to destroy OTE's wholesale value or force aggressive pricing cuts.
12.
Resilient commercial defense: OTE has successfully protected its broadband customer base against emerging discount competitors and satellite alternatives. The company launched Fixed Wireless Access in January 2025 to curb Starlink's momentum and prevent customer churn in rural areas. This proactive pricing strategy has allowed OTE to sustain stable broadband average revenues despite aggressive competitor discounts.
13.
Managed tariff interventions: EETT's past intervention in copper-based broadband tariffs showcases the regulator's balanced approach to pricing transitions. The regulator adjusted wholesale rates to make copper and cabinet connections less attractive, nudging users toward high-margin Fiber-to-the-Home connections. This policy did not hurt OTE's profits because the upsell to higher-priced fiber lines offset the regulatory copper adjustments.
14.
Comprehensive evidence scope: The regulatory and political assessment of Hellenic Telecommunications Organization is based on 32 relevant sources, comparing OTE's pricing power with Greek utility and telecommunications companies. The analysis examines multiple comparable past cases of EETT intervention and utility pricing caps to assess risk. This comprehensive evidence base provides a highconfidence baseline for OTE's outlook through 2028.
15.
Other side of the story: While EETT currently supports OTE's fiber pricing, future regulatory shifts could force aggressive wholesale tariff cuts if consumer adoption rates lag. If the Greek government demands rapid digital adoption but consumers find premium FTTH pricing too expensive, regulators may intervene by enforcing retail or wholesale price caps. This structural threat could cap OTE's long-term returns on its massive fiber network investment.

Section 3 – Greek Precedents in Other Sectors: Banks, Energy, and Utilities

Section summary
  1. The Greek government's historical interventions in refiners and utilities were driven by acute cost-of- living crises and do not represent a systematic threat to the telecom sector.
  2. Retroactive solidarity contributions and price caps have been strictly confined to highly exposed energy and banking operations, leaving defensive telecom cash flows insulated.

Refiner Solidarity Taxes and

1.
Subsidies Significant fiscal levies: The Greek government has repeatedly utilized targeted legislative interventions to extract temporary solidarity contributions from exceptionally profitable refining companies during periods of extreme commodity price volatility. Enacted in July 2024, Law 5122/2024 applied a temporary 33% solidarity contribution on incremental corporate profits from the 2023 fiscal year. This intervention demonstrates that the Greek state is willing to retroactively tax exceptional corporate gains to support national budget targets and fund consumer relief measures.
2.
Severe refiner impact: The solidarity tax structure created substantial cash flow and net income headwinds for the primary Greek fuel refiner HELLENiQ ENERGY during its payment cycle. HELLENiQ reported a temporary solidarity contribution of €222.4 mm, which translated to a net cash impact of €173.5 mm after corporate tax adjustments. This massive retroactive tax charge directly elevated HELLENiQ's effective tax rate to an extraordinary 81% for the 2024 fiscal year, limiting capital deployment.
3.
Comparable peer burden: Motor Oil Hellas experienced similarly severe retroactive fiscal charges under the same solidarity tax framework designed for Greek refining operations. The company's reported 2024 earnings after tax were heavily depressed by a net temporary solidarity contribution of €199 mm. This substantial fiscal cash outflow of €255 mm was paid in February 2025, which materially inflated Motor Oil's net debt to €2.1 bn during that operating period.
4.
Substantial subsidy costs: Morgan Stanley reported that the Greek government collaborated with domestic refiners to institute pump price subsidies as an alternative to direct windfall taxes. These voluntary fuel discounts of €0.10 per litre for gasoline and €0.05 for diesel cost each refining group €20 mm to €30 mm monthly. The annualized impact of these fuel price discounts corresponds to approximately 5% of estimated EBITDA and 10% of net income, mimicking a windfall tax.

Utility Revenue Caps and Agricultural Tariffs

5.
Constructed revenue caps: The Greek state intervened directly in wholesale electricity markets to capture generator revenues during the heights of the European energy crisis. Under Law 4971/2022, a temporary mechanism capped the wholesale compensation of power generation units at administratively determined regulated prices. This mechanism diverted all excess revenues above the cost-plus caps to fund the special Energy Transition Fund for subsidizing consumers.
6.
Suppressed utility profits: Public Power Corporation saw its generation profitability capped and was ultimately forced to absorb substantial retail supplier charges as a state-owned utility. The utility recognized an extraordinary supplier contribution of €200 mm under Joint Ministerial Decisions to help fund consumer bill relief. These interventions proved that the state would limit integrated utility returns to protect retail consumers during times of macroeconomic stress.
7.
Capped agricultural rates: The Greek government extended its utility price-mitigation measures to the agricultural sector through targeted, long-term electricity tariffs designed for farmers. Public Power Corporation launched the specialized "GAIA" electricity product to support active farming and livestock production. This tariff capped agricultural electricity rates at EUR 0.085 per kWh for the first two years of a ten-year contract term.

Bank Political Pressure and Opposition Proposals

8.
Intense political pressure: Greek banks have faced continuous political scrutiny regarding retail transaction fees and the wide spreads between deposit and lending rates. Prime Minister Mitsotakis successfully pushed banks to avoid charges for state bill payments and cap transfer fees at €0.50 for clients. While the ruling New Democracy party rejected formal windfall taxes, they extracted a one-off voluntary bank contribution of €100 mm in late 2024.
9.
Elevated bank costs: Greek financial institutions also absorbed increased regulatory expenses through mandatory hikes in state-managed deposit protection funds. For example, the Deposit Guarantee Fund increased its target level from 0.8% to 1.25% of covered deposits, resulting in a 0.15% semi-annual charge. This regulatory burden, combined with standard operating levies, represents an ongoing drag on banking efficiency rather than an outright cap on profits.
10.
Proposed banking levies: Opposition parties in Greece have actively campaigned for structured windfall taxes on bank profits to redistribute corporate gains. The centre-left PASOK party tabled legislative amendments proposing an extraordinary 8% tax on bank profits exceeding a €400 mm threshold. PASOK estimated this levy would raise €370 mm, indicating persistent political appetite for sector-specific taxation among opposition lawmakers.

Telecom Windfall Tax Read-Across Risk

11.
Contrary evidence: Greek banks and utilities have avoided further punitive windfall taxes as macroeconomic conditions and national fiscal balances have dramatically improved. Jefferies reported that Greece's strong fiscal position and persistent primary budget surpluses mean bank windfall taxes are not part of the political agenda for the ruling government. This suggests that the risk of windfall taxes expanding to other corporate sectors like telecommunications remains extremely low.
12.
Contained sector risk: The historical precedents in energy and banking suggest that Hellenic Telecom faces very low direct risk of windfall tax expansion. The government has historically restricted windfall taxes and revenue caps to sectors directly linked to essential cost-of-living crises, such as fuel refiners and power utilities. Because telecom service costs are not a primary driver of inflation, the sector is unlikely to attract punitive state interventions over the 2026 to 2028 period.
13.
Other side of the story: The Greek government's track record of retroactively imposing temporary solidarity taxes on refiners demonstrates that fiscal policy can pivot rapidly if economic shocks occur. If energy import dependencies trigger a renewed cost-of-living crisis, the state may be pressured to expand extraordinary corporate contributions to other sectors. Any sudden political shift during the upcoming 2027 elections could quickly elevate the fiscal threat level for OTE.

Section 4 – Regulatory and Pricing Interventions in Western Europe

Section summary
  1. Western European regulators and courts have consistently intervened against unilateral pricing and industry consolidation since 2015.
  2. Landmark court decisions and consumer-driven regulations have forced operators to refund billions and restructure their core tariff strategies.

German Fixed-Line Tariff Interventions

1.
Punitive judicial decisions: Vodafone Germany experienced a severe setback when the European Court of Justice ruled in September 2026 that telecom operators do not have an automatic right to increase prices unilaterally. This landmark ruling forced Vodafone to reverse a five-euro monthly price increase that had been applied to seventy percent of its broadband customer base since 2023. The forced reversal of this tariff hike is expected to reduce Vodafone's European cash flows by approximately one point five percent.
2.
Severe earnings pressure: Vodafone Germany faces a direct operating profit headwind from the retroactive price reversal that is expected to reduce EBITDAaL by one point five percent. The Federation of German Consumer Organisations mobilized over one hundred thousand broadband customers to join a massive class-action lawsuit in the Düsseldorf regional court. This judicial intervention demonstrates how consumer advocacy can severely depress incumbent operating margins even years after a tariff adjustment.
3.
Contrary evidence: German mobile services remain largely insulated from direct return-based regulatory interventions because cost-based wholesale obligations are rarely applied in Europe. This regulatory forbearance helps protect mobile average revenues per user even as fixed-line prices face heavy scrutiny. Consequently, the risk of reversing mobile tariff increases remains low across Germany.

United Kingdom Contract Restructuring

4.
Direct regulatory bans: Ofcom intervened aggressively in the United Kingdom by prohibiting telecom operators from linking mid-contract price rises to inflation indexes starting in January 2025. This regulatory ban targeted the traditional consumer-confusing pricing models of major operators like BT Group and Vodafone UK. The regulatory change forced operators to switch to flat monetary price increases written into contracts at the point of sale.
5.
Escalating political backlash: British operators face growing scrutiny as political figures and consumer advocates push back against these newly established flat-rate price increases. The Secretary of State for Science Innovation and Technology directed Ofcom in late 2025 to reevaluate mid-contract price hikes. This escalating political intervention could jeopardize future retail tariff growth and squeeze consumer margins if flat hikes are restricted.
6.
Costly financial penalties: BT Group was forced to refund and credit eighteen million pounds to customers following a ruling by Ofcom. The communications regulator determined that BT failed to provide clear contract information to over one million customers before they signed up. This enforcement action proves that regulatory compliance failures trigger material cash outflows and disrupt projected earnings.

Southern European Court Precedents

7.
Massive tax refunds: Spanish operators secured a major legal victory in early 2026 when the National Court ordered the state Treasury to refund over one hundred million euros. The Tax Agency had improperly collected the business activity tax based on mobile antenna numbers, which violated European Union law. This court ruling will return over forty-five million euros to Vodafone Spain's operating cash flows.
8.
Aggressive local taxation: Digi Spain successfully challenged improper regional taxes to secure refunds for business activity assessments paid between 2015 and 2018. Spanish courts ruled in February 2026 that local authorities had misapplied antenna-based tax levies to the low-cost carrier. This judicial precedent suggests that low-cost operators can recover damages from overreaching local municipal tax policies.
9.
Protracted billing disputes: Telecom Italia absorbed significant litigation costs and a three-million-euro fine following regulatory interventions against twenty-eight-day billing cycles. The Italian regulator AGCOM and civil courts ruled that billing customers every twenty-eight days instead of monthly was improper. This protracted legal battle shows that regulatory interventions in billing practices can drain administrative resources and damage corporate reputations.
10.
Favorable judicial reversals: Telecom Italia successfully overturned a massive historical dispute when Italian courts declared a hundred-and-thirteen-million-euro concession fee surcharge illegal. The Council of State and the Court of Cassation ruled in favor of the operator in late 2025, nullifying the state's claim. This victory indicates that courts can effectively shield operators from retroactive government fee grabs.

Benelux Tax and Merger Blocks

11.
Successful pylon tax abolition: Mobile operators in Belgium successfully negotiated the elimination of the Walloon regional pylon tax in late 2016. The regional government had taxed masts at eight thousand euros per site since 2014, creating significant margin pressure. In exchange for abolishing the tax, operators committed to invest sixty million euros in infrastructure over three years.
12.
Favorable judicial overrides: The highest Dutch administrative court overturned the regulator's symmetric joint dominance access rules in March 2020. 148 149 The regulator ACM had attempted to impose sweeping network access remedies on both KPN and VodafoneZiggo. This successful court challenge shows that judicial systems protect telecom operators from overreaching joint dominance regulations.
13.
Stringent merger blocks: The Dutch competition authority blocked KPN's proposed acquisition of a regional fiber network from Delta in June 2026. The regulator argued that the acquisition would harm consumer interests by reducing the number of local fiber network providers. This aggressive antitrust intervention highlights that regulators will prioritize consumer choices over corporate consolidation strategies.
14.
Constructive regulatory commitments: KPN managed to secure long-term pricing visibility by making voluntary wholesale fiber commitments to the Dutch regulator. The regulator made these wholesale local broadband access commitments legally binding until 2030, removing immediate regulatory threats. This proactive regulatory compromise demonstrates that operators can stabilize their pricing frameworks through longterm regulatory agreements. 154 155 of European Regulators for Electronic Communications.
15.
Other side of the story: European operators remain highly vulnerable to sudden corporate tax surcharges and local levies during periods of fiscal deficits. French telecoms and luxury sectors faced significant exposure to proposed temporary tax surcharges designed to address the national budget deficit in late 2026. If European states face intensifying fiscal pressure, they may retroactively increase telecom corporate taxes, severely diluting shareholder returns.

Section 5 – Regulatory and Pricing Interventions in Central and Eastern Europe

Section summary
  1. Central and Eastern European governments have historically imposed aggressive windfall taxes and price controls on telecom operators during periods of fiscal distress.
  2. Recent policy shifts indicate a significant regulatory easing, characterized by the phasing out of special levies in Hungary and fixed-line deregulation in Croatia.

Hungary's Windfall Taxes and Reversals

1.
Severe fiscal burdens: Magyar Telekom faced a progressive, revenue-based supplementary telecommunication tax of up to 7% introduced by the Hungarian government in June 2022 to support fiscal stabilization. This supplementary levy cost the company HUF 36.3 bn in 2024, which represented a substantial operating expense that directly depressed Magyar Telekom's cash flows during the high-inflation period. This intervention showcases the high financial impact of government-imposed revenue levies on corporate earnings.
2.
Favorable tax reversals: Magyar Telekom experienced significant margin relief when the Hungarian government phased out the supplementary telecom tax as of January 1, 2025, and abolished the public utility tax from January 1, 2024. These regulatory relief measures were established under a November 2023 strategic agreement with 4iG Group, which committed operators to accelerate national digital infrastructure investments up to 2030. This proves that CEE governments are willing to trade special levies for concrete network rollout commitments.
3.
Accelerated EBITDA growth: Magyar Telekom's parent entity, Deutsche Telekom, benefited directly from the elimination of these Hungarian sector-specific levies, which previously reduced the European segment's profitability. Analysts at Deutsche Bank Research reported in September 2023 that the complete tax phaseout would save the operator HUF 33 bn to HUF 35 bn annually, which significantly boosted Deutsche Telekom's European cash flows starting in 2025. This regulatory reversal demonstrates how structural tax rollbacks can rapidly expand corporate earnings and margin expectations.
4.
Robust profitability rebound: Magyar Telekom reported an outstanding 16.1% year-on-year increase in EBITDA AL to HUF 386.8 bn for the 2025 fiscal year, driven heavily by the tax removal. This fiscal relief, alongside an inflation-based fee adjustment clause in effect since mid-2022, allowed the company to substantially expand its adjusted net income to HUF 207.4 bn in 2025. This outcome demonstrates that removing punitive corporate levies can directly lead to higher earnings per share.

Regulatory Trajectories in Poland and Romania

5.
Favorable regulatory easing: Orange Polska received a major regulatory boost on January 20, 2026, when the Polish Office of Electronic Communications (UKE) lifted wholesale internet access regulations. This decision removed historical Bitstream Access and Local Loop Unbundling requirements, giving the operator greater pricing flexibility on fiber broadband across major urban areas. This indicates a clear regional shift toward reducing wholesale price interventions to incentivize private telecom infrastructure investments.
6.
Persistent consumer scrutiny: Orange Polska continues to navigate strict pricing oversight from the Polish competition and consumer protection authority UOKiK. The regulator concluded a probe on January 30, 2026, by imposing a PLN 34 mm financial penalty on the operator for improper prepaid inactivity fees.
