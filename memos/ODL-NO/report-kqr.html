Section 2 – Historical Precedents and Peak Earnings Multiples

Section summary
  1. Analyzing historical peak earnings multiples reveals how quickly valuation de-rating occurs when macroeconomic conditions deteriorate.
  2. Historical precedents indicate that even robust contract backlogs fail to defend premium multiples when oil prices fall and dividend distributions are suspended.

Peak Valuation Multiples

1.
Elevated valuations: Historical offshore drilling equities reached substantial peak multiples during the height of the previous upcycle. Morgan Stanley noted on 9 March 2015 that historical average price-to-earnings multiples achieved at the equity peaks of the previous two cycles ranged from 19 to 23 times forward earnings. These elevated peak earnings multiples reflected intense market momentum that typically overtook fundamental value as upward revisions accelerated.
2.
Premium asset pricing: Pure-play offshore drillers historically commanded high asset-based valuations during period peaks. Morgan Stanley reported on 1 February 2013 that pure-play drilling companies tended to trade at peak valuations of approximately two times net asset value during strong cycles. This asset-based premium was justified by high dayrates and tight fleet utilization across the global offshore drilling sector.
3.
Robust backlog support: Contracted earnings visibility historically supported premium multiples before cycles turned. Noble Corporation reported during its third-quarter 2014 earnings call that its premium fleet was protected by a contract backlog of 10.6 billion dollars. This substantial backlog provided strong near-term earnings visibility and delayed the immediate negative impact of declining dayrates on earnings per share.
4.
Contrary evidence: Market valuations began contracting sharply in late 2014 despite companies maintaining extensive contracted backlogs. Maybank reported on 20 November 2014 that offshore drillers de-rated by 19 to 31 percent from peaks as oil companies scaled back capital spending. This demonstrates that market sentiment and oil price declines can override robust contract backlog protection.

Dividend Yields and Capital Returns

5.
Generous distributions: Seadrill established a high benchmark for shareholder returns by distributing a significant portion of its peak cash flows. Norne Securities reported on 30 January 2013 that Seadrill maintained a dividend yield of roughly 10 percent annually since late 2009. This consistent payout strategy supported a premium valuation but heavily leveraged the corporate balance sheet.
6.
Stable asset premiums: High dividend payouts allowed yield-focused drillers to trade at substantial premiums to their asset values. DNB Carnegie highlighted on 11 September 2026 that Seadrill traded at an average price-to-net-asset-value of 1.4 times from late 2009 to early 2014. This premium valuation was directly linked to the company's ability to pay a stable and growing dividend over time.
7.
Structured yield vehicles: Specialized drilling yield companies attracted significant investor capital during the peak of the cycle. Morgan Stanley noted on 21 October 2014 that Seadrill Partners and Transocean Partners traded on a relative safety in offshore yield-cos with average yields between 6.5 percent and 7.8 percent. However, these structured vehicles rapidly lost their appeal as parent contract integrity concerns mounted.
8.
Precipitous policy shifts: Geopolitical challenges and oil price declines eventually forced rapid suspensions of capital returns. Morgan Stanley reported on 28 November 2014 that North Atlantic Drilling was forced into a sudden dividend suspension following the collapse in oil prices. This swift suspension triggered a massive de-rating because investors prioritized immediate cash distributions over paper backlog figures.

De-Rating Triggers and Collapsing Dayrates

9.
Severe contract erosion: The unwinding of peak cycles was accelerated by early contract terminations and rate renegotiations. Deutsche Bank reported on 18 March 2015 that oil companies successfully leveraged the weak market to weaken commercial terms in new contracts. Major operators like ENI renegotiated highspec drillship contracts down from 690,000 dollars per day to 450,000 dollars per day, which severely impaired operator earnings per share.
10.
Vulnerable fleet profiles: Older drilling units faced rapid displacement and cold-stacking as market demand deteriorated. Deutsche Bank highlighted on 25 March 2015 that nearly 60 offshore drilling units rolled off contracts by mid-2016. This massive wave of expiries forced operators to stack or scrap older assets rather than pay heavy survey costs.
11.
Heavy asset impairments: Falling asset values and contract expiries forced major write-downs that devastated reported earnings. Morgan Stanley reported on 6 February 2015 that Noble Corporation retired three mid-water semi-submersibles and took a non-cash impairment charge of 713 million dollars. This aggressive fleet trimming demonstrated that approaching survey years and weak dayrates quickly destroyed book value.
12.
Sudden liquidity crunches: High leverage and capital obligations created severe balance sheet pressures for aggressive operators during the downturn. Morgan Stanley highlighted on 22 June 2015 that Hercules Offshore filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy, which spooked the high leverage driller market and drove peer stocks to sell off heavily. This structural default proved that contracted backlogs could not save overleveraged companies when credit markets closed.

Client Spending and Asset Supply

13.
Gradual asset deflation: Shipyards experienced severe knock-on effects as offshore drilling demand dried up and delivery schedules slipped. Deutsche Bank reported on 4 March 2015 that major builders like Keppel saw five high-spec jackups for Transocean pushed out by 6 to 14 months. These delivery delays demonstrated that asset-heavy operators actively sought capital preservation over fleet growth as the peak of the cycle passed.
14.
Persistent demand challenges: Industry commentators highlighted that the downturn represented a prolonged structural shift rather than a quick correction. Maybank reported on 20 November 2014 that offshore drillers faced a perfect storm of low oil prices and rig oversupply. This dual pressure meant that forward earnings estimates required substantial downward revisions as multi-year contract expiries neared.
15.
Cautious operator spending: Oil companies prioritized financial discipline and short-cycle spending over long- term offshore exploration campaigns. DNB Carnegie reported on 18 August 2026 that major oil operators prioritized shareholder distribution over offshore industry spending after the 2014 market crash. This long-term change in client cash priorities has kept offshore drilling valuations below historical peak multiples.
16.
Declining leading dayrates: Market rates for premium floating rigs fell rapidly as uncontracted newbuilds flooded the market. Morgan Stanley reported on 9 March 2015 that leading-edge seventh-generation dayrates pegged at approximately 400,000 dollars daily during the downturn. This severe drop from peak rates of over 600,000 dollars daily caused a sharp contraction in operator free cash flow yields.
