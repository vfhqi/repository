Plausible problems to monitor

The most material cross-cutting weakness centers on cyclical earnings fragility driven by persistent external OEM build-rate dependency, evidenced by management's requirement to absorb 23% to 29% EPS forecast downgrades in late 2024 following Boeing strike action and Airbus tier-one delivery reductions. This operational vulnerability positions Senior materially worse than decentralized aerospace peer medians like Melrose Industries and Bodycote, which maintain greater end-market diversification and lower single-customer concentration risk.

A second structural weakness emerges through unilateral OEM contract power asymmetry, where former executives confirm Boeing retains contractual rights to redirect component work if Senior fails build-rate volume commitments. This contradicts management's public narrative emphasizing robust pricing leverage and customer partnership alignment, revealing material contract imbalance worse than peer supply chain norms where tier-one suppliers typically negotiate symmetrical volume protection clauses.

Non-GAAP reporting practice represents a third governance plausible problem to monitor, as management separately discloses site relocation costs, US class action expenses, and corporate undertaking fees to calculate adjusted operating profit, while simultaneously recognizing a net loss before tax of £42.2m in FY25 on the Aerostructures divestment and absorbing £35m in contingent corporate transaction costs during H1 2026. The frequency and magnitude of these adjustments exceed FTSE 250 industrial peer base rates, where recurring restructuring charges are typically embedded within reported operating margins.

Executive decision velocity exhibits partial agency constraints, requiring over nine years of CEO tenure to restore group margins to double-digit levels post-COVID and taking a full decade to complete the strategic pivot from a diversified aerospace conglomerate to a pure-play FCTM supplier. This extended transformation timeline compares unfavorably against activist-backed turnaround medians at peers like TI Fluid Systems or GKN (pre- Melrose acquisition), where portfolio rationalization was executed within three to five years.

Insider real-money conviction demonstrates a cross-cutting alignment gap, as CEO David Squires relies predominantly on LTIP equity vesting rather than direct open-market cash purchases, with all L12M insider buying activity driven by non-executive directors. This pattern diverges from top-quartile operator-CEO archetypes in UK mid-cap industrials, where chief executives typically deploy personal cash at material scale following transformational corporate actions or portfolio disposals.
