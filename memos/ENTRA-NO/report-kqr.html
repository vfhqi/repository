Section 1 – The Key Question and Why It Matters

Section summary
  1. The massive valuation gap between European public real estate and private market transactions directly impacts listed landlords' earnings, capital costs, and corporate survival over the next three years.

Defining the Key Question

1.
Research focus: "At what prices relative to book value have private, sovereign and pension buyers bought European office and mixed commercial portfolios, and stakes in listed landlords, in 2025 and 2026, and how does that compare with the 30% to 50% discounts at which listed office landlords trade? Which listed landlords do brokers and experts see as most likely to face a takeover bid, a block sale or a forced break-up in the next twelve months, and on what terms?"
2.
Persistent discounts: Listed European commercial landlords face a substantial public-private valuation gap that severely suppresses their market value relative to underlying assets. Deutsche Bank Research reported in October 2026 that Continental European REITs traded at a 33% discount to EPRA NTA, while UK counterparts traded at a 21% discount. This discount creates significant pressure on capital structures, making platform takeovers or forced asset break-ups the primary drivers of capital allocation decisions.
3.
Standardized metrics: European real estate companies utilize distinct EPRA net asset value metrics to reconcile statutory balance sheets with market realities. The EPRA Net Tangible Assets metric focuses on reflecting steady-state tangible assets by adjusting for deferred taxes, while Net Reinstatement Value assumes no asset sales. These valuation frameworks are critical because private buyers target entire platforms near book value, while public markets price shares at steep discounts to tangible assets.
4.
Refinancing pressures: Closely related questions focus on how upcoming debt maturities will reprice real estate capital structures over the next three years. Rothschild and Co Redburn reported in November 2026 that 54% of European corporate debt matures in 2027 and 2028. Higher refinancing rates pose a direct downside risk of approximately 4% to corporate earnings, making debt maturity profiles critical metrics for investors.
5.
Arbitrage opportunities: The massive public-to-private valuation gap has attracted private equity buyers looking to acquire entire portfolios at a discount. Morgan Stanley reported in January 2026 that private capital is actively arbitraging the listed discount, which makes public real estate highly vulnerable to takeovers. These platform acquisitions are often more attractive than buying individual assets because they allow buyers to bypass friction in the direct property market.

Implications for Corporate Survival

6.
Corporate restructuring: Activist campaigns and strategic disposals highlight the growing pressure on listed landlords to unlock value through structural reorganizations. Workspace Group faces demands from Saba Capital to pursue an accelerated asset sale program, despite trading at a 50% discount to Net Tangible Assets. For other players like Aroundtown, achieving target leverage requires completing disposals near book value to offset rising debt yields, illustrating how takeover bids dictate corporate survival.
7.
Swedish vulnerability: Nordic landlords are particularly exposed to interest rate volatility because of their shorter debt maturities and lower hedging ratios. Jefferies reported in October 2026 that Fabege maintains a debt duration of only 3.0 years with a 47% hedging ratio, leaving its earnings highly sensitive to debt repricing. Consequently, these companies face the greatest credit rating pressures unless they can execute aggressive asset disposal strategies successfully.
8.
Contrary evidence: Morgan Stanley reported in October 2026 that high bond yields and refinancing headwinds continue to suppress public valuations, leaving no immediate public catalyst to close the discount. Additionally, some investors remain willing to ignore the sector because alternative assets offer better earnings growth, indicating that valuation gaps may persist indefinitely.
9.
Other side of the story: Listed landlords might avoid takeover pressures if stabilising swap rates and organic rental growth allow them to refinance debt without dilutive equity raises. If occupancy rates and rental income recover toward historical averages, public markets could re-rate these stocks naturally, making forced platform sales unnecessary.
