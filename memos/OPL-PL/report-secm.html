1. The verdict

European Telecoms gives a stock bought now no Industry tailwind. The Telecoms tailwind is neutral today, after a headwind that for the median stock was made almost entirely in June 2026. The base expectation is that the Telecoms tailwind stays neutral over three and six months and is open beyond twelve months, because the question that took the multiples down has no operating evidence on either side yet. None of Richard's three questions gets a major positive answer, so Telecoms is the opposite case to Healthcare and Consumer staples: the median Telecoms stock is 8 points behind the index over the six months to 18 November 2026, fifteenth of 16 Industries.

Throughout, the index is the Stoxx Europe 600, the universe is the roughly 1,300 European stocks in our files, the multiple is the two-year-forward price-to-earnings multiple, and a percentile places a multiple in the stock's own ten-year range, where 0 is the cheapest. The Industry has one Sector, Telecoms - MNOs, with 33 stocks, of which 32 have prices. The median stock is used because two satellite operators, SES and Eutelsat, sit inside the Industry and distort averages.

Richard's three questions, answered for European Telecoms:

  • Fundamental improvement (question 1): present, weak, and not the cause of the lag. Deutsche Telekom, Orange, Vodafone, Telefonica and Telia each raised at least one 2026 target in the results season of October and November 2026, and no large operator cut profit or cash guidance (company releases and press reports, 22 October to 12 November 2026). Consensus 2027 earnings per share for the median Telecoms stock were unchanged over three months and (0.5)% over six. The improvement is in profit and cash and not in revenue, and the market did not pay for the improvement: Deutsche Telekom's shares fell after a third guidance raise. Durability: likely to hold through the 2027 guidance of February and March 2027; at risk beyond 2027 if either fear proves real
  • Money returning to an out-of-favour Industry (question 2): absent, and running in reverse. Telecoms was a well-owned winner: the median share rose 60% in the three years to May 2026 against 37% for the universe. The median multiple has fallen from the 59th percentile of its own range in May 2026 to the 35th. The 35th percentile is the middle of the pack and not the cheap end, so Telecoms is not yet an out-of-favour Industry to which money returns. Durability of the headwind: most of the fall in the multiple is done, and the fall can resume without operating proof
  • Defensive buying (question 3): absent when defensive buying was strongest. In October 2026 our Consumer staples basket gained 4 points against the index and our Telecoms basket lost 1. The money went to the defensive Industries that were cheapest and least owned, and Telecoms was neither; Telecoms also carried a structural question of its own. Telecoms still gains on days the index falls and loses more than before on days the index rises. Durability: a further move into defensive shares cannot be counted on to help Telecoms, and a reversal of defensive buying would not hurt Telecoms much

The three answers read together: Richard's test was that three strongly positive answers are the most supportive case and three negative answers the least. Telecoms gives one mildly positive answer that the market is ignoring, one negative answer that is mostly spent, and one answer of nil. The combination is a headwind that has stopped and a tailwind that has not started. The earnings are rising while the multiple falls, and the gap between the two is the only place from which a later tailwind could come.

How Telecoms differs from Healthcare and Consumer staples. Healthcare and Consumer staples entered the summer of 2026 cheap and unloved, at the 21st and 18th percentiles of their own ranges, and were bought when money left technology shares in October 2026. Telecoms entered the summer of 2026 dear and well owned, at the 59th percentile after a 60% rise in three years, and met a new structural fear in June 2026. The same market event therefore lifted two defensive Industries and passed over Telecoms. The median Utilities stock, at the 69th percentile and with no structural fear, was passed over too, so the starting valuation explains the missed rotation at least as much as the structural fear does. Weighted by size the largest utilities were bought in October 2026 and the largest telecoms groups were not (section 3.3).

3. The three sources, tested

The three sources give one weak positive, one negative that is mostly spent, and one nil. Fundamentals did not cause the lag. A well-owned winner was de-rated on a fear about profits several years out, and the defensive buying that lifted Healthcare and Consumer staples passed over Telecoms.

3.1 Fundamental improvement or deterioration

Verdict: fundamentals are supportive and weak, and are not the cause of the lag; confidence medium. Profit and cash improved modestly and broadly in the results season of October and November 2026. Revenue did not improve, estimates for 2027 did not rise, and the market did not pay for the improvement.

The share price fell while forward earnings rose. Over the six months to 18 November 2026 the median Telecoms share price fell 5%, forward earnings rose 5% and the multiple fell 9%. For the universe over the same six months the price rose 3%, forward earnings rose 7% and the multiple fell 3%. Of the gap of about 8 percentage points between the median Telecoms share and the median share in the universe, forward earnings account for about 1 point and the multiple for about 5 (medians do not sum exactly). Over twelve months the median Telecoms share price rose 16%, forward earnings rose 13% and the multiple rose 1%: across the full year the shares simply followed earnings, and the multiple made a round trip.

The rise in forward earnings is growth, not upgrades. Forward earnings rise as time passes, because the two-year-forward measure rolls onto later and larger forecast years. Analysts' estimates for a fixed year did not rise:

  • Consensus 2027 earnings per share for the median stock: unchanged over one month, unchanged over three, (0.5)% over six and 1% over twelve. Telecoms ranks ninth of 16 Industries on the three-month revision and twelfth on the six-month revision
  • Consensus 2027 sales for the median stock: unchanged over three, six and twelve months
  • Breadth of revisions over three months: 13 stocks upgraded against 15 downgraded, of 31
  • Analysts' changes in the last 45 days: 83 estimates raised against 103 cut, a net (8)% of estimates and thirteenth of 16 Industries. Healthcare is at 16% and Utilities at 13%

The results season said more than the estimates did. Public sources and the brokers agree that the season was good (our public-source report of 18 November 2026; AlphaSense digest of 18 November 2026):

  • Raised: Deutsche Telekom raised 2026 guidance for the third time in 2026, lifting its free cash flow target by about €200 million or 1%, to about €20.1 billion, with its profit target unchanged (Morgan Stanley, 5 November 2026, via AlphaSense); Deutsche Telekom also proposed a dividend up 10% and planned a buyback of up to €2 billion for 2027. Orange (27 October 2026), Telia (22 October 2026), Vodafone (10 November 2026) and Telefonica (12 November 2026) each raised profit or cash targets. Tele2 missed on profit in Sweden, then narrowed its profit growth outlook to 4% to 5%, the top of its earlier range of low-to-mid single digits, and cut its capital spending target to below 10% of sales (company report, 21 October 2026). Press reports add Proximus and Bouygues (Communications Today, citing Reuters, 6 November 2026)
  • Held: BT, Telecom Italia, Hellenic Telecom and, on headlines only, KPN. Swisscom confirmed its revenue and profit targets and raised its operating free cash flow target to more than 2.0 billion Swiss francs (company report, 5 November 2026)
  • Cut: no large operator cut profit or cash guidance. Telefonica cut its revenue growth target to a range of 1% to 1.5%
  • The brokers' summary: Morgan Stanley titled its round-up of 13 November 2026 "Solid fundamentals, subdued sentiment", and DNB Carnegie wrote on 29 October 2026 that investors had penalised the Industry over "newly perceived technological threats" and not over operations (both via AlphaSense)

The improvement is profit growth without revenue growth. Domestic revenue is barely growing: Vodafone Germany's service revenue was flat, between (0.5)% and 0.4% depending on the period (ING, 10 November 2026, via AlphaSense; company release, 10 November 2026), and Vodafone wrote down its German business by €4.85 billion in the same release (public sources); Vodafone United Kingdom's grew 0.9%; Orange France's total revenue grew 0.5%; and BT's service revenue fell 1% (company releases, 27 October to 10 November 2026). Barclays' own chart shows sector revenue growth slightly negative since the second quarter of 2025, before either fear began (our Sector Memo of 3 November 2026). The gains come from cost programmes and from capital spending that is flat to falling. Consensus for the 22 stocks with a forecast block says the same: sales growth of 2% a year, and an operating margin rising from 15% to 17% to 18%.

The gain from lower capital spending lands in 2027 and fades from 2028. For 2027 the cash growth is visible. BT's capital spending falls by more than £1 billion in the year to March 2027, to £4.2 billion to £4.3 billion, and BT guides normalised free cash flow from £1.5 billion to about £2 billion in the same year; BT's first-half fall of 59% in free cash flow was working-capital timing, and BT reconfirmed the full-year figure (BT, 5 November 2026, via AlphaSense). KPN's capital spending falls by about €250 million in 2027 (KPN, 5 November 2025, via AlphaSense). Cost savings and lower capital spending run out from 2028; revenue growth does not. The limit on the fundamental source is therefore a judgement about 2028 and after, and is built into the kind of improvement Telecoms is showing.

Good results met flat or falling share prices. Deutsche Telekom's shares fell after the third guidance raise of 2026, with traders citing capital returns and a possible transaction involving T-Mobile US. Deutsche Telekom closed (2.1)% on 5 November 2026 against (0.4)% for the index, Vodafone (1.0)% on 10 November 2026 against 0.3%, and Telefonica (0.7)% on 12 November 2026 against 0.1% (our price files). Swisscom's shares rose 2% (Communications Today, 6 November 2026). The market is not paying for upgrades at the level of the Industry. Inside the Industry the market did punish weak estimates, by a gap of 18 points over six months between the most upgraded and the most downgraded third of stocks. Across the whole universe the same gap was 19 points, 10.9% against (8.2)%, so the gap is the market's general pattern and not an Industry signal. In Telecoms an upgrade bought protection from the fall and did not buy a gain.

The threat that moved the shares is to the value of profits several years out. Barclays' sector note of 20 October 2026 wrote the two fears into lower long-run growth, cut its price targets by about 10% on average and changed no earnings per share estimates for the thesis (our Sector Memo of 3 November 2026). Barclays expects the damage to arrive slowly, at about 1% of group revenue a year for five years. A fall in the multiple with unchanged estimates is exactly what such a view produces. Fundamentals in 2026 and 2027 cannot answer a fear about 2029 and after; only the absence of damage, quarter after quarter, can.

Durability: the fundamental source is likely to hold at its present slow pace through the full-year results and the 2027 guidance of February and March 2027, because the cost programmes are announced and the fall in capital spending is guided for 2027. Four cautions apply.

  • Earnings growth slows: Deutsche Bank's strategists were underweight Telecoms on 18 November 2026 because they expect earnings growth to decline rapidly in 2027 (via AlphaSense), and consensus has earnings per share growth for the median stock falling from 14% in the first unreported year to 8% in the year after
  • The profit gain of 2026 is real and partly temporary: Deutsche Bank's strategists wrote that energy-cost hedges helped 2026 and start to bite on costs as the hedges expire (6 November 2026, via AlphaSense). Morgan Stanley records operating cash profit growth of 4.7% in the first nine months of 2026 against 3.5% in 2025, with mobile service revenue growth near zero (16 November 2026, via AlphaSense)
  • Shareholder returns support the income of mid-sized operators and not the Industry's multiple: buybacks continue at KPN, Telenor and Tele2 and shrink at Deutsche Telekom and Vodafone in 2027. Deutsche Telekom's buyback falls from €3 billion to €3.5 billion in 2026 to up to €2 billion in 2027 (Deutsche Bank, 5 November 2026, via AlphaSense); Vodafone's buyback has ended; BT, Telefonica and Orange run none beyond employee share plans. Deutsche Bank's 2027 dividend yields are 7.7% for Telenor, 7.2% for Tele2, 6.3% for KPN, 5.2% for Telia and 4.5% for Swisscom (15 October 2026, via AlphaSense)
  • Price rises are less automatic than in 2023 to 2025 (section 4, rating 5)

3.2 A crowded winner de-rated: the out-of-favour source in reverse

Verdict: the out-of-favour source is against, and now weak; confidence medium. Telecoms was the opposite of an out-of-favour Industry in May 2026. The de-rating has taken the median multiple to the middle of its range, which is neither dear enough to fall much further on valuation alone nor cheap enough to draw money back.

Telecoms was a well-owned winner in May 2026.

  • The run: the median Telecoms share rose 60% in the three years to May 2026 against 37% for the universe, and 23% in the year to May 2026 against 12%. Barclays puts the sector about 40% ahead of the market from January 2024 to mid-May 2026, after about ten years behind the market (our Sector Memo of 3 November 2026)
  • At the peak: Rothschild & Co Redburn recorded a new three-year high for Telecoms against the Stoxx Europe 600 (Rothschild & Co Redburn, 13 May 2026, via AlphaSense), and Bernstein wrote that the sector's relative recovery of about 20% since early 2024 came from a higher multiple and not from earnings (Bernstein, 24 September 2026, via AlphaSense). A run made by the multiple is the kind most exposed to a new fear
  • The multiple: the median percentile rose from 44 in November 2025 to 59 in May 2026. The universe was at 45 in May 2026, Healthcare at 21 and Consumer staples at 18
  • Ownership: no source measures European long-only ownership of Telecoms. Deutsche Bank's weekly data show net outflows of $1.1 billion from dedicated global telecom funds in its report of 6 November 2026, the largest of any sector, after inflows of $0.8 billion in its report of 2 October 2026 (via AlphaSense). The Deutsche Bank figures cover global funds and say nothing about June 2026. The evidence that Telecoms was crowded still rests on the price record, the percentile and single remarks, such as Bernstein's on 21 May 2026 that much good news was already in BT's price after a rise of 31% in six months (via AlphaSense)

History said a strong run in Telecoms is given back, and the strong run was given back. After each of the six strong six-month runs in ten years, the median Telecoms stock lagged over the following three months, and in five of the six over the following six months. The six months to March 2026, at 11 points ahead of the median stock in the universe, were followed by a lag of 10 points over three months and 13 over six, the largest reversal of the six. June 2026 therefore had two parts: a new fear, and an Industry whose record after strong runs was already poor.

The de-rating, measured. The median percentile fell from 59 in May 2026 to 35 on 18 November 2026, below the universe at 39. The dearest third of Telecoms stocks in May 2026 fell 8% over six months and the cheapest third fell 1%, so the stocks that started dear lost most. The fall in the multiple was made early: over the three months to 18 November 2026 the median Telecoms multiple fell 4%, almost the same as the universe's 3%. Since mid-August 2026 Telecoms has been de-rated no faster than the market.

About a third of the de-rating is specific to Telecoms (our arithmetic; no broker splits the de-rating this way). Utilities was also a well-owned winner in May 2026, at the 69th percentile after a rise of 44% in three years, and carries no fear about satellites or artificial intelligence agents. Over the six months to 18 November 2026 the median multiple fell (8.5)% for Telecoms, (5.4)% for Utilities and (3.2)% for the universe. About 3 points of the Telecoms de-rating are therefore market-wide, about 2 points are the ordinary de-rating of a dear and well-owned defensive Industry, and about 3 points are specific to Telecoms, the share the fears can explain. Part of the brokers' target cuts was also interest rates and not the fears: Bernstein raised its market risk premium to 6.0% (19 October 2026, via AlphaSense), and Morgan Stanley added about 0.5 points to tower companies' cost of capital (8 October 2026, via AlphaSense).

Has the de-rating gone far enough to become a source of returning money? Not yet, for three reasons.

  • The median is in the middle and not at the cheap end. A move back to the middle of the ten-year range is worth 7% for the median stock with earnings unchanged, and a move to the cheap end costs 17%. On the multiple alone the reward is smaller than the risk. Healthcare and Consumer staples drew money back from the 21st and 18th percentiles, not from the 35th
  • Broker valuation measures point different ways because the measures compare different things. Morgan Stanley finds the sector's enterprise value to operating cash profit about 50% below the market's, against a ten-year average discount of 40% (23 October 2026, via AlphaSense). Deutsche Bank found the sector's price-to-earnings multiple 9% above the sector's own ten-year average (17 September 2026, via AlphaSense). Both brokers weight by size and use one-year multiples; our median stock is at the 35th percentile of its own ten-year range. Telecoms looks cheapest against a market that has re-rated, and not against Telecoms' own history
  • The median hides two opposite groups. Deutsche Telekom (3rd percentile), Elisa (3rd), Gamma Communications (4th), Sunrise (4th) and Telefonica (7th) are near the bottom of their own ranges. Swisscom (100th), BT (89th), Digi Communications (87th), Telia (86th), 1&1 (86th), Telecom Italia (78th) and Orange (77th) are near the top. Fourteen of 27 stocks are below the 35th percentile of their own range. The Industry is therefore not uniformly cheap or dear: the valuation room sits in particular stocks, and for the largest of them, Deutsche Telekom, in the stock that carries the American exposure
  • History gives no reliable rebound after weak runs. After the five earlier six-month runs as weak as the run to September 2026, the median relative return of Telecoms was (3) points over the following three months, (2) over six, (1) over twelve and 2 over eighteen. Only the weak run that ended in April 2024 was followed by a clear recovery

How much of the fear is in the price (our arithmetic, and rough). The median multiple has fallen 9%, of which about 3 points are specific to Telecoms, against Barclays' own average target cut of 10%. The whole fall in the multiple prices roughly what one broker has written in, and the share specific to Telecoms prices less. Barclays' illustration shows how much more a harsher view would cost: a typical operator is worth about 7.2 times operating cash profit (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation) with revenue growing 1% a year, about 6.0 times with 0.5% and about 5.0 times with none (our Sector Memo of 3 November 2026). Half a point of lost growth is worth about 17% of the value of the whole business and no growth about 30%, and more for the shares where debt is high. The fall can therefore go much further if evidence appears, and can reverse if evidence does not.

Durability: the headwind from de-rating is mostly spent for the median stock and can resume without operating proof, as the 14% fall to 2 November 2026 came without proof (our Sector Memo of 3 November 2026). More brokers cutting long-run growth, a satellite wholesale agreement in Europe, or higher bond yields would each be enough to restart the fall.

3.4 How the three combine, and what each would have to do for the Industry to become a tailwind

The three sources combine into a standstill. Earnings push up slowly, the multiple has stopped falling faster than the market's, and defensive money does not arrive.

The pattern that ties the three together is earnings rising while the multiple falls. In Healthcare and Consumer staples the multiple rose faster than earnings, so the Relative Strength there ran ahead of the fundamentals. In Telecoms the fundamentals ran ahead of the shares. A gap of that kind closes in one of two ways: the multiple recovers, or the earnings give way. Which of the two happens is the whole question. The evidence on artificial intelligence agents arrives from 28 January 2027, and the decisive evidence on satellite spectrum may not arrive until late 2027.

Appendix B. The two fears, and the evidence by November 2026

1. The judgement

Both fears are priced and neither is yet observed in a European operator's numbers. The fear about satellites has concrete events behind it in the United States and a spectrum rule in front of it in Europe. The fear about artificial intelligence agents has a mechanism, sizes given by two brokers on different measures, and no data. The multiple has paid for both fears: the median stock's percentile fell from 59 in May 2026 to 35 on 18 November 2026.

The two fears differ in how each can be settled. The satellite question in Europe turns on a decision, the award of the 2 GHz band, whose date has become less firm. The current licences expire in May 2027 and the European Commission aims to settle its selection procedure by then (Telefonica, 29 July 2026, via AlphaSense), but Deutsche Telekom (5 November 2026) and Morgan Stanley (27 October 2026) date the bidding itself to late 2027, and Bernstein expects reallocation after 2027 (all via AlphaSense). The satellite test may therefore move from the six-month horizon to the twelve-month horizon. The question about artificial intelligence agents has no decision date; it can only be worn down by quarters of stable customer losses and prices, the first of which is reported from 28 January 2027. A fear that needs an absence of evidence to clear will clear slowly, which is why the Industry verdict stays neutral at three and six months.

2. Satellites: two services, two different risks

Satellite broadband to the home competes with fixed lines in places where fibre has not arrived. Direct-to-device satellite services connect an ordinary phone to a satellite where no mast reaches. The June 2026 fall was about direct-to-device satellite services; the sized estimate from Barclays is about satellite broadband to the home.

The fear, and who holds it.

  • SpaceX's steps in the United States are large and fast: about $17 billion of spectrum bought from EchoStar in 2025 and about $8 billion of low-band spectrum on 9 October 2026, after which SpaceX said Starlink would become a major mobile operator in the United States (Demócrata and Il Sole 24 Ore, 9 October 2026). T-Mobile US, Verizon and AT&T fell between (11)% and (12)% on 9 October 2026
  • United States carriers treat the threat as real: the three carriers formed a joint venture on direct-to-device coverage (TelecomTV, 12 June 2026)
  • Morgan Stanley is the broker voice of the fear: direct-to-device capacity raises competition in mobile and broadband, and T-Mobile US lagged its market by 60 points over 18 months on the worry (Morgan Stanley, 4 September 2026, via AlphaSense). Evercore ISI said on 9 October 2026 that operators' defences are weakening
  • Deutsche Telekom carries the fear into Europe by ownership, not by geography: the exposure runs through T-Mobile US

The limits in Europe.

  • Spectrum: without European Union spectrum of its own, Starlink can reach European phones only through operator partners (public-source report of 18 November 2026). The current 2 GHz mobile satellite licences expire in May 2027. The European Commission's proposal of 27 May 2026 reserves two of six blocks for a governmental system under European control and caps any one commercial applicant (our Sector note of 15 October 2026). Deutsche Telekom, Orange, Vodafone and Telefonica signed a letter of intent on a joint bid (Orange release, 19 October 2026, via AlphaSense). The European Parliament and the Council must still approve the proposal
  • Physics and density: Barclays treats direct-to-device service as a complement and does not model direct-to-device service, because about 80% of mobile use is indoors and capacity from space is three to five times less efficient (our Sector Memo of 3 November 2026). Bernstein (24 September 2026) calls satellite broadband a niche that saturates at about seven households per square mile
  • Take-up: satellites serve 0.2% to 0.5% of households in the large European markets against about 2.3% in the United States (Barclays, in our Sector Memo of 3 November 2026). A Bloomberg Intelligence survey reported by Deutsche Bank on 17 November 2026 found under 5% of Europeans would consider satellite broadband and 68% prefer fibre
  • Price of the service in 2026: Virgin Media O2 sells direct-to-device coverage as an add-on at £3 a month (TelecomTV, 12 June 2026). No subscriber number has been published for any European direct-to-device service

The evidence that cuts the other way. Starlink had more than 13 million subscribers worldwide by mid-August 2026 against 8.9 million at the end of 2025, and ran price promotions in four large European markets in 2026 (Barclays, in our Sector Memo of 3 November 2026). Starlink cut its British residential price to £32 a month (Deutsche Bank, 6 November 2026). Hellenic Telecom lost customers on its poorest copper lines to Starlink until Hellenic Telecom's fixed wireless service arrived in January 2025 (our Sector Memo of 3 November 2026). Starlink's growth is real; Starlink's base in Europe is small.

3. Artificial intelligence agents: a mechanism without a measurement

The fear. Agents such as Meta's Muse can compare contracts and switch or renegotiate for the customer. Operators charge long-standing customers more than new ones and price main brands far above value brands, so an agent that removes the effort of switching attacks the most profitable customers first. Barclays scores the risk from agents highest in Belgium and Germany and lowest in France and Italy (our Sector Memo of 3 November 2026).

The sized cases: two brokers, on measures that are not directly comparable. No further broker followed Barclays in cutting long-run growth, but a second broker sized the fear about agents. Barclays' note of 20 October 2026 (from our Sector Memo of 3 November 2026) measures revenue and operating cash profit:

  • Agents: about 5% of revenue from private customers lost over about five years, on an assumption that 5% to 10% of customers a year use agents and save 10% to 20% each
  • Satellite broadband: about 2.5% of revenue from private customers over the same period
  • At company level: 4% to 7% of revenue and 8% to 13% of operating cash profit over five years, and up to about 30% of equity value for the most indebted operators
  • Into valuations, not forecasts: long-run growth assumptions cut by 0.25 to 0.75 percentage points, price targets cut by about 10% on average, and no earnings per share estimate changed for the thesis. Barclays' own caveat is that it is "far too early" for detailed estimates

Morgan Stanley's measure is cash flow to shareholders (23 October 2026, via AlphaSense). Morgan Stanley estimates that a 15% price cut for customers paying out-of-contract prices would remove about 15% of the sector's cash flow after tax, and puts 10% to 20% of free cash flow at risk in its worst case. Morgan Stanley lowered its Industry view from Attractive to In-Line on the same note. In Morgan Stanley's survey with Accenture only 13% of consumers would let an agent buy for them without approval.

Why a small, slow loss moves prices so far. Barclays' illustration values a typical operator at about 7.2 times operating cash profit with revenue growing 1% a year, about 6.0 times at 0.5% and about 5.0 times with no growth. Revenue growth for the Sector has been slightly negative since the second quarter of 2025, before either fear began. The two fears therefore landed on an Industry whose valuation already rested on a thin growth assumption. The pattern in Appendix C follows from that arithmetic: the largest and most indebted groups have recovered least.

Who disputes the fear. Deutsche Bank upgraded Telia on the argument that the market overprices the risk from agents (dated 15 October 2026 in our AlphaSense digest and 20 October 2026 by Börskollen). Orange's chief executive expects no severe tariff erosion (Morgan Stanley, 27 October 2026), and Telecom Italia's management calls the downside very low (Deutsche Bank, 12 November 2026). Morgan Stanley itself places the risk lower where mobile bills are already low, naming Italy and Greece (23 October 2026).

Appendix D. Fundamentals and the Sector's Key Drivers on 18 November 2026

1. The judgement

Fundamentals are mildly positive and are not the cause of the weakness; the improvement is in profit and cash, not in revenue. Operators raised targets through the results season of October and November 2026, and consensus forecasts barely moved. The gains come from costs, merger savings and falling capital spending. For 2027 the cash growth is visible in company guidance; the judgement that these gains run out applies to 2028 and after. Home-market revenue is barely growing at several of the largest operators: flat at Vodafone in Germany, 0.9% at Vodafone in the United Kingdom, 0.5% at Orange in France and (1)% at BT.

The drivers that our Sector note of 15 October 2026 identified are intact, and each driver belongs to named companies, not to the Industry. No driver is strong or broad enough to lift every operator, which is one reason the Industry gives a stock no tailwind even while most operators report good numbers.

3. What consensus says, and why raised guidance has not produced upgrades

  • Forecasts for the 22 stocks with a forecast block: sales growth of 2% a year; operating margin rising from 15% to 17% to 18%; earnings per share growth of 14% next year and 8% the year after for the median stock
  • Revisions to 2027 earnings per share for the median stock: 0% over one month, 0% over three, (0.5)% over six, 1% over twelve; 13 stocks upgraded and 15 downgraded, of 31, over three months. Our Sector Memo of 3 November 2026 reported 2.5% over six months on a different basis (Appendix F)
  • Analysts are cutting more than raising: 83 estimates raised against 103 cut in the 45 days to 17 November 2026, a net (8)% that ranks thirteenth of 16 Industries

Companies raised guidance while analysts cut estimates. The two facts are compatible. Guidance is given for operating profit and free cash flow; the cuts fall on earnings per share, which also carries write-downs, interest costs and currency. Vodafone raised profit guidance and wrote down Germany by €4.85 billion in the same release. Deutsche Bank (6 November 2026) says higher interest costs take 30% of Telefonica's free cash flow to shareholders. The consequence for the tailwind is that the Industry's raised guidance does not show up as the earnings upgrades that the market has rewarded in Healthcare.

The deceleration is in consensus already. Earnings per share growth for the median stock falls from 14% to 8% between the next financial year and the one after. Deutsche Bank's strategists said on 18 November 2026 that they remain underweight Telecoms because earnings growth is expected to fall quickly in 2027 (via AlphaSense). A buyer of the Industry on fundamentals is buying growth that the forecasts themselves show fading.

4. The Key Drivers, driver by driver

Savings from mergers that have closed: intact, and the most dependable driver. Vodafone's British savings target stands at £1.0 billion by FY2032, and Swisscom's Italian savings are ahead of schedule (company comment, 5 November 2026). No regulator can now block savings from a merger that has closed. The driver belongs to Vodafone, Swisscom, Orange in Spain and Zegona Communications.

Capital spending past its peak: intact; the gain lands in 2027 and fades from 2028. BT's capital spending falls by more than £1 billion in the year to March 2027, to between £4.2 billion and £4.3 billion, and BT guides normalised free cash flow from £1.5 billion to about £2 billion in the same year (BT, 5 November 2026, via AlphaSense). BT's first-half free cash flow fall of (59)% was working-capital timing, and BT reconfirmed the full-year figure. KPN's capital spending falls by about €250 million in 2027 (KPN, 5 November 2025, via AlphaSense). Deutsche Telekom's capital spending fell (3)% over nine months. Orange is the exception: Orange's capital spending rose in the third quarter of 2026, to €1.71 billion from €1.47 billion (Orange, 27 October 2026, via AlphaSense). A step down in capital spending does not repeat, so the cash growth of 2027 is visible and the cash growth of 2028 and after is not.

New owners and cost programmes: intact, dated later. Vodafone guided to a net cost reduction in Europe of more than €200 million in FY2027 as a first step towards more than €1 billion, and Telia's 900 job cuts deliver in full from 2028. Board influence for Vodafone's new shareholder may wait until the summer of 2027 (Morgan Stanley, 2 September 2026). Savings are announced gross, and Morgan Stanley's caution on Deutsche Telekom's programme is that gross is not net.

Price rises written into contracts: weakening, and diverging by country, not breaking. The pricing driver has changed most since 2023 to 2025:

  • The size of each rise follows inflation down: Telekom Austria applied 3% in April 2026 against 8% in April 2024
  • The law has narrowed: the Court of Justice of the European Union ruled on 10 September 2026 against price rises imposed at an operator's discretion, and Vodafone Germany is reversing a rise of €5 a month for 2.5 million customers
  • The United Kingdom has banned inflation-linked rises; fixed rises in pounds and pence take effect in April 2027
  • Hellenic Telecom's management has not committed to its February 2027 rise
  • Price rises continue in France (Orange's targeted rises), in the Netherlands (KPN raised broadband prices 3.3% from 1 July 2026) and at the German incumbent (Deutsche Telekom's mobile revenue per account grew 4.2% in the third quarter of 2026)
  • Prices are weakening in Finland, where Elisa's mobile service revenue fell (2.3)% in the third quarter of 2026 after a new entrant (Bernstein, 21 October 2026, via AlphaSense); at the low end in Spain, where four-play bundle prices fell (6.5)% (Morgan Stanley, 17 September 2026, via AlphaSense); and below the incumbent in Germany, where Telefonica Germany's new-customer mobile prices are 40% lower than three years ago (Deutsche Bank, 6 November 2026, via AlphaSense)
  • Customers are already trading down without agents: 39% of Swisscom's private mobile customers have moved to cheaper brands (Morgan Stanley, 5 November 2026, via AlphaSense). Trading down is the behaviour agents would speed up, and no operator attributes the trading down to agents

Pricing was the driver that made Telecoms a winner in the three years to May 2026. The fear about artificial intelligence agents is, at root, a fear about the same thing: whether operators can keep charging existing customers more each year. A weakening price driver and an unproven fear about switching therefore reinforce each other in the multiple, even though only the weakening price driver is observed.

The satellite worry: covered in Appendix B. Our Sector note of 15 October 2026 expected the satellite worry to clear for operators that sell only in Europe once the 2 GHz decision is adopted; the bidding for the 2 GHz band is now dated to late 2027.

5. What would change the verdict on fundamentals

  • Towards supportive: 2027 guidance in February and March 2027 that holds profit growth at 2026 rates, with revenue in Germany and the United Kingdom turning from flat to growing
  • Towards against: 2027 guidance that implies slower profit growth or flat free cash flow; a price war started by Digi in Spain or by fibre rivals in the United Kingdom; new capital spending on satellite or artificial intelligence networks

Appendix E. Consolidation and regulation, with dates

1. The judgement

Policy is moving the operators' way and almost none of the structural help lands inside twelve months. Inside twelve months sit the first steps of one decision that matters for the satellite fear (the 2 GHz band: the current licences expire in May 2027 and the bidding is dated to late 2027) and several smaller rulings. The changes that would alter the structure of a market (three networks in France, a European law on spectrum and networks, mobile mergers in Germany or Sweden) sit in late 2027 or 2028, or have no date. Consolidation was the story that carried Telecoms shares from 2023 to 2025; consolidation now pays only where a merger has already closed.

  • British regulators are blocking as well as clearing: the Competition and Markets Authority provisionally blocked nexfibre's purchase of Netomnia on 2 October 2026 and is reviewing BT's purchase of TalkTalk, where Deutsche Bank sees a high chance that the benefits are unwound
  • The Digital Networks Act could arrive weaker than proposed: the draft supports longer spectrum licences, and the parliamentary rapporteur doubts indefinite ones (EU Perspectives, 2 November 2026). The pattern. The dates have kept slipping during 2026 (public-source report of 18 November 2026), and the help that falls inside twelve months is mostly defensive: the 2 GHz selection protects operators from a new rival, does not raise their profits, and may itself slip to late 2027. The profit-raising changes are at or beyond the edge of the twelve-month horizon. The Industry verdict is therefore neutral at twelve months and open at eighteen: policy cannot be counted as a tailwind for a stock bought now, and operators that already hold a closed merger (Appendix D) are the only ones for whom consolidation is in the numbers.

Appendix F. Method, sources and limits

1. How this memo relates to our Sector Memo of 3 November 2026

Our Sector Memo of 3 November 2026 asked whether the fall in Relative Strength was a pullback or structural, and concluded that the uptrend was broken and that the impairment was structural in one broker's valuation and unproven in operations. This memo asks a different question: how much help or hindrance the Industry gives a stock bought now, from each of three sources, at four horizons. This memo carries the Sector Memo's conclusion forward, updates the Sector Memo's price readings from 2 November 2026 to 18 November 2026, and takes Barclays' note of 20 October 2026 from the Sector Memo. Three differences of method produce differences in numbers:

  • The daily cap: baskets here cap each daily move at 25%; the Sector Memo capped at 50%. Lows differ by about 1%
  • The comparison: the Sector Memo measured from the peak of 19 May 2026; this memo uses windows of 21, 63, 126 and 252 trading days and calendar months
  • Estimate revisions: the Sector Memo used consensus for the 2027 financial year to 28 October 2026; this memo uses FactSet consensus for calendar 2027 as at 17 November 2026. The two six-month figures, 2.5% and (0.5)%, are not comparable

2. What was measured

  • Relative Strength: each stock's return minus the Stoxx Europe 600 over the same days; the Industry figure is the median stock
  • Weighting by size: market value at 31 August 2026; Deutsche Telekom is 30% of the Industry
  • Estimate revisions: FactSet consensus for calendar 2027 as at 17 November 2026
  • The split of a price change: forward earnings are the price divided by the two-year-forward price-to-earnings multiple; the remainder of the price change is the change in the multiple
  • The percentile: the multiple of 18 November 2026 in each stock's own ten-year range, held for 27 stocks
  • Defensive behaviour: an equal-weighted basket against the index day by day, split between days the index fell and days it rose
  • Past episodes: ten years of month-ends, measured against the median stock of the whole universe
  • A cross-check: a second price source reproduced the headline Relative Strength figures

3. The limits

  • Few large down days: the index fell 1% or more on nine days in six months
  • Forecast blocks cover 22 of 33 stocks
  • Past episodes use the members of 18 November 2026 only, and five or six cases cannot support a probability
  • The three-month figure differs between two window definitions: 1.0 points on one and 1.5 on the other
  • No ownership or short-interest data is held for the Industry, and no source measures European long-only ownership of Telecoms after May 2026.
