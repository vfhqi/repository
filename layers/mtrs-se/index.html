Group judgement

External change forces

Layer 1 · External change forces

Written last by the Investment Analyst for its group. The rating above stands for it, so it carries no number of its own.

Munters sits at the centre of the strongest demand force in its Sector, and over 2026 to 2027 that force is converting a capacity-constrained revenue trough into steep revenue growth on a backlog of about 1.8x sales. The external headwinds are concentrated on margin and cash, not demand: input availability and price-cost lag (now larger than the tariff), an unhedged SEK, and an AI build-out whose financing is weakening before its demand. Two later-cycle forces should shape the three- to four-year model: rivals' capacity normalising prices from about 2027 to 2028, and warm-water designs shifting value away from chillers. The external balance is positive for revenue modelling through 2027, neutral-to-negative for the margin level achievable, and increasingly negative beyond 2028.

C-02

To what extent, and in which direction, is competitive intensity changing?

F3819th of 22

Layer 1 · External change forces

Judgement. Competitive intensity on price is currently low because capacity is scarce, but it is rising structurally and will bite in 2027 to 2028 as large rivals' new capacity lands. Munters arrives at volume late in this pricing window: it is still ramping while rivals are adding capacity, so it risks shipping its largest volumes into the normalising part of the cycle.

Reasoning and critical evidence.

  • Today, benign pricing. A data-centre developer's procurement manager reports equipment makers raised prices about 10% across the board in Q3 2026 on capacity scarcity; Vertiv reports positive price-cost; industrial HVAC pricing rose 9% to 10% year on year.
  • Tomorrow, a capacity race. Trane has expanded applied HVAC and chiller capacity four-fold over three years; Carrier is building a Charlotte data-centre plant; Daikin is committing JPY 1.3tn of capital expenditure to its applied business; Vertiv is expanding capacity and modular factory-built systems (OneCore, SmartRun) that halve on-site deployment time; Modine has a USD 4bn hyperscaler chiller framework. DNB Carnegie expects peer capacity to "catch up rapidly over the next two years" and to drive pricing down as availability normalises.
  • Structural integration. Consolidation by power and electrical groups (Schneider-Motivair, Eaton-Boyd USD 9.5bn, Ecolab-CoolIT USD 4.75bn, Vertiv-ThermoKey and Vertiv-PurgeRite) creates integrated power-plus-cooling bundles that Munters cannot match. Vertiv holds 35% to 45% of hyperscale cooling against Munters' estimated 8% to 10%.
  • AirTech. Asian rotor makers and Seibu Giken have eroded Munters' share in standard battery dehumidification; Munters refused to match the discounts.

Implication for the model. Price should be a positive contributor in 2026 to 2027 deliveries of newer orders, fading to neutral or negative from late 2027 or 2028 for standard CRAH and CDU products; custom chilled-water systems are better protected.

C-05

How are input costs, input availability and supplier concentration changing, and is the net effect a headwind or a tailwind?

F3022nd of 22

Layer 1 · External change forces

Judgement. Clearly a headwind now, and the most under-appreciated driver of DCT margin. The Q3 2026 gross margin of 27.7% is the lowest in the seven years of data, despite revenue up 17.8%. Input availability, not price alone, is the binding problem: Munters' modest purchasing scale means suppliers deprioritise it in shortages. The net effect should improve during 2027 as repriced orders ship and secondary suppliers are qualified, but the improvement is slower than tariff removal alone suggests.

Reasoning and critical evidence.

  • Cost escalation. Q3 cost of goods sold rose 24% year on year (SEK 3,236m against SEK 2,611m) against sales up 18%. Tariff-driven component prices rose 15% to 16%. Steel, copper and aluminium are about half of raw materials (sources conflict on the split: 30/22/6 or 22/17/10), and sourcing is hand-to-mouth, which prevents hedging.
  • Availability. Shortages of pumps, fans, electronics, connectors and heat exchangers held back about SEK 300m of Q2 sales and continue to restrict throughput. DNB Carnegie: Munters "is not always prioritized by suppliers" because of its modest procurement scale. Munters is stockpiling components (FactSet net working capital up SEK 1.84bn in nine months).
  • The key analytical point: DCT's underlying margin worsened in Q3. DCT margin was 11.3% in Q2 with a 3 to 4 point tariff drag (about 14% to 15% before tariffs) and 9.7% in Q3 with a 2 point drag (about 11.7% before tariffs) (calculated). Sales rose about 32% quarter on quarter, yet the pre-tariff margin fell about 300 basis points. Labour inefficiency, higher material costs and expediting are scaling with volume, and the backlog now shipping was priced before 2026 cost increases. Management says price increases "will materialize gradually due to long contract lead times". The tariff is therefore no longer the main problem; ramp cost and price-cost lag are.
  • Supplier concentration is moderate overall (about 1,900 suppliers; top 25 at 38% of purchases), but critical components are effectively single-sourced at times of allocation. Supplier code-of-conduct compliance fell to 92% from 99%, a sign of a hurried supplier expansion.

Implication for the model. Model 2027 DCT margin recovery in three separable blocks: tariff removal (about +2 points, mostly by H2 2027), price-cost catch-up as post-2026-priced orders ship (gradual, perhaps +1 to +3 points over 2027), and ramp efficiency and absorption (+1 to +3 points, the least certain). Base case 14% to 16% for full-year 2027, not the high teens.

To what extent is a macro force acting on the company, on its sector and on its industry, and at which level does it bite hardest?

F3518th of 22

Layer 1 · External change forces

Judgement. Macro forces bite hardest at the company level, through two channels the sector does not share equally: an unhedged SEK reporting base and an above-target, variable-rate balance sheet. At industry and value-chain level the most important macro force is the financing of the AI build-out, which is cracking at its funding before its demand.

Reasoning and critical evidence.

  • Currency (company level, largest). Over 95% of revenue is outside Sweden; SEK costs are 10.6% of costs against 2.9% of revenue; translation is unhedged and hedge accounting is not used. A 10% SEK appreciation cuts operating profit by SEK 310m, about 17% of 2025 adjusted EBITA (calculated), more than double the typical Nordic manufacturer's sensitivity. Translation took -7% off 2025 sales and -12% off Q1 2026. Consensus FX forecasts point to further SEK strength (Rabobank EUR/SEK 10.50; USD/SEK about 9.05, calculated); the tail risk is the reverse (an energy shock weakening SEK).
  • Rates and the discount rate. The US 10-year yield is near 5.10% with the Fed still hiking; Munters' debt is Stibor-linked with no interest derivatives; leverage is 3.4x. The weekly Fitness for Fighting digest has the Sector at Underweight, Momentum at Underweight and Fragility at Double Underweight.
  • AI financing (industry and value-chain level). Neocloud and hyperscaler credit spreads are widening while demand readings hold. Colocation providers are 60% of DCT orders and depend on external financing; one colocation customer is 14% to 15% of group sales. Customer advances (10% to 30% of order value) are Munters' main working-capital funding, so a financing squeeze at customers transmits directly into Munters' cash flow.
  • Industrial macro. German construction PMI is 43.5; agricultural financing costs hurt FoodTech orders; electric-vehicle overcapacity keeps battery investment frozen.

To what extent is end-market demand changing, structurally or cyclically, and with how much visibility? (A cyclical swing counts when it has two-plus years of visibility.)

C+757th of 22

Layer 1 · External change forces

Judgement. Demand for Munters' core growth engine, data-centre cooling, has changed structurally and is visible for about two years of revenue, but the visibility is of demand, not of profit, and the record order intake of the last 12 months contains a cyclical "capacity-securing" surge that will not repeat. The Consumer should model rising revenue through 2027 against falling order intake from its 2026 peak, and should not read a 2027 book-to-bill falling towards 1.0x as a demand collapse. Outside data centres, demand is mixed: AirTech's industrial base is stable, its battery end-market is at a depressed floor, and FoodTech (to be sold) is soft.

Reasoning and critical evidence.

  • Scale of the change. Group order intake over the last twelve months is about SEK 32.1bn (Q4 2025 11,604; Q1 2026 4,701; Q2 8,699; Q3 7,070) against last-twelve-month sales of SEK 15.5bn: a book-to-bill of about 2.1x (calculated). Group backlog reached about SEK 28bn at Q3 2026 (+178% year on year), about 1.8x last-twelve-month sales. DCT orders were SEK 4,853m in Q2 (+264% organic, book-to-bill 3.6x) and SEK 4,907m in Q3 (+129% organic, book-to-bill 2.7x). The DCT backlog was SEK 19.2bn at Q2; rolling it forward with Q3 orders and sales gives roughly SEK 22bn (calculated), about 3.7x DCT's 2025 sales of SEK 5.9bn.
  • Why it is structural. AI rack densities (40 to 100+ kW per rack against 3 to 10 kW historically) roughly double mechanical and electrical content per megawatt; hyperscaler capital spending is forecast up 82% in 2026 to USD 815bn; the thermal-management market is forecast to grow at about 17% a year to 2030. Hyperscalers rose from 15% of DCT order intake in FY2023 to 36% to 40%.
  • Why part of it is cyclical. Lead times on chillers and coolant distribution units (CDUs) have stretched to 50 weeks and customers are booking capacity up to 24 months ahead. DNB Carnegie explicitly warns that colocation providers may be over-ordering to hedge shortages and that order intake normalises when competitor capacity catches up (Jefferies estimates industry capacity can add only 25% to 30% a year). The Q4 2025 (SEK 11.6bn) and Q2 2026 (SEK 8.7bn) spikes are lumpy mega-awards (SEK 2.1bn and SEK 2.0bn single orders).
  • Visibility is high on revenue, low on margin. Deliveries on the SEK 2.0bn April 2026 order run from early 2027 to Q1 2028. Customers re-allocate rather than cancel when grid connections slip (management, Q3 call), and custom contracts recover incurred cost plus a margin on termination. But the backlog was partly priced before Section 232 tariffs and before 2026 cost inflation, so revenue visibility is not margin visibility (see C-05).
  • Outside DCT. Battery orders have fallen to 10% to 15% of AirTech intake from a peak near 40%; general industrial (pharma, defence, food) is stable; US commercial (supermarket) dehumidification grew double digits in Q3; Asia-Pacific was AirTech's strongest region. AirTech's Q2 order spike (+103% organic) was partly customer pre-buying of evaporative pads, which management said will not repeat. In Q3, orders outside DCT were about SEK 2.16bn against sales of about SEK 2.68bn, a book-to-bill of about 0.8x (calculated), although still up roughly 7% year on year (calculated). FoodTech orders fell in Q3 on agricultural financing costs.

Implication for the model. DCT revenue in 2027 is capacity-limited, not demand-limited; group order intake in 2027 should be modelled below 2026 (perhaps SEK 20bn to 25bn rather than SEK 30bn+), while revenue rises; AirTech revenue growth should slow towards mid-single digits after the 2026 pre-buy.

C-03

To what extent is a disruption force strengthening or being disproved, and in which direction does it act on the company?

D5215th of 22

Layer 1 · External change forces

Judgement. The disruption force (the shift from air to liquid cooling, and towards warm-water designs) is strengthening and acts in two directions at different times: positive for volume and content through 2027, increasingly negative for Munters' product mix after about 2028. The key vulnerability is chillers, Munters' largest DCT product line, because warm-water direct-to-chip designs can reject heat with dry coolers alone.

Reasoning and critical evidence.

  • Positive now. Liquid cooling penetration for AI chips rises from 14% in 2024 to about 60% by 2027 (TrendForce); liquid configurations raise cooling content per megawatt (USD 1.3m to 1.8m liquid against USD 1.1m to 1.5m air, Bernstein); even liquid-cooled halls need 20% to 25% air cooling plus heat rejection; at least 60% of the market relies on chilled-water infrastructure (Aspman). Munters' answer covers hybrid designs: chillers (Geoclima Circlemiser), CRAHs and fan walls, CDUs (entered late 2025, part of the SEK 2.0bn order), SyCool thermosiphon, and direct-to-chip via the ZutaCore partnership.
  • Negative later. DCT's product mix is CRAHs 40%, chillers 37%, CDUs 15%, SyCool 1% (trailing twelve months to Q2 2026). Deutsche Bank's experts estimate 60% to 80% of data-centre gigawatt capacity could run on dry coolers alone at NVIDIA's 45°C inlet water, which would remove much chiller demand; the Sector digest concludes "warmer water moves the money from the chiller and the box maker to the specified valve" (Belimo, LU-VE, Carel), with a window "to about 2028". Munters lacks proprietary direct-to-chip cold plates and an NVIDIA reference architecture; CDUs are commoditising (LG's 2.6 MW CDU, Schneider 2.5 MW, Vertiv 2.2 MW) while Munters' CDU installed base is new.
  • Not disproved, not yet decisive. Modine's USD 4bn chiller framework shows chiller demand is still strong near term. Direct-to-die cooling is not expected at scale before 2030.

Implication for the model. Do not haircut 2027 DCT volume for disruption; do apply a mix and pricing headwind from 2028 on chillers and CDUs, and treat the share of chillers in new orders as a leading indicator.

C-04

To what extent is political or regulatory change under way, and how does it bear on the company?

C-559th of 22

Layer 1 · External change forces

Judgement. The dominant regulatory force, US Section 232 tariffs (a flat 25% on imports substantially of steel, aluminium or copper), is a current headwind that is fading and should become a year-on-year margin tailwind in 2027, conditional on US localisation of chiller bills of materials. Longer-term efficiency and refrigerant regulation is a modest structural tailwind; US data-centre moratoria, water caps and the November 2026 midterms are timing risks, not demand destroyers.

Reasoning and critical evidence.

  • Tariff drag on DCT margin: about 2 percentage points in Q3 2025, about 4 points in Q1 2026, 3 to 4 points in Q2 2026, back to 2 points in Q3 2026. Management expects a fully localised US bill of materials "for full-year 2027". The promise has slipped twice (relief was first expected after Q1 2026, then through Q3), so the Consumer should assume residual drag into H1 2027.

  • Regulatory tailwinds. EU Energy Efficiency Directive reporting for data centres above 500 kW, F-gas phase-downs, the US AIM Act HFC phase-down, and rising power costs all reward premium efficient equipment.
  • Timing headwinds. New York's one-year moratorium on data centres above 50 MW (July 2026), Texas audits of large-load connections, Michigan and New Jersey water and clean-energy bills (water caps penalise evaporative cooling), and Jefferies' view that Munters is the Nordic industrial most sensitive to the US midterms (permitting and power availability, affecting timing).
