APM Mini-Memo: Greggs

The one-line view. Greggs is a cash inflection you can count, as capital spending falls from its 2025 peak, resting on a shop-volume recovery you cannot yet see.

What the company does

Greggs is the United Kingdom's largest value food-on-the-go chain, selling sausage rolls, bakes, sandwiches and coffee from over 2,700 shops, 2,137 run by the company and 602 franchised. Revenue in 2025 was 2,151 million pounds, up 6.8 percent, with 88 percent from the company's own shops and the rest from franchise and wholesale, including frozen products in Tesco and Iceland. The unusual feature is that Greggs makes its own food in fifteen production and logistics hubs, which gives a gross margin of 61.5 percent and a 2025 operating margin of 8.7 percent, against about 5 percent for rivals who outsource. Greggs holds about 9 percent of the 24 billion pound daytime food-to-go market, leads out-of-home breakfast, and prices about 40 percent below Pret A Manger, though the supermarket basket is 12 percent cheaper.

A plausible investment thesis

The story is a capital spending cycle turning into cash. Greggs spent 287.5 million pounds in 2025, the peak of a programme of new hubs at Derby and Kettering built to supply 3,500 shops; spending falls to about 180 million in 2026, below the original guidance of 200 million, and to 150 to 170 million from 2027. Our research puts 2027 free cash flow at about 115 million pounds, a 6 percent yield, against negative free cash flow in 2025. The first half of 2026 showed the turn: operating cash flow after leases rose to 111 million pounds, capital spending halved to 78 million, and net cash stood at 46 million.

Earnings per share have a newer lever in costs. Greggs will close four manufacturing sites across 2028 and 2029 for 20 million pounds of annual savings, about 12 percent of 2025 pre-tax profit of 172 million, and brokers had not included this in their July 2026 forecasts. Because Greggs sets its own shop prices, the saving can stay in profit rather than go to customers, which a grocer cannot manage. Behind that sit 100 to 110 net new shops a year, wage inflation halving to 4 percent in 2026, and an operating margin already up a percentage point to 7.9 percent in the first half.

Three drivers to track. Like-for-like volume separated from price, since each 1 percent of like-for-like sales moves earnings per share by about 8 percent; utilisation of the Derby and Kettering hubs, which cost 40 basis points of margin a year through 2027 until volume fills them; and shop openings against the floor of 100 a year.

The Target Setup

The Target Setup is 'CfC Clearing in HQ low-cyclicality business on Q1/Q2 multiple, leading to reversion to normal', and this read is my own, since the card names none. The cause for concern is falling shop volumes and the fear that weight-loss drugs make the fall permanent; the business is steady daytime food; and the multiple, 14.6 times forward earnings on 6 November, sits at the 24th percentile of its ten-year range against a historical average near 21 times. The case matches this Setup only in part, and the Investment Analyst's memo of 14 August rated Setups' Fit C, because the concern has not cleared: like-for-like growth slowed to 1.0 percent in the final seven weeks of the first half, with volumes about 1 percent down. A demand-driven step-up is the bull case if volumes turn, but the files do not yet support it.

Case Riskiness and simplicity

Simple to state, hard to confirm. The Investment Analyst's memo of 14 August rated Case Riskiness C, where A is simple and low-risk, and rated all four Bottom-up ratings C; these were mapped from the memo and not re-judged since. One country, one brand and two levers, cash and volume, make the case simple; the number of headwinds makes it risky. What needs to go right is a return to flat or positive like-for-like volumes while the new hubs ramp, so that a fixed manufacturing base is filled rather than carried.

The main risk is that the volume decline is structural. Like-for-like volumes are down about 5 percent over two years. Weight-loss drug use in UK households rose from 2.3 percent in 2024 to 6.3 percent in 2026, grocers held sandwich prices while Greggs raised them 10 percent over two years, and the vertical integration that earns the margin turns any volume shortfall into manufacturing deleverage. The risk work on file reads the stock as a possible false friend, strong total sales from new shops and wholesale masking weakening volumes in the existing estate, and its triggers to re-examine the case are managed like-for-like at 1.0 percent or lower, Derby costs above the guided 10 million pound second-half headwind, and openings below 100. Smaller risks are nearly 10 percent of shares on loan, two activists, Lauro and Silchester, and a chief financial officer handover at the end of December 2026 during the hub ramp.

Business quality

A strong franchise whose returns are at a low point. Greggs gained visit share, to 8.7 percent, while UK out-of-home visits fell 1.9 percent, and its first-half revenue growth of 7.2 percent led the sector against a median of 3.9 percent. The balance sheet is net cash where pub and restaurant peers carry debt. Return on capital fell from 20.3 percent to 16.0 percent in 2025 under wage inflation and peak investment, and management targets a return to 20 percent; the volume problem reads as sector-wide rather than company execution. Pay outcomes look honest, with the bonus at 18 percent of maximum in a weak year.

Plausible returns and valuation

The weekly reading is generous; mine is more cautious. At 2,340 pence on 6 November, 3 percent below the 52-week high, the shares trade at 14.6 times forward earnings. The weekly TSR/Valuation rating of 6 November is B on valuation alone; the routine's plausible return of 29 October averages 32.7 percent a year, grade A, on consensus earnings growth of 7.7 percent a year and the multiple moving from 14.6 to 21.2 times, dividends included. That multiple carries the return. The shares have risen from 1,846 pence in August to above the sell-side median target of 1,740 pence, within a 1,330 to 2,485 pence range, so the ex-growth discount is partly closed. My own read: with the multiple unchanged, consensus earnings growth plus the dividend is a D; with volumes confirmed flat and the 2028 savings entering forecasts, a move toward 18 to 21 times gives a B to A.

Value chain and thematics: the pros and the cons

Pros. The weekly Thematic Fit rating of 6 November is A, from the food retail environment: Danske Bank names discount food retail as the place to be with rising inflation, and defensives with pricing power led in October. Greggs is domestic, in sterling, net cash and value-positioned, and its new hubs and hedged inputs give savings that it keeps.

Cons. Demand is the weak link: visits are falling, shoppers resist price rises, grocers take share with held sandwich prices and meal deals, Gail's and Wenzel's expand at the premium end, and weight-loss drugs cut appetite for the core savoury range. Regulated labour is 39 percent of costs, 900 basis points above the retail median. The Lists judgement's doubt stands: the neighbouring consumer row sits at a heavy underweight in Richard's target allocation.

In short

  • Worth its place on the Bench: capital spending falling from 287.5 million pounds in 2025 to 150 to 170 million from 2027 makes a 6 percent free cash flow yield in 2027 close to arithmetic
  • Watch volume, not sales: a 1 percent like-for-like swing moves earnings per share by about 8 percent, and the first half exited at 1.0 percent like-for-like with volumes down 1 percent
  • The savings are new: 20 million pounds from four site closures in 2028 and 2029, 12 percent of pre-tax profit, were not in July 2026 broker forecasts, and Greggs keeps what it saves
  • Valuation has done part of the work: 14.6 times forward earnings at the 24th percentile, but the price is above the sell-side median target and the weekly plausible return of 32.7 percent rests on a 21 times multiple
  • Decision point: the next like-for-like print with volumes separated from price, and Derby costs against the 10 million pound guide
