Section 1 – The Key Question and Why It Matters

1.
The Key Question examined throughout this report asks: Are transaction yields for European retail property in 2026 validating or contradicting the appraisal yields applied to listed landlords' portfolios, and what are the implications for earnings per share and equity valuations? This question sits at the heart of the investment case for European retail real estate investment trusts because the answer determines whether current share prices trading at substantial discounts to net asset value represent genuine value or justified caution. The resolution of this question carries direct consequences for landlord earnings because major UK shopping centre transactions in 2026, such as Land Securities' acquisition of the Metrocentre for £516 million, are crystallising in-place net rental yields of 7.9%, while appraisers are applying EPRA net initial yields of 6.45% to portfolios like Carmila's €6,813 million of assets. This gap of more than 130 basis points between real-world deal pricing and book valuations creates fundamental uncertainty about whether listed portfolios are accurately marked to market or whether substantial write-downs lie ahead. The earnings per share impact operates through multiple channels. When real-world transaction yields of 7.0% to 8.0% trend well above the 5.5% to 6.4% appraisal yields of listed landlords, investors must determine whether management teams can acquire assets at attractive spreads above their portfolio book yields and generate accretive growth, or whether the entire portfolio requires downward revaluation that would compress net asset value per share and elevate leverage ratios. Transactions like Hammerson's Manchester Arndale acquisition are expected to deliver more than 2% earnings accretion from day one, demonstrating that acquiring at market yields above book yields can boost near-term cash earnings even as it raises questions about the defensibility of existing appraisal assumptions. The equity valuation consequences are equally severe because European listed retail real estate trades at an average discount of 33% to last reported net tangible assets, which is wider than the sector's long-term average discount of approximately 11%. If transaction yields remain elevated at 7% to 8% while appraisers continue to apply 5.5% to 6.5% yields to portfolios, one of two outcomes must eventually materialize: either appraisers will mark down asset values to align with market pricing, compressing reported net asset value and widening the percentage discount even further, or share prices must re-rate upward as investors recognize that book values are defensible and current quotations offer exceptional value for long-term buyers.

Several closely related questions flow directly from this central inquiry. First, how rapidly will appraisers adjust their backward-looking valuation models to reflect the higher yields achieved in actual transactions? Real transaction yields are stabilizing at 7.5% to 8.0%, which is well above listed book values, yet appraisers applied EPRA topped-up yields of just 5.65% to portfolios like Mercialys, with average appraisal yield rates falling slightly to 6.63% in H1 2026.

Second, will private capital buyers continue to return to the market at these elevated yields, and if so, will their activity validate current share prices or trigger competitive bidding that compresses yields back toward appraisal levels? Third, can listed landlords maintain or grow earnings per share when they face refinancing pressures such as Mercialys issuing a €300 million bond at a high coupon of 5.25%, which creates negative leverage when borrowing costs exceed property yields?

This question matters profoundly to investors because it determines whether the current 20% to 45% discounts to net asset value represent temporary market dislocations that will close as private buyers return, or whether they reflect rational pricing of portfolios that must eventually be written down. High implied equity yields and rising swap rates present significant hurdles to securing truly accretive growth, and if listed companies must repeatedly issue shares at substantial discounts to fund acquisitions, the positive impact on earnings per share will remain highly constrained.

Investors who correctly anticipate the resolution of transaction yields versus appraisal yields can position themselves either to capture substantial upside as discounts narrow or to avoid capital destruction as valuations reset downward. The stakes are measured not in basis points but in the potential doubling or halving of equity values over the next eighteen to thirty-six months as this fundamental tension resolves.

Section 7 – If the Answer Differs from the Common View

The common consensus among market participants in late 2026 holds that European retail property transaction yields have stabilized in a 7% to 8% corridor for prime shopping centres and retail parks, reflecting a normalization after years of valuation uncertainty. This view is supported by high-profile deals such as Land Securities' Metrocentre acquisition at a 7.9% yield, Hammerson's Manchester Arndale stake at a 7.8% topped-up yield, and Eurocommercial's Avion Shopping purchase at an 8% yield.

However, if actual transaction yields deviate materially from this consensus–either compressing below 6% or expanding above 8% –the financial implications for listed landlords, their stakeholders, and the broader capital allocation landscape would be profound and multifaceted.

If transaction yields compress below 6%, the immediate beneficiaries would be the listed retail landlords whose portfolios are currently appraised at yields between 5.5% and 6.5%. Carmila's appraisal yield of 6.45%, Mercialys' EPRA net initial yield of 5.49%, and Eurocommercial's overall 5.7% yield would suddenly appear conservative rather than optimistic. This scenario would trigger valuation write-ups across portfolios, immediately boosting reported net asset values and shrinking the current 20% to 45% discounts to NAV at which these stocks trade.

The current average discount of 33% to net tangible assets would narrow sharply as equity investors recognize that book values understate rather than overstate true market pricing. Earnings per share would benefit indirectly through lower loan-to-value ratios, enabling landlords to pursue accretive acquisitions without the need for dilutive equity raises that have constrained growth. Hammerson's experience of raising £189 million at a 10% discount to net tangible assets demonstrates how current market conditions force value destruction; yield compression would reverse this dynamic entirely.

For tenants, a sub-6% yield environment would signal an intensification of landlord competition for prime retail space, likely translating into upward pressure on rental rates as landlords seek to justify compressed yields through income growth. However, tenants operating in grocery-anchored and convenience retail formats would face less immediate pressure because their formats remain structurally defensive. Retail Estates' high portfolio occupancy of 97.71% demonstrates that grocery-anchored retail formats remain resilient, and these landlords would have limited incentive to push rents aggressively if yields compress organically through capital inflows. Bank financing partners would respond to yield compression by tightening lending spreads and becoming more willing to extend higher loan-to-value facilities, as lower property yields would reduce perceived asset risk. This would particularly benefit landlords currently constrained by balance sheet limitations, such as Eurocommercial Properties, whose proportionate loan-to-value ratio sits slightly above 40.1% comfort targets.

Conversely, if transaction yields expand beyond 8%, the consequences for listed landlords would be severe and immediate. Portfolio valuations would face material write-downs as appraisers adjust their backward-looking models to reflect deteriorating market pricing. If appraisers mark valuations to match transaction pricing at 7% to 8%, asset writedowns would impact reported net income. An expansion to yields above 8% would amplify this effect, potentially erasing the modest valuation gains landlords have reported in recent periods.

Carmila's yield contraction of 11 basis points would reverse sharply, and the company's current 38% discount to net tangible assets would widen further as investors anticipate additional revaluation losses. Earnings per share would face direct pressure through two channels: lower asset values would mechanically increase loan-to-value ratios, restricting investment capacity, while higher market yields would force landlords to acquire assets at increasingly dilutive returns relative to their cost of equity capital.

Bank financing partners would respond to yield expansion by raising lending margins and imposing stricter covenant packages, as higher yields signal increased property risk and potential collateral erosion. This would disproportionately affect landlords with near-term refinancing obligations. Mercialys was forced to issue a €300 million bond at a high coupon of 5.25%, and a further expansion in property yields would compound refinancing stress across the sector.

Competing private buyers, particularly private equity sponsors and opportunistic funds, would paradoxically become more aggressive in a high-yield environment. Yields above 8% would create compelling risk-adjusted returns for all-cash buyers or those with access to low-cost institutional capital, potentially triggering a wave of take-private transactions as public market discounts become untenable. Jefferies notes that large-scale take-private transactions remain constrained by elevated borrowing costs, and visible bidders are unlikely to emerge in the near term, but this assessment assumes yields remain in the 7% to 8% range.

A sustained move above 8% would fundamentally alter this calculus by widening the gap between public equity pricing and private market valuations. Several early or ignored signals already suggest the market may be mispricing the direction of yield movement. Jefferies reports that retail yields expanded by up to 25 basis points for French, German, and Austrian shopping centres in September 2026, with France showing broad-based yield expansion.

This yield widening has not yet been reflected in the H1 2026 appraisal reports of major landlords, creating a valuation time lag that could crystallize into sharp write-downs in Q3 and Q4 results. Additionally, DNB Carnegie indicates that Swedish property values have faced continuous pressure as financing costs rise, and appraisers' backward-looking methodologies are likely masking true yield expansion. These regional signals suggest that the consensus 7% to 8% transaction yield range may underestimate the true market clearing rate, particularly for secondary assets and non-core geographies.

The market participants with the greatest leverage to shift outcomes are central banks, private equity sponsors, and strategic corporate anchors. Central banks retain ultimate control through monetary policy decisions that determine the risk-free rate and borrowing costs. A sustained pivot toward monetary easing would compress yields by reducing the discount rates applied to property cash flows, while any renewed tightening would push yields higher.

Private equity sponsors such as Ares Management, which acquired Castellana's Spanish retail park portfolio at a 7.1% disposal yield, possess the capital firepower to set market pricing through large-scale acquisitions. A coordinated series of transactions at yields above or below current consensus would rapidly reset market expectations and force listed landlords to revalue accordingly.

Strategic corporate anchors like Carrefour, which maintains a 29.9% ownership stake in Carmila, and Sagax, which holds a 22.56% equity stake in Retail Estates, can influence outcomes through capital allocation decisions and board-level governance. These anchors have the capacity to initiate take-private bids or capital partnerships that would eliminate public market discounts entirely. Activist investors represent another potential catalyst, particularly if they target landlords trading at steep NAV discounts and argue for asset sales, portfolio simplification, or formal strategic reviews.

The absence of visible activist campaigns in the sector suggests that current valuations have not yet reached levels that justify the cost and complexity of engagement, but a further widening of NAV discounts beyond 40% could change this calculus rapidly.
