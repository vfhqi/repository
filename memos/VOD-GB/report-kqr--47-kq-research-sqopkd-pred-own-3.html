Section 1 – The Key Question and Why It Matters

1.
Essential question: "For Vodafone Group Plc (VOD-GB) and Vodacom Group, what is the status and expected timetable of the Kenyan Court of Appeal case following the High Court ruling of 15 September 2026 that the Kenyan government's sale of a 15% Safaricom stake was void and the stay of execution granted on 13 October 2026; what remedies are being sought; and what would each possible outcome mean for Vodacom's consolidation of Safaricom, its non-controlling interests, the Kenyan shilling debt and the dividend purchase agreement with the Kenyan government, and Vodafone's reported EBITDAaL and adjusted EPS?"

Strategic Importance of Safaricom

2.
Strategic context: Safaricom represents the crown jewel of Vodacom's geographic diversification strategy as it transitions from a mature South African business into a high-growth pan-African operator. Following the consolidation of Safaricom on 30 June 2026, Vodacom's population reach expanded to over 600 million people across eight markets. This geographical shift significantly reduces the group's dependence on South Africa, which is expected to see its EBITDA contribution decline from 65.1% in FY26 to 42.2% by FY29.
3.
Financial contribution: Safaricom contributes approximately 25% of Vodacom's proportional EBITDA starting in FY27, elevating the group's growth profile through industry-leading EBITDA margins exceeding 50%. For the six months ended 30 September 2026, Safaricom reported service revenue growth of 13.1% and EBITDA growth of 27.5% in local currency. This robust operational performance directly supported Vodacom's 40.0% reported EBITDA growth and helped the group surpass its medium-term targets.

Financial Implications and Transaction Structure

4.
Transaction details: The disputed transaction, announced in December 2025 and completed on 30 June 2026, increased Vodacom's effective interest in Safaricom from 35% to 55%. Vodacom funded the R35 billion transaction by paying R26 billion to the Kenyan government and R9 billion to Vodafone. Additionally, Vodacom entered into a dividend purchase agreement to acquire rights to future Safaricom dividends from the Kenyan government for KES 40.2 billion.
5.
Impact on Vodafone: At the parent level, Vodafone Group fully consolidated Safaricom starting 1 July 2026, which added €1.1 billion of pro-forma EBITDAaL to its updated FY27 guidance. Although the transaction has a neutral impact on Vodafone's FY27 adjusted free cash flow, it represents a key driver of emerging market growth. Deutsche Bank Research notes that Safaricom consolidation simplifies Vodafone's investment case by focusing on consolidated cash flows rather than equity-accounted associates.

Key Risks and Court Dispute

6.
Contrary evidence: The Kenyan High Court recently declared the transaction null and void, creating immediate legal uncertainty regarding Vodacom's majority ownership. While the Nairobi Court of Appeal ordered that the status quo be maintained, the legal challenge exposes Vodacom to significant economic-ownership and valuation risks. If the appeal is unsuccessful, Vodacom's economic interest would revert to 40%, diluting the financial benefits of the transaction.
7.
Other side of the story: Even if the Court of Appeal upholds the voiding of the 15% acquisition, Safaricom's operational performance remains solid and continues to drive the underlying pan-African growth thesis. Slower cash conversion and increased exposure to emerging market currency volatility represent more pressing operational hurdles than the legal ownership dispute itself. Ultimately, the strategic benefits of regional diversification and the growth of mobile financial services like M-PESA remain intact.

Section 2 – Legal Status and Timetable of the Court of Appeal Case

Section summary
  1. The Court of Appeal has maintained the status quo for Vodacom's Safaricom stake while fast-tracking an expedited appeal expected to begin hearings by late December 2026.
  2. Legal arguments focus on the lack of public participation and procurement violations during the initial privatization process, contrasting with a history of contested state asset sales in Kenya.
  3. Petitioners seek a full unwind and return of the fifteen percent Safaricom stake to Kenyan state ownership.
  4. The Kenyan government and Vodacom are seeking a total reversal of the nullification to preserve critical infrastructure funding.

Core Legal and Regulatory Arguments

5.
Insufficient consultation: Safaricom's 15% stake sale was nullified by the Kenyan High Court primarily due to a severe lack of public participation. The three-judge bench ruled on 15 September 2026 that the privatization process bypassed constitutional requirements for robust and transparent citizen involvement. Commentators note that bypassing these institutional checks has repeatedly compromised previous state transactions. The lack of broad-based consultation undermines the legal defensibility of major stateownership changes.
6.
Flawed procurement: The Kenyan government's procurement of KCB Investment Bank as the transaction adviser violated strict constitutional standards. The High Court found that the procurement bypassed Article 227 of the Constitution and the Public Procurement and Asset Disposal Act. The state failed to demonstrate that the financial adviser was competitively selected, rendering the entire valuation process legally vulnerable. This procurement failure represents a major hurdle that the state must overcome on appeal.
7.
Unapproved merger: The High Court ruled that the Safaricom transaction violated domestic competition laws because it lacked formal regulatory consent. Although the transaction secured regional merger approval from COMESA, the court found no evidence of domestic competition clearance. The ruling held that the transaction fell directly within Kenya's merger-control framework and breached the Competition Act. Restoring the deal will likely require Vodacom to retroactively seek and obtain formal domestic antitrust clearance.

Historical and Comparative Precedents

8.
Systematic bypass: Telkom Kenya's previous controversial privatization represents a clear historical parallel of rushed, legally flawed state transactions. The state bypassed essential parliamentary oversight when executing the Telkom transaction, leading to protracted legal and regulatory disputes. Analysts observe that this pattern of avoiding institutional checks frequently triggers judicial intervention in Kenya. Rushed deals driven by short-term fiscal needs consistently run into aggressive judicial review.
9.
Contested privatization: Kenya Pipeline Company is currently facing major constitutional petitions challenging its proposed 65% state divestiture. The High Court consolidated multiple petitions raising issues about public participation and privatization frameworks. Although an interim stay was granted in May 2026, the ongoing litigation has significantly delayed KPC's privatization timeline. This precedent demonstrates that Kenyan courts routinely freeze state transactions to investigate governance failures.
10.
Extended timelines: Emerging market state-ownership disputes typically require several years to resolve through formal appellate structures. In Switzerland, a major telecom competition dispute involving Swisscom was drawn out from 2020 to 2026 before the administrative court upheld the appeal. This lengthy timeline suggests that even when appeals are fast-tracked, final judicial resolution takes longer than corporate management anticipates. Investors must prepare for extended periods of legal uncertainty in such complex disputes.
11.
Legislative delays: State-ownership restructurings in Kenya are regularly delayed by constitutional challenges to the underlying legislative framework itself. The Kenyan government had to enact the Privatization Act of 2025 to replace the Privatization Act of 2023, which was declared entirely unconstitutional by courts. This legislative friction demonstrates that regulatory overhauls in emerging markets often suffer from years of procedural restarts. Such legal bottlenecks suggest that the Safaricom appeal may face systemic, institutional delays.

Contrary Evidence and Counter-view

12.
Contrary evidence: The Court of Appeal declined the National Treasury's request to completely freeze the High Court ruling voiding the transaction. A three-judge bench ruled on 13 October 2026 that the government's application failed to meet the threshold required for a full stay of execution. While the court ordered the status quo maintained, the refusal to suspend the voiding order exposes Vodacom to ongoing legal vulnerability. This decision indicates that appellate judges are not guaranteed to overturn the lower court's landmark ruling.
13.
Highly probable: Vodacom's management appears highly likely to face a protracted legal process that extends well into late 2027. While an initial hearing date may be scheduled by December 2026, comparable Kenyan cases suggest that a final appellate ruling is unlikely before mid-2027. The complexity of the procurement and public participation issues guarantees multiple rounds of submissions. Consequently, Vodafone and Vodacom will have to operate under a cloud of ownership uncertainty for several quarters.
14.
Other side of the story: The strong political alignment and completed financial transfers between Vodacom and the Kenyan government may compel an out-of-court settlement. The National Treasury has already received and spent the KES 244.5 billion proceeds, making a full transaction reversal practically and fiscally impossible for the state. Rather than risk a chaotic unwinding, the state and Vodacom will likely execute retroactive legislative cures. This cooperative approach would ultimately validate the 55% majority consolidation without requiring a destructive judicial defeat.
