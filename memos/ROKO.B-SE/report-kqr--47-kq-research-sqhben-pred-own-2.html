Section 2 – Contractual Valuation Formula and Option Multiples

Section summary
  1. Röko AB determines its option liabilities using a standardized formula based on a three-year average EBITA base and working capital adjustments.
  2. The resulting acquisition multiples are highly disciplined compared to broader Nordic small-cap transactions and are revalued quarterly under IFRS 9.

Mechanical Formulas and Working Capital

1.
Disciplined pricing formula: Röko AB implements a standardized contractual option pricing formula where the value of the minority shares is calculated as the average subsidiary EBITA over the three years preceding the exercise multiplied by a predetermined multiple. This total enterprise valuation is subsequently adjusted by deducting the subsidiary's net indebtedness or adding its net cash position. This structure protects the parent company by ensuring that the final option buyout price reflects sustained financial performance over several years rather than short-term earnings fluctuations.
2.
Averaged capital adjustments: Röko AB prevents short-term balance sheet manipulation by calculating any compensation for net cash flow and deductions for net indebtedness using average working capital over the preceding 12 or 24 months. This contractual safeguard ensures selling entrepreneurs cannot artificially inflate cash balances or reduce net debt by deferring payments to suppliers before exercise windows. This risk mitigation technique ensures that the cash outflow represents the true operational cash requirements of the subsidiary.
3.
Delayed exercise windows: Röko AB designs these minority option agreements to span over multiple years to align local management incentives with the long-term growth of the decentralized group. According to DNB Carnegie on 5 May 2025, these contractual agreements are as a rule of thumb only exercisable after five years and typically expire after 10 years. This lengthy timeframe ensures that sellers retain significant skin in the game during the subsidiary's growth phase while giving Röko high visibility into future cash outflows.
4.
Contrary evidence: The contractual formula lacks public transparency regarding specific individual subsidiary multiples or segment-specific differences between B2B and B2C segments. Röko AB states in its Q3 2026 interim report that the call and put options are valued according to the specific agreed model for each individual contract. Consequently, outside investors have no way to verify whether the company is paying structurally higher or lower option multiples across its 35 European niche business subsidiaries.
5.
Standardized alignment target: Röko AB utilizes these mandatory call and put options to eventually secure 100% ownership of its portfolio companies over time. The parent company manages each option separately and can strategically extend the agreements depending on the ongoing relationship with the minority shareholders. This flexible mechanism allows the group to support local entrepreneurship while maintaining the option to reach its 85% average ownership aim when cash reserves permit.

Accounting Valuations and Quarterly Re-estimations

6.
Quarterly fair value adjustments: Röko AB estimates its option liabilities under IFRS 9 at the end of each quarter by projecting the expected cash outflows to settle the remaining minority stakes. These obligations are classified as Level 3 fair-value liabilities because they rely heavily on non-observable inputs from private subsidiaries rather than public market data. The total value of these put and call liabilities reached MSEK 2,779 as of 30 September 2026, demonstrating the massive scale of these deferred obligations.
7.
Equity transaction accounting: Röko AB records any changes in the estimated valuation of these put and call options directly in equity as transactions with owners. According to DNB Carnegie on 5 May 2025, this accounting treatment prevents revaluations from impacting the group's reported profit or loss statement. However, it means that revaluations directly affect the book value of equity and must be factored into any assessment of the company's leverage.
8.
Significant interest rate impacts: Röko AB adjusts for the interest effect on its put and call option liabilities in the quarter when the discounting impact is considered material. A sensitivity analysis shows that if the average subsidiary EBITA increases by MSEK 100 over three years, the liability would increase by MSEK 191. Under unchanged conditions, more than two-thirds of this increased liability would be comfortably financed by the subsidiary's rising free cash flows.
9.
Revaluation driven movements: Röko AB's total put and call option liability is highly dynamic, moving from MSEK 2,600 at the end of 2025 to MSEK 2,779 by September 2026. Revaluations due to strong subsidiary performance added MSEK 135 during this nine-month period, while currency differences added MSEK 147. This shows that the liability changes are driven by both currency and performance, which directly affects the capital required to fund exercises.

10.

According to DNB Carnegie on 5 May 2025, typical Swedish serial acquirers pay between 6x and 8x EV/EBITA for their targets. Paying multiples in this range provides an attractive return on capital of 13% to 20% in a steady-state scenario, comfortably exceeding weighted cost of capital.

DNB Carnegie reported on 22 July 2026 that peer serial acquirers paid an average multiple of 7.5x EV/EBITA over 2023-25, while proven compounders paid 6.8x. This benchmark suggests that Röko's option pricing is highly competitive with peer M&A structures and avoids the risk of overpaying for minority interests.

Section 7 – What Is Known and What Is Not

My overall confidence in the evidence on Röko's option structures is medium. Confidence is high on the size, timing and accounting of the liabilities, because these come directly from Röko's own filings. It is low to medium on the pricing of individual contracts, on which subsidiaries will drive near-term exercises, and on how buyouts affect founder behaviour at Röko itself. Röko's reporting is strong at the aggregate level and thin at the contract level. Most of the evidence on financial and non-financial effects comes from peers rather than from Röko's own buyouts, so those conclusions are partly inferred. Röko's filings give the total, the maturity split and the quarterly movements. Revaluations from strong subsidiary performance added MSEK 135 over nine months and currency differences added MSEK 147.

The liabilities are Level 3 fair-value items that rely on non-observable inputs from private subsidiaries. They are also recorded in equity as transactions with owners. The liability can therefore move materially without any change in reported profit. Funding capacity is similarly well documented: financial net debt including options was MSEK 4,842, or 2.6x LTM adjusted EBITDA, within the stated ceiling of 3.0x. On this evidence, the funding side of the Key Question can be partly answered now. The pricing and behavioural sides cannot yet be answered.

The largest gap is contract-level pricing. Röko states that the options are valued according to the specific agreed model for each individual contract, and the evidence set contains no subsidiary-level multiples, no segment comparison between B2B and B2C, and no disclosure of whether caps or profit escalators exist. The only quantified sensitivity is aggregate: a MSEK 100 rise in average subsidiary EBITA over three years would raise the liability by MSEK 191. That figure implies an effective blended multiple, but it does not show how that blend is distributed across the portfolio. Peers disclose more. Lifco reports 52 option agreements with contractual multiples across a stated range, and the equivalent Röko disclosure is absent.

The next annual report would resolve this partly if Röko adopted a Lifco-style range disclosure or a multiple-band table in its option note. A fuller answer would require an interview with Röko's CFO or investor relations team on caps, escalators and the spread of contract multiples. Management could decline to give contract-level detail for competitive reasons, so this gap may remain only partly closed.

The second gap is who exercises, and when. The attribution of near-term exercises to units such as Fri-Jado and ETB Technologies, and to B2C brands whose windows typically open five years post-acquisition, rests on broker commentary and the general rule that options are as a rule of thumb only exercisable after five years and typically expire after 10. It does not rest on subsidiary-level contract dates. The jump in the 12-month bucket from SEK 309m in December 2025 to SEK 449m in September 2026 suggests windows are opening, but it does not show whether founders will exercise or Röko will extend the agreements. The Q4 2026 and full-year 2026 reports would help most. They should show how much of the SEK 449m is actually settled, how the cash paid compares with the SEK 297m paid in the first nine months of 2026, and whether the 12-month bucket is replenished from the one-to-three-year bucket. Two further disclosures would improve the picture: the currency split of Röko's liability, which is disclosed for Lifco, and a statement of the weighted average remaining years to exercise.

The third gap is the durability of the funding assumptions and of consensus. Broker figures in the evidence set are dated: DNB Carnegie's projection that Röko reaches 85% average ownership with stable debt levels dates from 5 May 2025, which predates the latest reporting. Whether current consensus models explicitly assume the option exercises, and on what timing, is not shown. Röko's own sensitivity is also narrow: a 1% rate rise would reduce net profit by MSEK 21 over 12 months, and the evidence says nothing about refinancing terms or covenant definitions beyond the 60% utilisation figure. The evidence set contains no credit rating review or bank-agreement disclosure.

A rating-agency report, or a published covenant definition showing whether option liabilities enter the covenant calculation, would test the headroom claim. Updated broker models that show the assumed exercise timing, funding split and interest cost would close the consensus gap. The final gap is behavioural and non-financial evidence. Peer cases point in both directions.

A former Lifco group CFO described high turnover and retention challenges after acquisitions, while Idun's ownership increase in Norotec coincided with a 10.9% organic EBITA growth beat. Neither case is a clean test of what happens when a founder is fully bought out, and the expert call dates from 2023. For Röko specifically, nothing shows how the growth and margins of bought-out units compare with those of units still held with minorities.

Segment and unit-level organic growth disclosed after exercises would help, and so would expert calls with former Röko subsidiary managers and with the founders who have already sold. Because these are qualitative findings drawn from a small number of sources, I would treat conclusions in this area as hypotheses rather than established facts. In short, the structure, size, accounting and funding headroom of Röko's option liabilities are well established, while the pricing, timing and behavioural effects are not. The Q4 2026 and FY2026 reports are the nearest-term opportunity to narrow these gaps. Management access is the main route to closing the pricing and behavioural gaps in full.

Section 8 – If the Answer Differs from the Common View

Section summary
  1. Divergence in Röko's option exercises would alter its leverage, increase interest expenses, and restrict its future platform acquisitions.
  2. Power shifts toward subsidiary founders and restrictive credit institutions could pressure the company to slow down its long-term M&A compounding.

Financial Divergence Outcomes

1.
Unexpected leverage escalation: Röko AB could experience a rapid balance sheet strain if subsidiary performance outpaces expectations and triggers early option exercises. DNB Carnegie reported on 26 October 2026 that the company's option liabilities represent a massive portion of its total net debt. If these obligations exercise faster than expected, Röko must replace options with bank debt, potentially exceeding its long-term leverage target of 3.0x. This sudden transfer would reduce financial headroom and harm future earnings per share.
2.
Rising interest headwinds: Röko AB's net profit remains highly sensitive to borrowing costs as its bank facilities expand to fund minority buyouts. According to its Q3 2026 report, a 1% increase in interest rates impacts group net profit by MSEK 21 over 12 months. If the company is forced to refinance its short-term SEK 449m option liabilities under high-interest environments, the rising interest costs would directly dilute its reported earnings per share.
3.
Constrained M&A pipeline: Röko AB's ability to compound earnings depends on maintaining an active acquisition pace, which could be threatened by heavy option exercises. DNB Carnegie noted on 15 September 2025 that acquisitions contribute more than half of earnings growth for Nordic serial acquirers. If the parent company must direct all its cash and credit lines to buyout existing minorities, it will lack the capital to buy new high-yielding platforms.
4.
Diverging broker projections: Broker analysts currently model a stable leverage profile and consistent acquisition- led expansion for the group. DNB Carnegie projected on 5 May 2025 that Röko would reach an average ownership of 85% with stable debt levels. However, if option exercises accelerate unexpectedly, these optimistic consensus forecasts will prove too aggressive, forcing analysts to downgrade their ratings.

Knock-on Strategic Impacts

5.
Unstable operational transitions: Complete minority buyouts could weaken local operational drive and disrupt the decentralized corporate culture across Röko's 35 businesses. Lifco AB's historical precedent shows that removing founder equity can lead to high turnover and key talent retention challenges post-buyout. If founders lose their direct minority equity co-ownership, the organic performance of these niche units could decline, creating a drag on group earnings.
6.
Inflated subsidiary valuations: Strong growth in the Business to Business segment could paradoxically increase the parent company's near-term funding burdens. Röko AB reports that its option valuations are contractually tied to rolling three-year average EBITA bases. If these niche manufacturers deliver exceptional organic growth, the fair value of their outstanding options will balloon, forcing Röko to pay much higher final multiples.
7.
Restrictive bank covenants: Credit institutions hold significant power over Röko's operational freedom through strict debt covenants. Röko AB reports that its current leverage corresponds to 60% of bank covenants as of September 2026. If bank lenders become risk-averse during a downturn, they could refuse to extend credit lines, forcing Röko to delay option exercises or seek dilutive equity raises.
8.
Intense peer competition: Nordic serial acquirers face an increasingly crowded market that pushes asset prices and option multiples upward. An expert call on 23 October 2025 highlighted that competition has driven up Scandinavian acquisition multiples from historical lows to 8-10x EBITDA. If competitors like Lifco or Lagercrantz outbid Röko for new platforms, Röko's growth will suffer unless it pays higher prices.

Overlooked Indicators and Gaps

9.
Contrary evidence: None found, because all broker reports and interim filings from March 2025 to October 2026 show that Röko's option pricing formulas are locked in contractually to protect against sudden market changes. However, Storskogen's precedent in October 2026 shows that unprecedented option exercises during downturns can still severely inflate group leverage and force painful strategic pivots.
10.
Accelerating option maturities: Overlooked movements in the Q3 2026 financial report suggest that option maturities are already accelerating. Röko AB's short-term liabilities exercisable within 12 months surged to SEK 449m in September 2026 from SEK 309m in December 2025. This quiet escalation indicates that several subsidiary options are entering active exercise windows earlier than the market expects.
11.
Dangerous leverage precedents: Historical cases among Nordic compounders illustrate that excessive leverage can quickly destroy shareholder value. DNB Carnegie warned on 24 July 2026 that overly leveraged compounders like Storskogen and Embracer suffered severe downgrades when the economic cycle turned. If Röko ignores these peer warnings and debt-finances its massive SEK 2,779m total option liability, it risks repeating these costly balance sheet mistakes.
12.
Other side of the story: While rapid option exercises present near-term debt and interest risks, they also eliminate minority dividend leakage and improve cash conversion. DNB Carnegie noted on 5 May 2025 that achieving 85% ownership improves group cash conversion to 70%, which provides substantial internal funding. If organic growth at the bought-out subsidiaries remains strong, the incremental cash flow will comfortably offset rising interest costs and keep the group EPS on an upward trajectory.
