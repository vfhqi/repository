Section 3 – Working Capital Dynamics and Counter-Cyclical Buffering

Section summary
  1. Working capital fluctuations act as a vital counter-cyclical balancing loop for BP and its peers, releasing cash during price downturns but absorbing significant liquidity when oil prices spike.
  2. BP's planned multibillion dollar working capital release in late 2026 has been severely restricted by the surge in Brent crude prices to USD 120 per barrel.

BP's 2026 Working Capital Dynamics

1.
Seasonal build: BP experienced a massive working capital build of USD 6.0bn during the first quarter of 2026. This build was driven by USD 4.1bn in seasonal effects, USD 1.1bn in payment timing, and USD 0.8bn in Gulf of America settlement payments. The seasonal accumulation reflected higher inventory transit volumes due to longer ocean shipping routes and rising crude prices. Operating cash flow consequently dropped to USD 2.9bn, which directly pressured the major's near-term gearing metrics.
2.
Projected release: BP's management originally guided to a working capital release of USD 2-3bn in the second half of 2026. This guided release was expected to unwind as products moved through peak consumer demand periods. However, the unwind was heavily contingent on how volatile geopolitical tensions evolved in the Middle East. Management counted on this cash release to support its path toward further reducing corporate financial obligations.
3.
Commodity headwind: BP's anticipated working capital release was severely curtailed in the third quarter of 2026 due to surging oil prices. Although seasonal draws occurred, Brent rising from USD 70 to USD 120 per barrel largely offset those volume reductions. Management subsequently lowered its remaining release expectations to only about USD 0.5bn by the end of the year. Realized crude and product prices are therefore expected to remain the dominant factor determining the year-end working capital balance.
4.
Permanent build: BP's cumulative working capital build reached USD 6.5bn over the first nine months of 2026. This cumulative balance included a permanent build of USD 2.8bn that is not expected to reverse. The permanent build was primarily driven by USD 1.5bn in Gulf of America settlement payments, USD 800m in assets held for sale, and USD 500m in decommissioning payments. This permanent lock-up of cash means the balancing loop's overall cushioning capacity has been structurally reduced.

The historical precedent highlights how rapidly cash can be extracted from the supply chain when crude prices fall.
6.

The major recorded a working capital build of USD 3.7bn in the first quarter of 2020 due to unsold products. This build was subsequently mitigated by a USD 1.5bn release in the second quarter as supply chains adjusted. This demonstrates that working capital builds during sudden crises can reverse quickly once demand stabilizes.
7.
Peer comparisons: Peer majors also recorded massive counter-cyclical cash inflows during the price crashes of 2014 and 2020. Shell generated a positive USD 7.5bn working capital swing in the fourth quarter of 2014 as Brent oil prices dropped. Similarly, Shell captured a positive USD 7.5bn cash impact in the first quarter of 2020 during the pandemic pricing trough. These historical comparisons suggest that the buffering mechanism is highly consistent across peer integrated business models.
8.
Divergent trajectories: Chevron and TotalEnergies followed similar buffering patterns but with unique operational exposures during market shocks. Chevron faced USD 2.0bn of adverse working capital effects in the first quarter of 2015, which reversed as prices fell further. Meanwhile, TotalEnergies targeted a USD 1.0bn working capital release by end-2020 to preserve corporate liquidity. These experiences demonstrate that downstream and marketing assets dictate the speed of working capital cash conversion.

This commercial strategy acts as a natural cash stabilizing mechanism across volatile commodity price regimes. The physical asset base allows trading to remain profitable even when upstream production values are depressed.

However, trading operations require maintaining sufficient baseline working capital to generate commercial arbitrage returns. Stripping too much cash from the supply chain can limit trading profitability.

When oil prices fall, declining accounts receivable and inventory values automatically release cash into operations. This counter-cyclical release helps majors defend progressive dividend commitments when underlying earnings drop. The buffering mechanism prevents companies from having to rapidly gear up during brief commodity price troughs.
