Section 1 – The Key Question and Why It Matters

Section summary
  1. Medartis expects a normalized tax rate of approximately 12% in the second half of 2026.
  2. The company separates its core tax normalization from the temporary impacts of the 39% US import tariff and CHF 4.4 million refund.

The Key Question. What tax rate should investors use for Medartis Holding AG (MED-CH) in H2 2026 and FY2026, and does that rate explain why broker EPS forecasts for the company deviate? The report frames the question on a core basis and an IFRS basis separately, because the two can diverge materially. The deviation is also visible across brokers.

The paired EPS figures flagged for scrutiny are UBS at CHF 1.59/2.50 and Stifel at CHF 1.41/2.50. The later sections test whether differing tax-rate assumptions account for these gaps. Why it matters for EPS. Tax is the last deduction between operating performance and the earnings attributable to shareholders.

On a small profit base, a modest change in the assumed rate moves EPS by a large percentage. The reported core result illustrates this. Medartis reported a core tax expense of CHF 5.65 million against core income before taxes of CHF 12.43 million for H1 2026. That is an effective core tax rate of 45.5% for the six-month period.

The burden held core net profit margins to only 4.2% of core revenue. The FY2025 pattern is similar: core tax expense was CHF 5.20 million alongside core net profit of CHF 6.10 million. Against that history, the guidance is striking. Peter Hackel said that, based on current knowledge, he expects a similar tax rate of 12% in the second half of 2026.

He described the first-half normalized rate of roughly 12.5% as the rate expected going forward. Management also cites a long-term target: a tax rate of 18% to 19% for the entire group. An analyst could therefore assume about 12%, about 18% to 19%, or something near the 45.5% reported core rate. Each choice implies a very different EPS, so tax-rate assumptions are a plausible source of the broker spread.

- Earnings Calls Mechanics of core versus IFRS differences. Core earnings strip out items that management considers nonrecurring, while IFRS earnings include them. Tax-related adjustments can therefore open a gap between the two measures even when operations are identical. The H1 2026 US tariff refund is the clearest example.

After applying for a refund of the excessive 39% tariff, the group received a CHF 4.4 million refund in H1 2026. It was recognized in the profit and loss statement but not yet received in cash by the call date. Management then split the benefit. Hackel noted that the funds were used to normalize the tax rate to approximately 12.5%.

The portion not used for that purpose was recorded under IFRS, lifting the IFRS result by CHF 900,000. A separate CHF 1.7 million gain from the tariff refund was excluded from core calculations to maintain comparability. Because the refund is allocated differently between the two bases, the same event produces different tax and netprofit outcomes. IFRS EPS is therefore likely to be more volatile than core EPS.

A forecaster who mixes bases, for example by applying a core tax rate to an IFRS profit line, can build an apparent EPS deviation that reflects no real difference in business expectations.

Structural items sit beneath these one-off effects. The applicable group tax rate was 13.04% in both 2024 and 2025, but the effective rate was significantly higher because of unrecognized local tax losses and intercompany profits. Unrecognized tax losses of CHF 22.50 million were disclosed in the 2025 report. The path to a lower rate depends on whether these items reverse. As foreign units become profitable, recognition of deferred tax assets is expected to begin lowering the effective rate.

Second, Medartis does not provide a detailed geographic reconciliation of its interim tax adjustments, which makes the sustainability of the 12% target hard to verify.
