Written last by the Investment Analyst for its group. The rating above stands for it, so it carries no number of its own.

The external forces are net mildly positive for adidas's 2027 earnings and mildly negative for its multiple, and they are time-dated. The positives are company-specific and mechanical or semi-mechanical (2027 currency hedges, supplier cost bidding, a rival in retreat, a cleaner channel); the negatives are broad and cyclical (a weakening consumer, a risk-off regime, a lifestyle franchise rolling over, the World Cup pulse reverting). The Consumer should model the external contribution as front-loaded: helpful to gross margin and share in 2027, fading in 2028. The single most important external judgement is that adidas's share gains are partly borrowed from Nike's weakness, so 2028 revenue growth should not be extrapolated from 2026.

C-02

To what extent, and in which direction, is competitive intensity changing?

F3826th of 35

Layer 1 · External change forces

Judgement: Competitive intensity was unusually benign for adidas over the last 12 to 18 months because its two largest Western rivals were in self-inflicted resets. It is now turning up from that trough and will rise through 2027, as Nike re-arms, Chinese distributors liquidate Nike stock and the challenger brands lose growth and start discounting.

  • Last 12 to 18 months (benign): Nike revenue fell from USD 51.4bn (fiscal 2024) to USD 46.3bn (fiscal 2025) with gross margin down 190 basis points to 42.7%; Nike Greater China fell 17% in its fiscal fourth quarter of 2026; Nike's sportswear and Jordan lines fell mid-teens. Puma sales fell 9.4% currency-neutral in Q2 2026 after a 2025 net loss of EUR 645m. adidas grew 14% currency-neutral in Q2 against a negative sector median.
  • Next 6 to 18 months (rising): Nike's Pace programme targets about USD 2.5bn of savings to fund demand creation and product; its first full Sport Offense range ships from spring 2027; its investor day is on 16 and 17 November 2026. Nike's inventory is about 120% of next-quarter cost of goods sold, which keeps promotions elevated. Nike ends online sales rights for its Chinese distributors on 1 January 2027, leaving a five-month liquidation window (Morgan Stanley warns of discounting spill-over). On is buying US growth through paid search and its specialty-running sales fell 10% to 15% in Q3; Hoka grew only 7%; New Balance is named the main threat in men's lifestyle; Anta now owns 29% of Puma.
  • Offsets: Deutsche Bank expects Nike's sales recovery not to inflect until its fiscal 2028; Nike is cutting supply (Dunk revenue down nearly 50%, fewer Jordan retros), which is supply discipline rather than a price war.

Insight: much of adidas's 2025 to 2026 share gain is "outperformance by absence" (Wisdom Library): it reverses when the absent rival stops falling, even if nothing at adidas changes. For the model: share gains taper from the second half of 2027, and marketing must stay near 11% to 12% of sales (above Nike's demand-creation rate of about 10%) to hold shelf space.

C-05

How are input costs, input availability and supplier concentration changing, and is the net effect a headwind or a tailwind?

A632nd of 35

Layer 1 · External change forces

Judgement: A net tailwind for 2027 gross margin, driven mostly by currency hedges and supplier cost renegotiation, partly offset by freight, cotton and oil-linked materials. The risk is deferred, to 2028 if the dollar strengthens and to the health of a supplier base that adidas is squeezing.

  • Tailwinds: dollar hedges secured at 1.15 to 1.20 per euro on a rolling basis up to 24 months ahead (most seasonal volume locked about six months before the season); Redburn, Morgan Stanley and Bernstein all expect transactional currency to turn positive for gross margin in 2027, reversing part of the 2026 currency drag (about EUR 200m of operating profit). Supplier bidding, with Huali added in 2024 and supplier cost structures decoded with AI, is expected by Jefferies to add about 100 basis points of gross margin; Huali's adidas share of its own sales rises from 1% (2025) toward 10% (2028E). Up to a third of European wholesale volume now ships directly from factory to retailer.
  • Headwinds: China to Europe container spot rates were up 64% year on year in Q3 2026; Red Sea and Strait of Hormuz disruption; the Chinese cotton index up 13% to RMB 16,762 a tonne; oil feeding polymer and rubber costs; factory wage inflation.
  • Concentration: low at factory level (largest factory 6% of volume), high at country level (Vietnam 27% of all volume and 41% of footwear; Vietnam and Indonesia together 72% of footwear).
  • Supplier distress (a balancing loop): Yue Yuen warned on 26 October 2026 of a 50% to 55% fall in nine-month profit (labour, utilisation, tariff-sharing imposed by brands); Huali's margins hit record lows; Lai Yih (58.6% of 2024 revenue from adidas) faces falling Samba volumes. adidas's gains are partly suppliers' losses, which limits how long the squeeze can run.
  • Size of the currency lever (my arithmetic): the net USD transactional deficit was USD 8.7bn in 2025 (about 38% of sales); each 1% move in EUR/USD on that exposure is about EUR 75m of cost before hedging. If EUR/USD falls toward 1.14 by end-2027 (Morgan Stanley), the 2028 hedges roll at worse rates, turning the 2027 tailwind into a 2028 headwind.

To what extent is end-market demand changing, structurally or cyclically, and with how much visibility? (A cyclical swing counts when it has two-plus years of visibility.)

F4729th of 35

Judgement: End-market demand is growing structurally at mid-single digits, is softening cyclically into the winter of 2026 to 2027, and underneath both there is a sharp category rotation away from lifestyle trainers (the terrace franchises: Samba, Gazelle, Spezial) toward performance running, football and apparel. Layer 1 · External change forces

Judgement: Trade policy was the largest single external headwind of 2026 (about EUR 200m of US tariff cost in the profit outlook) and is easing at the margin: the emergency-powers tariffs were voided by the US Supreme Court on 20 February 2026 and EUR 200m was refunded in Q3; adidas's US exposure is low (about 20% of revenue) and below 3% of its US product comes from China.   1. The model's cross-check gaps: what causes each, and what the Financial Analyst should do

The one-page summary flags gaps in every forecast year. None of them changes the second set's view of the business; three are model artefacts, one is a correction to the second set's own cash-flow arithmetic. The Consumer should work from the second set's EPS (adjusted by my input change in note 3) and from the model's free cash flow for FY2027 to FY2030, not from the model's EPS, until the model is corrected.

Gap A: FY2026 EPS (model 14.31 against 9.70) and FY2026 EBITA of 3,518.8. The model's FY2026 revenue (27,118.4) and EBITDA (3,728.8, 13.75%) match the second set exactly, so the gap arises below EBITDA. EBITDA less the D&A input of 1,200 gives 2,528.8, the second set's company EBIT; the model shows EBITA of 3,518.8, which is 990 higher. 990 is FactSet's adjusted EBIT for the quarter to 30 September 2026, the period for which FactSet's EBITDA reads 0.0 (the first set's reading note 3 flagged this artefact). My reconstruction, which ties to the euro: the model carries the three reported quarters from FactSet with EBITDA 967, 846 and 0.0 and EBIT 687, 558 and 990, so the third quarter enters with EBIT but no EBITDA and no D&A to reach the year's 13.75% the model lifts the forecast fourth-quarter EBITDA margin by 20.5 points (the phasing note) to about 1,916, or 29% of sales; the year's EBITA then sums the three reported EBITs (2,235) and a fourth-quarter EBITA of about 1,284 (1,916 less a fourth-quarter D&A of 632, being 1,200 less the 568 implied for the first two quarters), total 3,519. The year's effective D&A is 210 rather than 1,200, so FY2026 EBITA, pre-tax profit (3,303.8), net income (2,428.3) and EPS are overstated by the 990 and its tax, and the FY2026 quarterly path is wrong (third quarter EBITDA nil, fourth quarter about 1.9bn). This is not an input error. The Financial Analyst should replace FactSet's zero EBITDA for the quarter to 30 September 2026 with the company's figure (company EBIT 1,008 plus D&A of about 300, about 1,310; or on FactSet's adjusted basis 990 plus about 300), so that FY2026 EBITA returns to about 2,529, the fourth-quarter phasing shift disappears and FY2026 EPS returns to about 9.70 (clean about 9.24).

Gap B: FY2027 to FY2030 EPS (model 12.21, 14.28, 16.16 and 18.28 against 11.49, 13.33, 14.91 and 16.63), with net income identical. The model's net income matches the second set to the decimal (1,949.6 against 1,950; 2,193.2 against 2,193; 2,384.5 against 2,384; 2,589.9 against 2,589), so the whole gap is the share count. The model's implied average shares (net income over EPS) are 159.7m, 153.6m, 147.6m and 141.7m against the second set's 169.7m, 164.6m, 159.9m and 155.6m: 6% to 9% fewer shares. Two features explain it. First, the model's FY2026 average share count (2,428.3 over 14.31, 169.7m) is 5.6m below the second set's 175.3m, which is consistent with the model applying the FY2026 buyback input of EUR 1bn to a FactSet share count (172.5m diluted at the refresh) that already reflects the EUR 1bn buyback completed in 2026 (6.3m shares at about EUR 159, of which 6.0m were cancelled). Second, the model removes about 6.0m shares a year from FY2027, which is EUR 1bn at about EUR 167, roughly today's price, whereas the second set assumed buybacks at EUR 185, 205, 225 and 245 (5.4m, 4.9m, 4.4m and 4.1m shares a year), because a return case that assumes a rising share price must also assume dearer buybacks. The Financial Analyst should (i) start the share count from about 172.4m at end-2026 (178.7m at end-2025 less 6.3m repurchased), with an FY2026 average of about 175.3m, so that the completed 2026 buyback is counted once; and (ii) state the price at which the model converts future buybacks into shares and, if it is the current price, change it to a price that rises with the return case; the model's EPS otherwise embeds a buyback yield of about 3.6% a year against the 2.5% to 3.0% that the second set and consensus assume. Until then the model's EPS for FY2027 to FY2030, and the two-year-forward EPS on which the return at the guideline multiple is computed, are about 6% to 9% too high.

Gap C: FY2026 free cash flow (model 1,907.6 against 1,254). My hypothesis, to be confirmed: the model builds FY2026 from three reported quarters whose cash-flow lines FactSet holds incompletely (the financials block shows capital spending and buybacks of 0.0 for the year to date, and the summary says capital spending and lease payments had to be spread evenly because FactSet held none), plus a forecast fourth quarter that carries the whole seasonal working-capital release from 25.1% of trailing sales at September (6,683) to the 22.5% year-end input (about 6,102), a release of about 580. The summary's own net-cash reconciliation (a difference of 579 between the model's year-end net debt and opening net debt plus the year's cash flow) points the same way. The second set's 1,254 (about 0.74 times net income) stands as my figure for 2026 cash after leases; the year-end working-capital ratio is the test.

Gap D: FY2027 to FY2030 free cash flow (model 80 to 92 lower each year: 1,711.9 against 1,792; 1,863.3 against 1,948; 1,860.2 against 1,948; 1,958.4 against 2,050). I do not change the lease input: 680 against a 2025 principal of 643 growing about 3% to 5% is right as principal.

  1. Where I disagree with, or go beyond, the first and second sets

  1. Cash after leases is about EUR 90m a year lower than the second set stated (gap D above). This also settles the second set's open item ("about EUR 0.7bn of lease payments a year, to be checked"): the 2025 cash cost of leases was EUR 752m, being principal 643 and interest 109.
  2. The second set's cautious wholesale read for 2027 rests partly on a Nike data point. "JD Sports keeping spring 2027 pre-orders flat" (first set C-33; second set P-06) comes from Bernstein's 9 October channel checks, which report that Nike's spring pre-orders are tracking flat while retailers reallocate open-to-buy funds toward adidas, On and Hoka. The supplier evidence in my files points the other way for adidas: Huali's sales to adidas grew more than 50% in the nine months to September (partly share taken from Yue Yuen), Lai Yih's September sales grew 14%, Pou Chen's 10.4% and Yue Yuen's 6.6%, and the wholesale report finds adidas's forward order books "showing resilient expansion". Supplier shipments have led adidas's reported wholesale sales by three to six months in past cycles (2018 to 2019, 2023). I raise FY2027 revenue growth by half a point (note 3) and treat the revenue cone as balanced around the new figure rather than skewed down; the EBIT cone stays skewed down for the reasons the second set gave (upside is reinvested, downside meets fixed costs).
  3. The World Cup lap is an apparel event whose group effect depends on footwear having a product cycle. The tournament history report shows group currency-neutral growth of +10% in 2015 against an initial mid-single-digit guide (the football category itself grew, from EUR 2.1bn to 2.2bn, on new boot franchises), +6% in 2019 within a 5% to 8% guide (apparel +9%), and flat in 2023 against a guided high-single-digit decline (apparel -11%, but with Yeezy and the inventory clean-up). In 2027 footwear is the weak category, so the lap bites harder than in 2015 and 2019; the second set's -3 point drag is right in direction and perhaps slightly heavy, because Deutsche Bank sizes 2026 kit, replica and ball sales at up to EUR 1.2bn and Bernstein at EUR 1.0bn to 1.5bn against Jefferies' EUR 1.5bn to 1.7bn gross.
  4. The 2027 hedge book is a blend, not uniformly "1.15 to 1.20". The 2025 annual report shows average forward-contract rates at end-2025 of 1.113 (long-term, maturing beyond a year) and 1.104 (short-term), against 1.133 a year earlier; the 1.15 to 1.20 refers to the hedges placed during 2026 at higher spot rates, which cover most of 2027. The second set's +80 basis points for 2027 sits between Jefferies (+20) and Bernstein (+115) and I keep it. The 2028 rates are not knowable until the 2026 annual report in early March 2027; this is the single largest open item for the second half of the 18 months.
  5. The guideline fair multiple in the model jars with the sets' phase assessment. The model computes the return at a guideline fair multiple of 28.03x two-year-forward earnings, which is the dashboard's ten-year 90th percentile ("mania" band), against 13.5x today; of the +181% return to 31 March 2028, +107% is the multiple. The first set placed the multiple at the 1st percentile of ten years but also described a stock turning up from a de-rating with a wide revenue cone, a sector the regime rates Double Underweight, technical momentum C and a dashboard Stage 2 bucket of "C_s1trans" (a stage-one transition); the guideline itself says lower in the range for stalling or turning-up stocks. The multiple is the memo Analyst's to set, but the Consumer should see the sensitivity: on the model's own two-year-forward EPS at March 2028 (about 16.95, which falls to about 15.5 once gap B is corrected), the value per share is about EUR 475 at 28.0x, about 330 at the ten-year median of 21.4x, about 305 at the 35th percentile of 19.7x, about 250 at the 10th percentile of 16.3x and about 210 at today's 13.5x; the corresponding total returns from EUR 169 are roughly +185%, +100%, +85%, +50% and +30% including about 5% of dividends. The case does not need the mania band to work; it needs the multiple to stop falling.
  6. The broker free-cash-flow divergence is definitional, not analytical. The lease report shows Jefferies deducts lease repayments (and reports 2025 operating cash flow of EUR 751m, capital spending 363 and lease repayments 643), whereas Bernstein's "free cash flow to the firm" does not; FactSet's consensus free cash flow line (2,046 for FY2027) is therefore a mixture of definitions and should not be compared with operating cash flow less capex (2,555) as if it were one number, which confirms the second set's instinct that the line is inconsistent.
  7. Suppliers no longer share tariffs, but adidas still extracts the cost through prices. The tariff report confirms Jefferies' 29 October finding that Asian suppliers now quote flat free-on-board prices and leave Section 301 duties to the importer, and that adidas instead negotiated average 5% free-on-board cuts for summer 2026 orders; the remaining refund claim is USD 25m to 75m (company, second-quarter report), smaller than the first set implied. Both points support the second set's -20 basis points net tariff line and its exclusion of further refunds.
  8. On marketing flexibility I side with the second set against the first. Of EUR 7.9bn of promotion commitments at end-2025, only about EUR 1.1bn a year is contracted for 2027 to 2030, so about two-thirds of the EUR 3.3bn marketing budget can be flexed within a quarter (the third quarter of 2026 fell to 11.5% from 13.7%); this matters for the width of the downside cone (D-04), because it gives management about one point of sales (EUR 290m) of EBIT protection in a bad year.
