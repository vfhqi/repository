Section 4 – Impact of Spain's Draft Decree on Hourly Matching

Section summary
  1. Spain's proposed Royal Decree introduces a highly restrictive framework for new data centres by mandating 80% hourly renewable matching and strict additionality.
  2. Our confidence is medium that this regulation will act as a temporary grid barrier because industry pushback is highly likely to soften the final decree.
  3. While the regulation acts as a formidable grid barrier, it serves as a lucrative tailwind for integrated developers with co-located storage.
  4. Comparative grid reforms in Ireland, the United Kingdom, and France highlight a broader European shift toward conditioning grid access on resource readiness.

1.
Tightening regulatory framework: Spain's proposed Royal Decree-Law 7/2026 mandates that data centres above 1 MW must match at least 80% of their hourly consumption with newly built renewable plants. According to Morgan Stanley's report of 10 September 2026, the decree requires qualifying power purchase agreements to have a minimum 10-year term and utilize generation assets commissioned within 18 months of operations. Threatened capital flight: Industrial associations warn that Spain's aggressive regulatory stance could backfire by driving crucial digital infrastructure investments out of the domestic market. This capital flight would result in up to massive investment relocation as capital moves to less restrictive European jurisdictions.

High asset replication: Achieving hourly matching is physically demanding and requires a massive build-out of diversified generation and storage assets. This high multiplier means that developers must deploy substantial capital into batteries and wind alongside traditional solar projects.

Section 7 – If the Answer Differs from the Common View

The prevailing market consensus assumes that data center connections will command an estimated 15% pricing premium in 2027, rising to approximately 20% by 2030, underpinned by stable regulatory frameworks and sustained hyperscaler demand. However, if this narrative resolves differently–through either a collapse in pricing premiums or rejection of Spain's draft decree–the financial and operational consequences would cascade across the entire European renewable energy ecosystem, fundamentally reshaping the earnings trajectory of developers like Solaria, Grenergy, Voltalia, EDP Renewables, and their peers.

A collapse in pricing premiums would most immediately impact developers whose forward earnings assumptions embed substantial infrastructure monetization revenues. Solaria's FY25 earnings presentation indicated unitary revenues of €0.7mn per megawatt on grid connection sales, allowing developers to crystallize value from legacy grid rights. If competitive pressure from alternative grid solutions or oversupply of secured connections drives these premiums downward toward standard wholesale power purchase agreement rates, the front-loaded revenue that materially supports early-year earnings per share would evaporate.

This repricing shock would force developers to revert to merchant exposure on a larger portion of their portfolios, exposing them to the same declining Iberian solar power purchase prices falling by roughly 14% year-over-year that have pressured baseline profitability across the sector. The knock-on effects would extend far beyond developer balance sheets. Data center customers, particularly hyperscalers who have committed to aggressive decarbonization timelines, would face a bifurcated outcome.

On one hand, lower premiums would reduce their power procurement costs and improve project economics for campus construction. On the other hand, the elimination of premium pricing signals would slow new renewable capacity additions, potentially creating grid capacity shortages that delay data center commissioning schedules. Grid availability is the limiting factor despite excellent renewable access in Spain, and without the financial incentive of premium contracts, developers may deprioritize data center-adjacent projects in favor of traditional utility-scale solar with faster payback periods.

Industrial power users would likely benefit from premium collapse in the near term, as developers redirect capacity toward standard power purchase agreements to maintain asset utilization rates. However, this reallocation would prove temporary. The absence of premium economics would curtail the aggressive capital expenditure required to meet Spain's proposed hourly matching requirements, which the renewable association APPA estimates requires 11.26MW of mixed generation per megawatt of data center load.

Without the storage and hybrid generation build-out incentivized by current premium structures, the Spanish grid would face greater evening peak stress and increased curtailment during high solar production hours, ultimately raising system costs for all industrial users. Grid operators would confront a paradoxical regulatory challenge if Spain's draft decree is rejected outright. Spain's draft decree requiring data centres of 1 MW and above to match 80% of hourly consumption with newly built renewable plants represents a major structural shift from standard annual green certificates.

Rejection of this framework would remove a critical mechanism for conditioning grid access on resource readiness, potentially flooding the connection queue with speculative capacity requests similar to the backlogs plaguing the United Kingdom, where Ofgem's demand-connection reform is built around three pillars of Curate, Plan, and Connect, using returnable commitment fees and milestone tests to filter out speculative capacity. The resulting queue congestion would delay genuine projects alongside speculative ones, exacerbating the grid bottleneck rather than alleviating it. The rejection scenario would create a competitive windfall for integrated utilities with diversified technology portfolios. Integrated companies with diversified technology portfolios can meet hourly matching at reasonable costs without undue delays, whereas pure-play developers like Solaria face higher execution risks because combining solar and storage alone remains economically challenging.

In a deregulated environment without hourly matching mandates, larger utilities could leverage existing gas and hydro assets to capture data center contracts at lower all-in costs, systematically undercutting smaller renewable developers who lack dispatchable generation. This market consolidation would concentrate infrastructure revenues among a narrow group of incumbents, permanently reducing the addressable opportunity for mid-sized developers.

Several specific actors possess the leverage to materially shift the outcome. Spain's Ministry for Ecological Transition holds ultimate authority over the final decree language and enforcement timeline. Industry feedback suggests that major utilities like Endesa expect the proposed requirements to be softened, as keeping the strict 80% hourly matching would make new developments entirely uneconomic. If ministry officials prioritize domestic investment retention over environmental stringency in response to sector lobby SpainDC's warning that the draft decree's strict additionality and correlation rules put 80% to 90% of prospective projects at risk of massive investment relocation to less restrictive European jurisdictions, a significantly diluted final decree could preserve premium economics while easing compliance burdens.

Major hyperscalers –including Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and Meta–wield enormous procurement leverage through their collective multi-gigawatt European capacity pipeline. If these buyers conclude that Spanish regulatory complexity renders Iberian projects uncompetitive versus alternative jurisdictions, they possess the scale to redirect capital expenditure toward France, Ireland, or Nordic markets with more transparent grid access frameworks.

French grid operator RTE reserved nearly 18 gigawatts of capacity for approximately 80 data center projects by May 2026, providing a ready alternative for hyperscalers seeking regulatory certainty. A coordinated hyperscaler pivot away from Spain would collapse both pricing premiums and pipeline volumes simultaneously, creating a self-reinforcing downward spiral for Iberian developers. Rival developers with offshore or international diversification strategies would benefit asymmetrically from Spanish regulatory turbulence.

Potential development on Voltalia's Pecem data centre project in Brazil, where the group is executing a disposal plan to raise up to €0.35bn by the first half of 2027, would significantly support Voltalia's highly leveraged earnings per share if European opportunities contract. Similarly, Grenergy announced its 1GW GR Data plan next door to generation sites in Chile to save expensive grid connection fees, delivering power at roughly €30 per megawatt hour.

If premium economics deteriorate while interest rates remain elevated, Grenergy's refinancing negotiations would occur under duress, potentially forcing asset sales at depressed valuations or equity dilution that permanently impairs per-share metrics.

The persistent disconnect between regulatory timelines and project execution schedules constitutes a final unheeded warning. Acciona Energia submitted a connection request in Chile for an 80 megawatt project branded Datacenter ADC-CH-Santiago, but construction is not scheduled to begin until mid-2029 with operations deferred to late 2031. These extended development horizons, replicated across international markets, suggest that the sector's capital intensity and regulatory complexity create structural barriers to rapid scaling that consensus growth assumptions fail to incorporate.
