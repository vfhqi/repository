Section 2 – Allowed Return on Equity: Pre-2024 vs New Assets

Section summary
  1. The Bundesnetzagentur's proposed shift to a unified regulatory asset base and nominal WACC model in the fifth regulatory period will significantly improve baseline returns for older utility assets.
  2. Industry participants and brokers highlight that the draft gas grid return of 4.91% is too low, though electricity grid returns are expected to settle much higher due to massive capital expenditure requirements.

Regulatory Transition and Asset Distinctions

1.
Unified asset base transition: Under the current fourth regulatory period, Germany maintains a strict distinction between pre-2024 (existing) assets and newer investments. For existing assets, the Bundesnetzagentur set the baseline allowed return on equity at 5.07% (or 4.13% post-tax), while investments made in 2024 and 2025 receive a higher preliminary return of 7% based on updated market interest rates. E.ON notes that approximately 25% of the German regulated asset base consists of "old" assets built before 2006, which are currently indexed via asset-specific inflation metrics every five years. This complex dual-track system will be replaced in the fifth regulatory period starting in 2029 with a unified nominal WACC model. Unified return expectations: Bernstein Research highlights that the transition to the fifth regulatory period will eliminate the dual-track system by establishing one unified allowed nominal WACC across all grid assets. This transition is highly favorable for utilities because pre-2024 assets will experience a substantial return uplift of approximately 120 basis points compared to their current low baseline levels. Bernstein's early modeling of the fifth regulatory period parameters points to a nominal pre-tax equity return estimate of 8.97% (based on a 2.93% risk-free rate, 14% trade tax, and 4.95% market risk premium), compared to the historical 5.07% level. This shift would represent a structural improvement in E.ON’s asset remuneration, given that pre-2024 assets constitute roughly 60% of the company's German regulated asset base.

European Jurisdictional Comparisons

3.
Iberian framework structures: Regulated utility returns in Spain and Italy operate under structurally different mechanisms compared to the proposed German system. Bernstein Research points out that Spanish electricity transmission returns are set at a nominal pre-tax WACC of 6.58% for the 2026–2031 period, representing an increase from 5.58% previously. However, the Spanish framework offers virtually no in-period protection against interest rate and inflation volatility, keeping the allowed return entirely fixed for six years. By contrast, Portugal offers annual indexation to 10-year government bonds, meaning a 100 basis point yield movement triggers a 30 basis point adjustment in the allowed regulated return. Italian trigger mechanisms: Italy's regulator ARERA employs a flexible three-year WACC determination cycle (currently covering 2025–2027) with an automatic annual trigger mechanism. Bernstein Research explains that the WACC is recalculated annually and adjusted if market parameters move the rate by 30 basis points or more in either direction. Under this methodology, Italian utilities face near-term return headwinds, with a 30 to 40 basis point reduction in allowed returns expected for 2027 due to a sharp compression in Italian sovereign bond spreads relative to Germany. Even with this reduction, Italy remains one of the most attractive European jurisdictions, offering a 5.20% real return on equity (translated to approximately 7.20% nominal under a 2% inflation assumption) compared to Spain’s nominal 6.58% and Germany’s historical 5.07% level. Contrary evidence: The Swedish regulatory framework for electricity distribution offers no inflation or interest rate indexation whatsoever, keeping its real allowed WACC fixed at 4.53% for the 2024–2027 regulatory period. This represents a much more rigid and less supportive framework than the German system, where the regulator updates returns on new investments annually to reflect changing capital market conditions. Thus, even if BNetzA's final fifth period decisions fall slightly below utility targets, the German system remains structurally superior to fixed-rate jurisdictions like Sweden and Spain.
