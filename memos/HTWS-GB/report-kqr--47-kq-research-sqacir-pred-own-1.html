4.
Consistent accounting treatment: Morgan Stanley explains that under standard accounting guidelines, the "if- converted" method is technically required for all convertible notes in diluted EPS calculations. Valuation modeling preferences: Morgan Stanley recommends that for conservative stock valuations, analysts should include full convertible dilution regardless of current profitability. This distinction is critical for forecasting cash flows because it prevents overstating the dilutive share count.
6.
Net share settlement: Morgan Stanley notes that many profitable companies prefer the "Net Share Settlement" format, where the principal is paid in cash at maturity. This choice ensures that dilution only arises for the inthe-money portion of the bond, using the treasury-stock method. Consequently, if the stock price remains below the conversion threshold, no share dilution occurs under this model.
7.
Methodological model variations: Morgan Stanley research emphasizes that many investment models struggle to accurately capture the optionality embedded within convertible bonds. Some analysts prefer to ignore dilution entirely and focus on enterprise value, while others apply full dilution. This variation in broker models explains the wide dispersion often observed in long-term street EPS forecasts.
8.
Accounting transition precedents: Jefferies notes that when companies adopt new accounting standards for convertible debt, their reported metrics can shift dramatically without any change in cash flows. For example, adopting the "if-converted" method under ASU 2020-06 reduces interest expenses but elevates the diluted share count. This historical precedent demonstrates how accounting shifts distort historical comparisons for tower sector investors.
9.
Interest add-back mechanics: Morgan Stanley explains that under the standard if-converted method, the numerator must be adjusted by adding back the after-tax interest expense. This adjustment compensates for the assumed conversion of the debt into equity at the beginning of the period. Consequently, this accounting mechanic creates an artificial boost to net income that masks the underlying cash interest obligations.
