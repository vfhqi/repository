APM Mini-Memo: PGE

The one-line view. PGE is a growing regulated grid wrapped inside a state-controlled coal company, and the case depends on power prices and the regulator carrying the coal years until offshore wind pays.

What the company does

PGE is Poland's largest producer of electricity and heat, with four businesses inside one listed company. The group mines its own lignite at Bełchatów and Turów, runs a 19.1 gigawatt fleet of coal, gas and renewable plants, owns the regulated distribution network serving 5.86 million customers, and supplies 5.73 million retail accounts. PGE holds 32% of Polish generation and 29% of distribution, and the Polish State Treasury owns 60.86% of the shares, so the state decides strategy.

The profit mix is already more grid than coal. The distribution network earned 41% of recurring operating profit before depreciation in 2025, district heating 27% and renewables 11% in early 2026, while conventional coal generation earned only 357 million zloty of recurring operating profit before depreciation in 2025. The market value is about 7.1 billion euros at a share price of 16.3 zloty on 30 October.

A plausible investment thesis

The story is a re-rating from coal holder to grid owner, with a power-price tailwind. Investors in Poland, the Czech Republic, Greece and Romania have begun valuing the state utilities as grid owners, because regulators there allow high or rising returns on state-funded grids. PGE's distribution business earns up to 12.96% on its regulated asset base against a 7.48% baseline, because the regulator pays a premium for reinvestment, and that asset base is planned to grow above 9% a year to 2030 on 37 billion zloty of grid spending. Regulated activities are forecast to reach 62% of group profit by 2030 from 38% in 2020 (Bernstein).

The near-term earnings are stronger than the headlines. Recurring operating profit before depreciation rose 21% to 3,963 million zloty in the second quarter of 2026, with record heat and gas-fired results on favourable fuel spreads and generation up 18%. Our Sector note of 9 October argued that a European gas price shock is a power price shock for every unhedged coal megawatt-hour, which could turn the Polish generators from state-controlled coal into the cheapest generation in Europe, and the weekly Lists judgement promoted PGE on the first European power-price reading in a month.

Earnings per share rise through three channels. First, the grid's asset base compounds at a premium return. Second, Baltica 2, the 1.5 gigawatt offshore wind farm, carries a 25-year contract for difference indexed from 319.60 zloty per megawatt-hour, had all 111 foundations installed on schedule in August 2026, and is due to deliver first power by the end of 2027. Third, 600 million zloty a year of capacity payments on 1.86 gigawatts of coal reserve through 2028 keep the legacy fleet from losing money while the wind farm is built. The research's bull mechanism puts adjusted earnings per share at 2.19 zloty for 2026 with a 56% joint probability: more likely than not, not safe. The drivers to track are the next household tariff decision, the distribution allowed return against the 8.3% reference, Baltica 2's first power, and the new board's 2035 strategy update.

Case Riskiness and simplicity

The case is not simple, and the main risk is sequencing under state control. The transition is funded by a regulated business that earns its premium by spending, while the cash to service that spending comes from coal plants whose economics are deteriorating: the 2025 carbon bill was 15,732 million zloty, every 14.2 zloty per tonne rise in carbon costs 220 million zloty of profit, and capital spending roughly doubles to 21 billion zloty a year. The state has shown it can rewrite the plan: the Ministry abandoned the coal carve-out in July 2025, leaving a 12,050 million zloty impairment, and the chief executive was replaced in January 2026 after less than two years. The weekly Lists judgement's own doubt is that this week's promotion rests on one broker's upgrade, one deal and a move in forward power prices without a figure.

What needs to go right: power prices stay high enough for coal to cover carbon, the regulator keeps the distribution return, Baltica 2 connects on time, and net debt stays below 3.5 times operating profit before depreciation against Fitch's 3.7 times downgrade trigger, after the cut from BBB+ to BBB in 2025. The early warnings are the next household tariff decision, the distribution allowed return against 8.3%, net debt against 3.5 times, and the receivables cycle extending beyond 42 days. Five of the six ranked drivers are set by the regulator or the state.

Business quality

Good grid, poor earnings quality: the research's confidence in business quality is MEDIUM. The distribution moat is a legally designated territory with a cost-of-capital tariff formula, and retail customers rarely leave their default supplier. Against that, 2025 recurring operating profit before depreciation of 12,892 million zloty became a reported net loss of 3.4 billion zloty on 7.5 billion zloty of coal write-downs, household tariffs do not cover costs (a 560 million zloty potential loss), and no dividend was paid for 2019 to 2024; a restart needs recurring net profit, two years of positive free cash flow and an investment-grade rating. The balance sheet is the best in its cohort, with net debt of 4.2 billion zloty and 14,197 million zloty of cash, which is what makes the spending plan possible.

Plausible returns and valuation

The plausible return is a C: 14.7% a year on average. The weekly routine of 29 October reads 19.2% a year over 18 months and 10.1% a year over three years, assuming the multiple moves from 6.8 times to 8.6 times (its fair multiple for a broadening market) while consensus earnings fall 4.7% a year, dividends included. The shares trade at 6.8 times 24-month forward earnings of 2.40 zloty, the 59th percentile of their own range, while the eastern state utility cohort sits at 8.3 times, the 95th percentile of its range, after rising 43% in twelve months.

The shares have run ahead of published targets. The price of 16.3 zloty is above every published target in our files, which span 8.0 to 14.2 zloty, and PGE was up 50% relative over thirty days by 9 October. The weekly Technical Momentum rating of 30 October is A, with the shares 7% below their 52-week high, and the weekly Sell-side Momentum rating of 28 October is A. A B return needs earnings to hold rather than fall, which is what higher power prices and the Baltica contract could deliver.

Value chain and thematics: the pros and the cons

Pros: regulators across Central and South-East Europe allow high or rising grid returns and the state funds the grids; electrification lifts network volumes, up 4%; a gas price shock lifts power prices for unhedged coal; capacity payments are locked in to 2028; and the Polish state assets minister expects higher dividends from state companies (3 September 2026). The weekly Thematic Fit/Momentum rating of 30 October is B, from the environment only.

Cons: the cohort at the 95th percentile of its range needs the coal separation as its next catalyst, and no terms have been agreed since July 2025; Poland taxes 2026 fuel revenues more than 20% above average at 60%; prosumer solar of 13.3 gigawatts and batteries eat into merchant prices; carbon sits at 74.76 euros a tonne with 14% a year growth projected; and bond yields move the whole Industry. The Pool of Stocks Eligible for Middle Innings Portfolio Selection marks PGE as leaving, in the first of two weeks, so the tactical window may be short.

In short

  • Interesting because the market is re-pricing eastern state utilities as grid owners, and PGE's distribution business earns up to 12.96% on an asset base growing above 9% a year
  • Timely because recurring profit rose 21% in the second quarter, the weekly Sell-side and Technical Momentum ratings are both A, and a European gas shock lifts power prices for coal
  • Risky because the state owns 60.86% and has already torn up the coal separation, and carbon costs of 15.7 billion zloty a year drain the cash the grid and wind farm need
  • A C return at 14.7% a year, with the price above every published target in our files, so a B needs earnings to hold rather than fall 4.7% a year
