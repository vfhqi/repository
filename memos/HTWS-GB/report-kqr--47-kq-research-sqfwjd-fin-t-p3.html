Section 2 – Loop One: Density and Site Returns

Section summary
  1. High density colocation drives superior asset yields because incremental leases incur minimal operating costs on established towers.
  2. Structural variations across markets explain why mature regions achieve high tenancy ratios while newly acquired geographies remain significantly under-leased.
  3. Customer switching costs remain highly prohibitive because physical migration entails substantial capital outlays and severe operational risks.

Economics of Tower Colocation

1.
Aggressive lease-up: Helios Towers experiences significant margin expansion when adding multiple colocations to existing passive structures. According to the company Q3 2026 interim report, these incremental tenancies require minimal additional capital expenditure compared to new site builds. This operational leverage allows the vast majority of new leasing revenue to flow directly into adjusted EBITDA, boosting cash returns on invested capital.
2.
Predictable cashflows: Independent tower operators benefit from long-term, inflation-linked master service agreements with blue-chip mobile network operators. Deutsche Bank Research noted that these multi-decade contracts typically include fixed annual rent escalators of approximately 3% to protect against localized inflation. This structure creates highly visible recurring cash flows that support long-term debt servicing and shareholder distributions.
3.
Compounding returns: Increased asset density significantly enhances the valuation multiples of telecommunications infrastructure companies. A report by Deutsche Bank Research explained that mobile towers command premium ratings because tenancy growth combined with high incremental margins supports visible cash flow expansion. This compounding effect is particularly strong in underpenetrated markets where data consumption continues to grow rapidly.

Market Specific Tenancy Analysis

4.
Strong core performance: Helios Towers has established highly dense network positions in its most mature African operations. The company reported in its Q3 2026 trading update that the tenancy ratio reached 2.79x in the DRC and 2.72x in Tanzania. These ratios reflect dense, hard-to-replicate portfolios that deter competitors from building overlapping infrastructure.
5.
Lagging newer portfolios: Newly acquired markets exhibit much lower lease density due to their early stage of commercialization. Helios Towers documented a tenancy ratio of 1.24x in Senegal and 1.32x in Madagascar in late 2026. Morgan Stanley noted that while these portfolios are currently under-leased, the rollout of 5G networks in Senegal will drive significant future densification requirements.
6.
Sustained volume growth: Geographic expansion beyond the core markets provides a long-term runway for tenancy additions. Morgan Stanley reported that Tom Greenwood, the chief executive officer of Helios Towers, highlighted very strong growth in Madagascar where the portfolio has doubled and EBITDA has tripled over five years. This suggests that the density loop is actively repeating in smaller markets as they mature.

For instance, IHS Holding reported an African colocation rate of 1.50x in Q3 2026 across its portfolio of 28,599 towers. This lower density reflects IHS's reliance on single-tenant anchor portfolios that have not yet been aggressively leased up to competing carriers.

Barrier to Site Switching

10.
High physical friction: Mobile network operators face extreme operational and financial hurdles if they attempt to migrate off established tower portfolios. Deutsche Bank Research estimated that it costs approximately EUR 50k per point of presence to physically remove active radio access network equipment and reinstall it elsewhere. This substantial upfront cost represents a powerful switching barrier that protects incumbent tower networks.
11.
Contractual lock-ins: Long-term master service agreements are structured to prevent piecemeal network migration by carriers. Expert insights from a former Cellnex director confirmed that these all-or-nothing contractual clauses make it extremely painful for mobile network operators to switch tower providers. Consequently, tower companies retain substantial bargaining leverage during contract renewals because replicating existing grids is financially unviable.
12.
Reinvestment pressure: High tenancy yields generate superior free cash flows that tower companies can reinvest into site upgrades. American Tower Corporation highlighted that tenants renew leases because repositioning sites is expensive and compromises network quality. This loop reinforces the dominance of the firstmover tower, as subsequent tenants prefer sites that already support robust power and backhaul infrastructure.

Forces Disrupting Density

13.
Carrier cost resistance: Rising tower bills driven by inflation indexation can eventually trigger aggressive customer pushback. Deutsche Bank Research noted that Telefonica is attempting to renegotiate its contracts with American Tower, showing a willingness to change operators if concessions are not made. This pressure demonstrates that compounding rent escalators can eventually destabilize long-term anchor relationships.
14.
Consolidation empty spaces: Mergers between mobile network operators pose a significant threat to established tower density. A former Cellnex executive explained that operator consolidation often leads to duplicate site decommissioning and fewer lease renewals. While contract terms protect near-term revenue, the longterm potential for colocation additions is structurally reduced in a consolidating market.
15.
Other side of the story: While high density in the DRC and Tanzania demonstrates a powerful reinforcing loop, the extremely low tenancy ratios in Senegal and Madagascar indicate that density loops are not automatic and can be stalled by carrier concentration. Jefferies Research cautioned that more challenging macroeconomic conditions could keep customer investment soft, preventing under-leased portfolios from achieving the scale economies observed in mature markets.

Section 3 – Loop Two: New Sites Seeding Colocations

Section summary
  1. Rapid site lease-up significantly enhances the return on invested capital as anchor portfolios attract colocations.
  2. Geographic and demographic variations determine whether new tower infrastructure serves urban capacity demands or rural coverage mandates.
  3. Strategic partnerships and built-to-suit commitments secure predictable volume additions for global independent operators.

Accelerating Tenant Lease Up

1.
Extensive portfolio research: Reviewing 12 relevant sources covering two major tower companies and two historical precedents shows that site lease-up velocity has increased significantly over the past decade. Helios Towers has successfully shortened the interval required to attract a second tenant to newly built structures. Consequently, this faster lease-up pace helps the company achieve its target 25% return much quicker.
2.
Accelerated leasing: Helios Towers reported at its Q3 2025 Capital Markets Day that towers built in the past four years lease up at 0.40x colocations per year, meaning a second tenant arrives in 2.50 years. This compares to older 2010 to 2015 vintages that required 5 years to reach a second tenant at 0.20x colocations annually. This rapid acceleration is driving superior asset yields across mature markets.
3.
Innovative analysis: Helios Towers credits this lease-up acceleration to its proprietary geospatial software database. Management stated during the Q4 2023 earnings call that the company uses advanced geolocation tools and artificial intelligence to predict exactly where carrier capacity will emerge. These tools have helped newer vintages achieve a faster lease-up pace of 0.50x colocations per year, reducing the lag to only two years. This technology ensures that new capital is only deployed in high-potential locations.
4.
Compounding profitability: Higher lease-up velocity significantly enhances the unit economics of individual tower structures. Morgan Stanley broker research from November 2026 stated that an initial anchor tenant delivers a modest cash-on-cash yield of 12%. However, the arrival of a second tenant raises the return on invested capital to 25%, while a third tenant pushes the yield above 34% on average. Therefore, accelerating this window directly pulls forward the timing of cash compounding.
5.
Contrary evidence: Jefferies Research pointed out in March 2025 that macroeconomic volatility and currency challenges in emerging markets could force mobile network operators to delay their physical equipment installations. Specifically, if operators postpone their co-location rollouts to preserve capital, the actual tenant arrival lag could stretch well beyond the projected 2.50 years. Such operational delays would directly stall the anticipated expansion of the company's free cash flow margins.
6.
Optimistic projections: Strategic expansion plans continue to focus heavily on driving high-margin organic lease- ups across the existing asset base. Jefferies Research highlighted in August 2024 that the vast majority of future tenancy additions are expected to be colocations. Because colocations flow through to EBITDA at approximately 80% margins, this lease-up velocity is the single most critical driver of near-term earnings growth.
