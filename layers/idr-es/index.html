C-02

To what extent, and in which direction, is competitive intensity changing?

A601st of 46

Layer 1 · External change forces

Judgement. Competitive intensity is falling in Spanish defence, stable to slightly rising across European defence, and rising in IT services. Weighted by profit, the change is modestly favourable, but the favourable part is granted by policy rather than won in open competition.

Reasoning. In Spain the state has channelled share to Indra: its share of Spanish defence rose from 7% to 17% (Morgan Stanley), it is prime or lead partner on 16 to 17 of 31 PEMs, Spanish authorities use Article 346 direct awards to bypass peacetime tendering, more than 80% of PEM budget must be executed domestically and 77% of defence procurement goes to domestic suppliers. Competitors complain that state-financed programmes "effectively expel them from Spain", which is the clearest sign of falling intensity at home and of a latent legal and political backlash. In Europe, Thales, Leonardo, Hensoldt, Saab and Rheinmetall are all adding capacity, and defence-technology entrants are targeting drones and AI, but demand still exceeds capacity, so price competition is muted. In IT services, AI is turning time-and-materials work deflationary (Rothschild & Co Redburn: ten engineers replaced by two, an 80% price cut), Microsoft is deploying about 6,000 forward-deployed engineers direct to clients, and challengers offer 40% to 45% upfront savings on legacy outsourcing.

Skew. Over 12 months the skew is mildly negative: Minsait intensity keeps rising, while the defence benefit is already in the order book.

C-05

How are input costs, input availability and supplier concentration changing, and is the net effect a headwind or a tailwind?

D4225th of 46

Layer 1 · External change forces

Judgement. A mild headwind, concentrated in labour. Labour costs are rising faster than inflation, specialised defence engineers are scarce, and electronic components are constrained; defence escalation clauses protect the defence margin, but Minsait cannot pass labour inflation on, and the factory ramp adds cost ahead of volume.

Reasoning. Personnel costs were EUR 2,824m in FY25 (52% of revenue, +8% year on year) and rose 10.1% to EUR 1,583m in 1H26 (11% in 2Q26) from salary indexation and a richer mix of defence engineers. The Risks report treats salary inflation above a 6% baseline as the warning level. Indra must add about 3,000 defence staff and has committed to 1,500 jobs in Catalonia within a year; EU sovereignty rules restrict offshore delivery. Electronic components and dual-use raw materials (antimony, mainly from Russia and China) are constrained; Air Traffic Management contracts are about 75% hardware by content. Oil at USD 111 adds energy and freight inflation. Mitigants: military contracts carry escalation clauses; Indra has consolidated 90% of aerospace and defence spend onto fewer than 450 Tier-1 suppliers, reporting savings above 10% on key sub-assemblies; it sources 77% to 78% domestically, replaced Israeli suppliers, and took a 37% stake in Sparc Foundry for gallium nitride chips. Supplier concentration is rising by design (fewer, larger Tier-1 suppliers), which lowers cost but raises dependence. Indra also pays its own suppliers slowly (48 days, with only 63.6% of payments inside Spain's legal deadline), relying on confirming lines whose withdrawal would hurt suppliers.

For the model. So far absorbed: personnel costs +10% against organic revenue +16%. The pressure point is 2027, when new plants and hires are in the cost base before full volume.

C-01

To what extent is end-market demand changing, structurally or cyclically, and with how much visibility? (A cyclical swing counts when it has two-plus years of visibility.)

A824th of 46

Layer 1 · External change forces

Judgement. Demand for the aerospace and defence businesses is undergoing a structural step-change with unusually high visibility, contracted and pre-financed to roughly 2028-2030. Demand for Minsait is softening, partly cyclically (telecoms and media clients) and partly structurally (AI deflation of legacy outsourcing). Net, demand is strongly positive because the structural upswing sits in the businesses that make most of the profit.

Reasoning and evidence.

  • Spain raised 2025 defence spending to EUR 33bn (+46%) to reach 2.0% of GDP; equipment spending is projected to rise from EUR 11bn to EUR 21bn by 2030, lifting the equipment share from 32% to 51% of the budget. The PEM framework is EUR 34bn across 31 to 35 programmes running to 2037. Indra holds about EUR 13.8bn of awards as prime on 17 programmes.
  • Conversion has barely started: PEM revenue was EUR 217m in 1H26 against EUR 97m in all of FY25. Defence backlog is EUR 11.7bn (+159% year on year), about five times FY26 consensus Defence revenue. Group backlog is EUR 20.5bn, 3.32 times last-twelve-month revenue against 1.90 times a year earlier. 2Q26 Defence orders rose 195%.
  • Air Traffic Management orders rose 57% in 1H26 (book-to-bill 2.10x, backlog EUR 1.38bn), anchored by USD 800m of US Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) radar awards with a further USD 1.7bn pipeline under bid. Mobility booked the EUR 605m Transport for London contract.
  • Minsait: 2Q26 orders fell 6.8% (-9.0% in local currency) to EUR 720m, book-to-bill 0.91x; legacy outsourcing (about 47% of Minsait sales) fell 21% in 2Q26; digital solutions grew 25%; public administration and healthcare grew 19%.

Horizon comparison. Over the last 6 months the defence step-up moved from awards to cash (EUR 1.9bn of prepayments) and to revenue (Defence +103% in 1H26). Over the next 6 months the story is conversion: revenue growth stays very high while order growth must slow from a very high, lumpy base (PEM awards were front-loaded in 2025-26). Against the prior 12 to 18 months, when demand was promised rather than contracted, visibility has risen sharply.

Fragility. Visibility is highest for contracted, pre-financed programmes, which are legally committed. It is lower for the 2029-2030 step to EUR 21bn, which requires Ministry of Defence budget lines to replace Ministry of Industry pre-financing. Spain is NATO's lowest spender relative to GDP, publicly declined to commit to the 3.5% core target at the June 2025 Hague summit, has funded the uplift through extraordinary decrees while rolling over its 2023 budget, and faces a general election due by 2027.

For the model. Defence growth of roughly +60% to +65% in FY26 (including TESS) and +20% to +27% in FY27-28 is backlog-supported and capacity-limited rather than demand-limited. Minsait should be modelled at low single-digit organic growth with a downside skew.

C-03

To what extent is a disruption force strengthening or being disproved, and in which direction does it act on the company?

D4729th of 46

Layer 1 · External change forces

Judgement. Two disruption forces are strengthening and act in opposite directions. AI is strengthening against Minsait's legacy outsourcing; drones and software-defined warfare are strengthening in favour of Indra's electronics, electronic warfare and command-and-control content, but against heavy platforms, which is exactly where Indra is now investing most of its capital. Net, near-term negative on revenue mix and neutral to positive on profit.

Reasoning.

  • AI: legacy outsourcing (about 27% of group FY25 revenue) fell 21% in 2Q26. Bernstein warns generative AI raises outsourcing price pressure faster than digital growth can offset it. The sector digest's rule applies: firms that sell hours pass the AI gain to clients; firms selling fixed-price outcomes keep it. Minsait is mainly an hours seller in its legacy book.
  • Defence: Indra's electronic warfare, counter-drone, radar and AI products (IndraMind, the SCEPTER multifunction radio-frequency project, a tethered-drone electronic warfare system, drone manufacturing in León, Spain's Class-I programme for 49 tactical drones) sit on the winning side of the shift to software-defined, drone-saturated warfare.
  • The tension: the largest capital commitments (land vehicles at Gijón and As Pontes; the satellite operator Hispasat, a geostationary operator facing low-orbit competition) are in the most physical, most disruptable parts of the value chain. Indra mitigates this by turning the Dragon 8x8 into a sensor-to-shooter platform (its NEMUS radar in Rheinmetall's StrikeShield), but this is a hedge, not an immunity.

Interplay. The AI threat is concentrated in the lowest-margin revenue, and its drag shrinks as that revenue shrinks (a balancing effect on group margin). The risk is that IndraMind's EUR 700m by 2028 target is a relabelling of existing cyber and analytics revenue (it was already EUR 300m in 2025 and EUR 191m in 1H26) rather than a new engine.

C-04

To what extent is political or regulatory change under way, and how does it bear on the company?

A852nd of 46

Layer 1 · External change forces

Judgement. Political and regulatory change is large, under way and strongly positive for Indra, bearing on demand, market share, financing and the investor base at the same time. Its weakness is that it rests on Spanish political will, the least committed in NATO.

Reasoning. NATO's 5% of GDP by 2035 target (3.5% core, 1.5% resilience, cyber and industrial base); the EU ReArm plan (up to EUR 800bn through 2029); EU defence loans requiring at least 65% European components, and buyers able to reject offers with under 50% European content across about EUR 2.6 trillion of annual public spending (Jefferies); the European Commission's clarification that defence is compatible with sustainable-finance rules, which removes an exclusion for many funds. In Spain: the PEM framework, about EUR 10.5bn of direct pre-financing allocations, EUR 800m of zero-interest capacity loans under Royal Decree 913/2025, and the formal positioning of Indra as central system integrator, which has delivered over 66% of lead-contractor awards.

(1) Spain declined the 3.5% core target in June 2025 and reached 2% only in 2025; (2) the uplift has been delivered by decree while the 2023 budget is rolled over; (3) a general election is due by 2027 and the governing coalition includes parties hostile to defence spending; (4) excluded European competitors could challenge direct awards and zero-interest loans under EU state-aid rules; (5) Spain's peacetime procurement laws (Ley 9/2017, Ley 24/2011) slow conversion of budgets into milestones. The contracted EUR 13.8bn is hard to reverse; the political risk lies in the next wave (2028 onwards) and in the timing of milestones.

Notes for the Consumer before the answers

What has changed since the first set was written (26-Oct-26).

  1. Third-quarter 2026 results were published on 29-Oct-26, but only fragments reached my sources. The AlphaSense reports written for this set quote: backlog EUR 22.2bn; quarterly order intake down 33% year on year (Morgan Stanley, which calls it "a softer quarter, but FY26 guidance reiterated and achievable; focus shifts to the Capital Markets Day"); FCAS revenue down 62% in the quarter; Defence EBIT margin 14.2% for 9M26 (17.3% excluding TESS) against 17.0% (21.3% excluding TESS) for 1H26; Air Traffic Management 3Q26 revenue +39% to EUR 147m, 9M26 EBIT margin 12.8%; Space 9M26 EBIT margin 9.9%; Minsait 3Q26 revenue EUR 699m (-3.5% reported, +3% organic for 9M26), 3Q26 EBIT margin 6.6%; Mobility 3Q26 EBIT margin 2.9%; personnel costs +9% in 9M26; 9M26 working-capital inflow EUR 1,497m and underlying (comparable) free cash flow EUR 70m; net cash EUR 1.19bn at September 2026; foreign-exchange translation EUR -28m of revenue in 9M26. Group 3Q26 revenue, EBITDA, EBIT and net income are not in my sources. My 3Q26 figures in the forecast inputs are therefore estimates and must be replaced with the actuals.
  2. The single most important unverified number is the 9M26 Defence margin. If 14.2% for 9M26 against 17.0% for 1H26 is correct, the third-quarter Defence EBIT margin alone was roughly 9% to 10% (on about EUR 0.5bn to 0.6bn of revenue), and the margin excluding TESS fell from about 21% to about 10% in the quarter. That is either a one-off charge (the FCAS halt is the obvious candidate, given FCAS revenue fell 62%) or a run-rate problem in the core electronics business. These are very different cases. I have assumed mostly one-off and timing (Defence 14.4% for FY26, recovering to 15.0% in FY27); the Consumer must check the 3Q26 release before relying on my FY27 Defence margin.
  3. A Spanish general election is now set for 29-Nov-26 (Deutsche Bank, 12-Oct-26; Cinco Días, 6-Oct-26), not "by 2027" as the first set assumed. The Capital Markets Day is dated 25-Nov-26 (Bernstein), four days before. Both main blocs support higher defence spending (the PP has called for more than 2% of GDP, some voices above 3% by 2030; Morgan Stanley, 29-Sep-26), so the demand trajectory is not at stake; leadership stability at state-influenced companies (SEPI holds 28% of Indra) is, and the PP has questioned the government's "strange" operations at Indra (13-Feb-26).
  4. The legal character of the Spanish pre-financing is now clearer. The 2025 annual report states that the EUR 2,189m received in January 2026 under several Royal Decrees is a zero-interest loan from the Treasury whose repayment is secured on the Ministry of Defence contract and made by automatic offsetting against programme inflows, so repayments slip in step with milestones. Legally it is a loan; economically it behaves as a customer advance that unwinds as Indra delivers. Separately, Santa Bárbara Sistemas (General Dynamics) is challenging Royal Decree 915/2025 before the Supreme Court on procedural grounds that, if upheld, could affect a EUR 14.4bn pre-financing framework (CE Noticias Financieras, 4-Feb-26). This is a low-probability, high-impact item for the balance sheet, not for EBIT.

Where I disagree with the first set, and why.

  1. Tax. The first set (C-54) called 25% "a reasonable forward assumption". I disagree. FY25's 25.0% rate was computed on pre-tax profit that included the EUR 99.6m TESS revaluation gain, which appears to have carried no tax; excluding it, the FY25 rate was about 30% (EUR 147m of tax on about EUR 489m of underlying pre-tax profit). In 1H26, tax plus minorities took 31.5% of pre-tax profit (pre-tax about EUR 320m, net income EUR 219m), implying a tax rate of about 28.5% to 29%. Bernstein forecasts 29% for 2026 and 30% for 2027. Consensus net income of EUR 494m on pre-tax EUR 680m for FY26 (72.6%) implies about 25%. This alone makes consensus EPS about 4% to 5% too high in every year.
  2. The size of the FY26 beat. The first set expected revenue of EUR 7.2bn to 7.3bn and EBIT of EUR 730m to 770m. After a third quarter that Morgan Stanley calls softer, with Defence orders down and FCAS revenue collapsing, I expect the guidance floors to be met with a small margin: revenue about EUR 7.06bn and EBIT about EUR 715m.
  3. FY27 revenue. The first set judged consensus revenue credible. I think FY27 consensus (EUR 7,975m, +13.7%) is about EUR 300m too high, mainly because Minsait loses the revenue of Minsait Business Consulting (about EUR 160m a year, sale to Waterland signed, held for sale) and the residual quarter of the outsourcing (BPO) unit, while consensus has Minsait growing 2.5% in FY27; and because Space has been shrinking organically (-14% in 1H26; -6% per Bernstein), so EUR 509m in FY27 needs IRIS2 revenue earlier than is evidenced.
  4. The cash reversal. I agree with the first set's direction (free cash flow well below net income from FY27) but quantify it as gradual rather than sudden: advances are consumed roughly in proportion to programme revenue (about 15% of each euro billed, since about EUR 2.1bn of advances sit against about EUR 13.8bn of prime awards), so the net unwind is about EUR 0.55bn over FY27-30, not EUR 1.9bn in two years. The reversal is a long, slow drag on reported cash, not a cliff, unless the offsetting schedule is front-loaded or a court annuls the decrees.

The base case in one paragraph. Revenue grows from EUR 5,457m (FY25) to about EUR 7,060m (FY26, +29%, roughly 17 points organic), EUR 7,670m (FY27, +8.6%, about 11% organic after the Minsait disposals) and about EUR 9,820m (FY30); the company EBIT margin rises from 9.5% to 10.1% (FY26), 10.8% (FY27), 11.5% (FY28), 12.1% (FY29) and 12.4% (FY30), almost entirely from mix and absorption, not price or cost cutting; EPS is EUR 2.69, 3.08, 3.63, 4.15 and 4.58 for FY26-30, 4% to 12% below consensus because of tax, minority interests and FY27-29 revenue; reported free cash flow is about EUR 1.94bn in FY26 (advances), then about EUR 0.31bn to 0.49bn a year for FY27-30, about 0.55x to 0.6x net income; on the company's comparable basis (excluding advance movements) about EUR 0.38bn to 0.69bn, which puts the cumulative 2027-2030 target of EUR 2.0bn to 2.5bn at its low end.
