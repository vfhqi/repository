Section 8 – If the Answer Differs from the Common View

Section summary
  1. Mobile network operators possess the structural power to disrupt the consensus growth thesis by accelerating active infrastructure sharing.
  2. Downside scenarios driven by aggressive pricing concessions during Democratic Republic of Congo renewals would severely impair long-term earnings per share.
  3. High local interest rates and elevated refinancing costs could restrict cash generation and limit post-2027 shareholder distributions.

Threat of Customer Infrastructure Integration

1.
Disrupted organic growth: Vodacom Group and Orange SA are actively collaborating to build and operate 2,000 solar-powered rural base stations in the Democratic Republic of Congo. This unprecedented operator-led joint venture directly bypasses traditional independent tower infrastructure providers by utilizing active sharing networks. If this co-building model expands widely, it will depress independent built-to-suit demand and severely restrict long-term earnings per share growth.
2.
Weakened customer demand: Airtel Africa and Vodacom Group established a major network-sharing agreement in August 2025 to optimize capital deployment across Tanzania and the Democratic Republic of Congo. This alliance allows competing operators to swap fiber networks and share passive field infrastructure to eliminate expensive capital expenditure duplication. Such cooperation threatens to slow intermediate tenancy leaseup velocity and erode the pricing leverage of independent tower operators. - Earnings Calls - Earnings Calls Restructured asset ownership: MTN Group agreed in February 2026 to acquire the remaining seventy-five percent stake in IHS Holdings to internalize its passive assets. If other major carriers follow this trend, the addressable market for independent sale-and-leaseback portfolios will contract significantly across sub- Saharan Africa, limiting future earnings per share expansion.
3.
Increased lease pressures: Vodacom Group is actively engaging in discussions with tower operators in Tanzania and the Democratic Republic of Congo to lower its cost base. If these negotiations succeed, it will dilute independent tower company margins and impair projected earnings per share.

Downside Risks to Contract Backlogs

4.
Planned revenue contraction: Helios Towers faces a scheduled drop in its Central and Southern Africa contracted revenue from US$472 million in 2027 to US$363.2 million by 2030. This steep decline reflects the conservative exclusion of upcoming Airtel Africa and Orange renewals in the Democratic Republic of Congo from the backlog. If these anchor tenants demand severe discounts, the contracted backlog will fail to recover past levels and depress future earnings per share.
5.
Lower valuation multiples: Morgan Stanley Research models a bear case fair valuation of 135p per share based on slower network roll-outs. This downside scenario assumes average tenancy additions fall to only 1.5k per year and maintenance capital expenditure increases. Consequently, applying a higher WACC of twelve point five percent to reflect rising risk-free rates will compress the company's trading multiple and restrict longterm earnings per share growth. Persistent pricing friction: Cellnex Telecom and other peer tower companies face intense customer pressure to renegotiate historical lease agreements as master agreements expire. Major telecom clients are demanding discounts of up to fifty percent, highlighting a global trend toward cost reduction. Unless Helios Towers maintains exceptional site quality and high power uptime, it will face comparable margin compression during upcoming contract renewals.
6.
Contrary evidence: Deutsche Bank Research argued that the company's dollarized contracts in the Democratic Republic of Congo protect it from typical emerging-market risks. This structural currency framework helps insulate cash flows from local inflation and currency devaluations, supporting stable earnings per share. However, this protection is vulnerable to customer-led contract renegotiations if operators demand to reduce the US dollar-indexed portion of their leases.

Structural Pressures on Cash Distributions

7.
Elevated financing costs: Jefferies Research highlighted that refinancing the outstanding March 2027 convertible bond at an eight percent rate will increase the cost of gross debt. While cash yields can partially offset this expense, a persistently high global interest rate environment will elevate the company's cost of capital. If financing conditions tighten further in frontier markets, the rising interest burden will restrict discretionary cash flow generation and weigh on future earnings. Reduced return capacity: Morgan Stanley Research emphasized that the sector remains highly sensitive to elevated yields and high cost of capital under a 5% US ten-year yield environment. If frontier financing conditions worsen, the company may prioritize debt reduction over returning capital to equity holders. Consequently, the group would be forced to restrict its share buyback program, keeping actual distributions near the conservative IMPACT 2030 floor.
8.
Localized tax leakage: Helios Towers faces structural tax inefficiencies because holding company losses in the United Kingdom, Mauritius, and the Netherlands cannot be group relieved. This localized tax leakage inflates the group's effective tax charge even as operating subsidiaries generate strong double-digit growth. Consequently, persistent withholding taxes and unrecognised deferred tax assets will continue to depress consolidated net earnings and limit cash available for shareholder distributions.
9.
Severe currency headwinds: Helios Towers remains highly exposed to foreign exchange fluctuations on its extensive intercompany monetary balances. Sudden devaluations of volatile regional currencies, such as the Tanzanian Shilling or South African Rand, generate severe intermediate exchange losses. These unhedged currency exposures introduce considerable net income volatility, distorting short-term earnings per share and complicating the tracking of dividend cover.
