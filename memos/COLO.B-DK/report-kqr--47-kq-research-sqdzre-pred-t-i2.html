Section 1 – The Key Question and Why It Matters

Section summary
  1. The market consensus on Coloplast underestimates the structural headwind of below-EBIT pressures and continuous reinvestment on long-term earnings per share.

Below-EBIT and Capital Concerns

5.
Hidden debt pressures: DNB Carnegie warns that Coloplast’s cash flows will be heavily pressured by an extraordinary tax payment of about DKK 2.0bn. 6.

To fund this elevated payout while managing high Hungarian blue-collar wage inflation and Portugal ramp-up costs, the company must rely heavily on its DKK 500m Funding the Journey savings program. This capital allocation bottleneck could restrict future earnings per share growth if borrowing costs increase.
7.

Thus, if these growth engines mature rapidly, the company's operating leverage could return sooner, refuting the flat-margin thesis and driving an upward rating cycle.

Section 3 – US Medicare Competitive Bidding and European Pricing Reforms

Section summary
  1. Upcoming Medicare competitive bidding and European price reforms present significant mid-term headwinds for Coloplast's margin profile.
  2. Analyst models diverge on US exposure, while bipartisan legislative efforts offer a potential but uncertain delay to the competitive bidding timeline.

US Medicare Competitive Bidding Timelines

1.
Negative pricing: Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services finalized a rule to include ostomy and urological supplies in competitive bidding. This cost-containment initiative is designed to introduce market-based pricing for certain medical supplies provided to patients in their homes. Consequently, manufacturers face downstream wholesale pricing pressures as distributor margins are compressed under the new program. This dynamic typically creates a direct financial impact on retailers who compete on price in order to secure specified volumes.
2.
Delayed implementation: Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services scheduled the nationwide remote item delivery competitive bidding program to take effect no later than January 1, 2028. The official window for bidder registration is set to open on December 1, 2026, with the bidding window scheduled to open January 5, 2027. This timeline provides a temporary reprieve for manufacturers as they prepare mitigation strategies. Contracts and single payment amounts will be finalized and published in late 2027 before the program officially launches.
3.
Softened pressure: Medicare plans to set the fixed distributor price at the 75th percentile of the best bids rather than the lowest bid. This methodology is expected to reduce the immediate pricing pressure on medical supply distributors. Consequently, larger distributors will be better positioned to absorb the reimbursement cuts without demanding extreme pricing concessions from manufacturers. Patient volume gains should also help successful distributors spread fixed costs across a larger base, lowering the impact.
4.
Contrary evidence: Significant industry pushback and patient advocacy concerns could still lead to the competitive bidding program being altered, delayed, or withdrawn. Historical precedents show that applying price-based bidding to clinically managed chronic supplies presents a threat to patient safety and limits product availability. Therefore, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services may alter the program before its scheduled implementation.

This legislation, titled the Access to Medical Supplies Act, proposes a five-year postponement of Medicare competitive bidding for ostomy and urological supplies. This legislative pause would buy the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services time to fully analyze patient impact.

Section 5 – Macro Sentiment, Valuation Re-rating, and Sector Capital Flows

Section summary
  1. Macroeconomic headwinds and rising interest rates have compressed European medical technology valuation multiples from their historical peaks.
  2. Structural rotation into defensive healthcare stocks has supported a tactical sector recovery despite persistent pressure from high Treasury yields.

Sector Valuation Re-rating and Recovery

1.
Gradual recovery: DNB Carnegie reported on October 22, 2026, that the European medical technology sector has begun a gradual recovery from its historical valuation troughs. This structural rebound is characterized by a 13% recovery over the three months leading to late October after a painful 14% decline over the preceding twelve months. Consequently, this tactical re-rating provides a more supportive backdrop for large-cap players as underlying chronic care demand remains highly stable. This suggests that valuation multiples are stabilizing near historical bottoms after a multi-year de-rating cycle.
2.
Persistent compression: Morgan Stanley observes that European medical technology valuations have hovered near their historical lows relative to the broader market. The sector's historical price-to-earnings premium relative to the MSCI Europe index has compressed significantly since its pandemic-era peaks. The compression reflects persistent concerns regarding inflationary pressures and localized healthcare reimbursement changes. Consequently, any sustained operational recovery could trigger a significant relative valuation expansion across the European medical technology peer group.
3.
Appealing valuation: Jefferies highlights that Coloplast’s valuation has contracted to an appealing 20x forward price-to-earnings multiple as of November 4, 2026. The multiple represents a 5% discount to the broader European medical technology sector and a compressed 40% premium to its peer Convatec. Restoring longterm valuation premiums will require the company to overcome near-term execution hurdles and clarify its commercial strategy. Consequently, this valuation entry point suggests that negative sentiment is heavily priced in at current levels.

Institutional Capital Rotation Dynamics

4.
Extreme rotation: Deutsche Bank observed on November 18, 2026, that extreme sector rotations have characterized European equity markets in recent months. Institutional investors have rotated capital away from crowded technology and banking sectors into unloved defensive areas like healthcare and consumer staples. The rotational shift has provided a powerful technical lift to European medical technology valuations after a prolonged period of underperformance. Consequently, these flows demonstrate how defensive sectors benefit from market volatility and macroeconomic uncertainty.
5.
Subtle regime: ING Wholesale Banking noted on October 22, 2026, that global equity investors are starting to scale back technology exposure. Volatile earnings and policy concerns around the US midterm elections have accelerated rotations into healthcare, consumer staples, and utilities. The trend favors geographic allocation to European equities where technology benchmarks have lower index weights. Consequently, European defensive companies are attracting significant defensive institutional capital to protect year-end performance.
6.
Contrary evidence: Deutsche Bank’s global fund flow data on November 13, 2026, showed that dedicated technology sector funds still led inflows with 8.9 bn in new capital. Meanwhile, broader European equity funds continued to lag other geographies with muted net inflows. Such persistent technology preference suggests that any defensive rotation into healthcare remains tactical and highly vulnerable to rapid reversals when growth sentiment improves. Consequently, long-term valuation expansion for medical technology names cannot rely solely on passive sector rotation.

7.
Structural ceiling: DNB Carnegie notes that the historical relationship between high interest rates and medical technology valuation multiples remains a key concern for investors. During the previous zero-rate environment, investors willingly paid extreme premiums of 35x to 45x for Coloplast's highly predictable defensive growth. Consequently, the structural shift in risk-free rates places a hard ceiling on valuation multiples across the sector.

With ten-year US Treasury yields rising to 5.14% in November 2026, higher borrowing costs directly threaten interest expenses and capital allocation flexibility.
