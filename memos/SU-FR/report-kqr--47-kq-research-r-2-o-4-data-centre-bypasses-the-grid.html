Executive Summary

Section summary
  1. The key thematic question is highly resolvable because accelerating interconnection queues are actively forcing hyperscalers to deploy self-generation infrastructure. The behind-the-meter data center power transition represents a fundamental architectural shift in how hyperscalers and colocation developers secure electrical capacity amid unprecedented grid congestion. Data from Aterio indicates that behind-the-meter projects represent 22% of data center capacity under construction and 40% of the active pipeline, reflecting an accelerating movement away from traditional utility-tied power procurement. This transition is being driven by severe transmission bottlenecks, with ERCOT facing an unprecedented 474 GW interconnection queue bottleneck where data centers represent 90% of active requests for grid capacity, while in France, transmission system operator RTE manages a 6 GW queue in Île-de-France alongside a 7 GW queue in Hauts-de-France. & Gallagher LLP The economic implications for electrical equipment manufacturers are profound and multifaceted. Hyperscalers bypassing grid queues are dramatically multiplying electrical content per megawatt by purchasing localized power plants, substations, and eHouses directly from equipment makers, creating a substantial valuation step-up opportunity for pure-play exposed companies. The content escalation is quantifiable and material, as developers transition from traditional grid-tied configurations to comprehensive behind-the-meter systems that include onsite generation, redundant substations, and modular electrical enclosures. This shift compresses deployment timelines while simultaneously expanding the addressable revenue pool per installed megawatt, enabling equipment manufacturers to capture significantly higher dollar content per project than conventional gridconnected facilities.

The market sizing dynamics underscore the multi-year runway ahead for this transition. McKinsey and Company has upgraded its 2030 U.S. cumulative IT load forecast by 25% to roughly 121 GW, projects global leased data center capacity to expand from 82 GW to 219 GW by 2030, and hyperscalers and colocation developers are projected to invest roughly $800bn in electrical equipment globally through 2030. These projections reflect not merely incremental capacity additions but rather a structural reconfiguration of power delivery architecture that favors integrated equipment providers capable of delivering turnkey solutions. Pricing power has emerged as a critical investment dimension within this thematic framework. Due to component shortages, lead times for Western heavy-duty gas turbine deliveries now stretch out to seven years, creating an environment where dominant manufacturers can enforce multi-year contract structures with favorable terms and substantial backlog visibility. The scarcity-driven pricing environment is reinforced by capacity constraints across the Western OEM base, enabling market leaders to lock in elevated margins through raw-material indexation clauses and structured advance payment mechanisms that protect against cost inflation.

From a pure-play exposure perspective, Vertiv represents the closest proxy to a data center pure-play because it derives 80% of its total revenue from this high-growth vertical, while integrated system providers like Schneider Electric and Eaton offer comprehensive grid-to-chip product stacks that capture the full behind-the-meter value chain. These purest plays benefit disproportionately from the architectural transition because their portfolios align directly with the equipment categories experiencing the most acute supply constraints and the highest pricing leverage.

In contrast, diversified conglomerates face business model dilution from traditional utility, industrial automation, and transport exposures that dampen their direct sensitivity to behind-the-meter growth. The catalyst timeline is actively unfolding across both operational and regulatory dimensions. At xAI's Colossus site in Memphis, massive power swings of 10 to 20 MW wore out turbine shafts until the company deployed 150 MW of Tesla Megapacks to stabilize their unpermitted generators, illustrating the real-world execution challenges developers face when bypassing grid infrastructure.

Legislative reforms and utility dockets scheduled throughout 2027 are expected to provide critical permitting clarity that either accelerates project conversions or imposes environmental constraints that redirect capital toward alternative technologies. However, the investment thesis confronts material countervailing forces that could compress the competitive window. If grid-enhancing technologies rapidly unlock 23 GW of capacity, the immediate need for costly behindthe-meter generation will collapse, representing a fundamental risk to sustained equipment demand.

Additionally, rising community opposition to localized gas-powered generation, coupled with tightening emissions frameworks in jurisdictions like Virginia, could force developers to abandon behind-the-meter projects in favor of renewableheavy grid connections or large-scale battery storage alternatives. The emergence of Chinese turbine manufacturers like Dongfang Electric, which are rapidly expanding production capacity, introduces competitive pressure that may erode Western OEM pricing power earlier than consensus anticipates.

The second-order implications extend well beyond electrical equipment manufacturers into adjacent infrastructure categories. The behind-the-meter transition is catalyzing substantial capital deployment into midstream natural gas pipelines, commercial battery storage systems, and fuel cell technologies that offer noncombustion alternatives to reciprocating engines. These spillover effects create both complementary investment opportunities and potential substitution threats, depending on how regulatory frameworks and technology cost curves evolve over the next eighteen to thirty-six months. Ultimately, the behind-the-meter data center power transition represents a high-conviction thematic opportunity supported by observable grid capacity deficits, quantifiable content per megawatt step-ups, and extended equipment lead times. The core thesis remains that dominant electrical manufacturers with integrated product portfolios will capture outsized share of an expanding addressable market while sustaining elevated margins through scarcity-driven pricing power. Nevertheless, investors must continuously monitor regulatory developments, alternative technology adoption rates, and competitive capacity additions to assess whether the favorable supply-demand imbalance persists through the end of the decade or contracts prematurely due to grid modernization or environmental constraints.
