Section 2 – Historical Behaviour in Container Downturns (2009, 2016, 2019, 2023)

Section summary
  1. APM Terminals demonstrated resilient EBITDA margins and ROIC during historical downturns through aggressive cost-containment measures.
  2. The segment consistently outperformed global volume contractions, securing steady cash flows to support Maersk Group's broader capital allocation.

Resilient Performance in the Great Recession

1.
Stable profitability: APM Terminals achieved a remarkable financial performance during the global container trade collapse of 2009. The company reported segment revenue of USD 3,021 mm in 2009, representing a mild 3% decline compared to USD 3,119 mm in 2008. This top-line resilience cushioned the Maersk Group as broader container shipping activities suffered severe losses. Consequently, the terminals division protected overall group cash flows during one of the worst maritime downturns.
2.
Efficient cost-containment: APM Terminals expanded its operational profitability in 2009 through aggressive corporate reorganization and negotiation. The segment EBITDA rose to USD 738 mm in 2009 from USD 573 mm in 2008. This cost discipline drove a substantial EBITDA margin expansion to 24.40% from 18.40% in 2008. Strong cost-mitigation measures thus allowed the company to keep its return on invested capital stable at 9.80% in 2009.
3.
Superior volume performance: APM Terminals limited its throughput losses during the 2009 crisis relative to wider industry averages. The operator recorded a 7% decline in containers handled during 2009. This drop was significantly better than the 12% contraction in global throughput reported for the same period. By outperforming the market, the segment demonstrated its strategic positioning and strong retention of major shipping lines.
4.
Defensive market share: APM Terminals successfully broadened its third-party customer base to mitigate reliance on Maersk Line during the 2009 downturn. Non-Maersk Line volumes rose to 41% of total segment throughput in 2009, up from 38% in the prior year. Meanwhile, major Chinese port peers expected a 6% container contraction in 2009 due to weakening global demand. This customer diversification strategy insulated APM Terminals from the direct financial shocks of ocean shipping rate collapses.

Operational Stagnation and Impairments in 2016

5.
Challenging market conditions: APM Terminals faced a severe slowdown in 2016 driven by industry overcapacity and low oil prices. Segment revenue was flat at USD 4,176 mm in 2016 compared to USD 4,138 mm in 2017. High exposure to oil-dependent African economies and stagnant European volumes pressured operating margins. This exposure dragged down the return on invested capital to 5.70% in 2016 from 10.90% in 2015.
6.
Underwhelming margin performance: APM Terminals lagged behind its global peers in operational profitability during the 2016 shipping downturn. The division's EBITDA was USD 764 mm in 2016, translating to an EBITDA margin of 18.30% (calculated) which was much lower than its historical levels. By comparison, pure-play port operators maintained an industry average EBITDA margin of 45% over the long term. This margin gap highlighted regional dynamics and asset control issues where ownership is shared with local authorities.

Section 3 – Concession Tariffs: Indexation, Caps, and Jurisdictions

Section summary
  1. APM Terminals secures long-term concession agreements with inflation-indexed tariffs to defend its unit revenues during shipping downturns.
  2. Regulatory price caps and high variable fee structures in emerging markets restrict pricing flexibility but provide strong volume-based downside protection.
  3. Standard port contracts across major peers combine fixed lease royalties with consumer price indexation to insulate terminal margins from operating cost inflation.

Global Concession Frameworks and Fixed-Fee Structuring

1.
Progressive duration: APM Terminals structures its portfolio around long-term concessions that secure multi- decade operational rights and predictable cash flows. The operator secured a thirty-three year lease extension at Port Elizabeth with the Port Authority of New York and New Jersey through December 2062. This long duration ensures ample time to amortize expensive yard automation and infrastructure investments. Consequently, these extended timelines act as a protective barrier that defends long-term return on invested capital from short-term volume shocks.
2.
High upfront: APM Terminals frequently utilizes public-private partnerships that require substantial initial payments to secure exclusive gateway access. The company paid a twenty-five million dollar upfront fee to the Chittagong Port Authority for the Laldia Container Terminal in Bangladesh. This terminal is being developed under a thirty-three year public-private partnership model that expands the country's maritime capacity. These heavy upfront investments restrict entry from smaller competitors and preserve localized gateway market share during overcapacity periods.

The company pays an annual fixed fee of seven hundred thousand Bahraini dinars that grows at two percent annually. The operator must meet minimum key performance indicators for vessel working rates and crane productivity. These operational requirements demonstrate that infrastructure concessions carry regulatory default risks if labor disruptions or vessel delays compromise terminal efficiency.

Inflation Indexation and Automatic Tariff Adjustments

5.
Contrary evidence: APM Terminals has failed to meet the mandatory transshipment volumes required under its Bahrain concession since commercial activities began. The government has the legal authority to terminate the Khalifa Bin Salman Port agreement due to this persistent volume deficit. However, the Ministry of Transportation and Telecommunication has waived its termination rights as long as marketing efforts continue. This waiver shows that concession volume covenants are sometimes flexible rather than rigid points of operational failure.
6.
Price indexation: APM Terminals protects its operating margins from labor and energy cost inflation by embedding automatic indexation formulas in its concession contracts. Standard lease agreements allow the operator to adjust its tariff rates annually in accordance with the American Consumer Price Index. This price indexation provides a direct hedge against rising dockworker wages and utility costs. Consequently, these contractual escalators insulate terminal operating margins from severe macroeconomic cost pressures during high-inflation cycles.
7.
Regulated limits: APM Terminals operates within strict pricing boundaries because a significant portion of its global gateway ports are subject to public tariff caps. Concession agreements in emerging markets often prescribe maximum tariffs that operators can charge shipping lines. These contracts prohibit unilateral rate hikes without the explicit prior approval of local port authorities. These regulatory caps limit short-term pricing flexibility but protect importing nations from exploitative logistics pricing.

Peer Comparative Downsides and Structural Shields

8.
Peer indexation: Peer operator International Container Terminal Services Inc secures similar downside protection by structuring its concession agreements with automatic tariff adjustments. The company operates long-term agreements with an average remaining life of approximately twenty-two years. These concessions frequently contain provisions that allow for periodic repricing based on local inflation rates. This widespread indexation guarantees steady average revenue growth across its diversified global portfolio.
9.
Peer restrictions: Peer operator International Container Terminal Services Inc faces strict regulatory tariff boundaries that govern its pricing power. Approximately seventy to eighty percent of the operator's ports are located in environments where tariffs are heavily regulated. These public restrictions mean that the operator must petition local governments for tariff adjustments, which often causes major execution delays. This heavy regulation exposes terminal cash flows to political intervention and delayed inflation adjustments.
10.
Peer tariffs: Peer operator Hutchison Port Holdings Trust experiences divergent tariff trends across its key Asian port concessions. The trust negotiates low-single-digit tariff increases tied to consumer price inflation at its high-performing Yantian terminal. In contrast, tariff rates at its Hong Kong terminal have remained flat for quite a while because the port is losing cargo volumes. This divergence suggests that market demand dictates pricing power even when contracts allow inflation-linked adjustments.
11.
Minimum commitments: Peer operator DP World mitigates container volume risks by incorporating strict minimum throughput commitments in its landlord concession contracts. The company pays fixed rent combined with a variable royalty per container moved. Some contracts contain specific volume guarantees that require minimum concession payments if shipping lines fail to meet agreed throughput targets. These commitments transfer operational volume risks from the terminal operator back to the primary shipping lines.
12.
Regulatory interventions: Peer operator COSCO Shipping Ports remains exposed to unexpected regulatory price interventions in highly centralized jurisdictions. The National Development and Reform Commission of China forced several major ports to cut handling tariffs by eleven to twenty-one percent in 2018. These cuts demonstrated that regulators can override contract terms to lower national logistics costs. Consequently, such regulatory overrides threaten expected terminal earnings growth despite the presence of long-term concessions.
13.
Other side of the story: APM Terminals' reliance on inflation-indexed tariffs may not fully protect its profitability because high variable concession fees and strict minimum volume commitments can squeeze operating margins. While indexation increases average revenue per move, it also triggers automatic hikes in variable concession fees paid to local port authorities. Furthermore, if alliance partners fail to meet volume targets, operators must still pay fixed lease royalties. Therefore, these rigid cost structures dilute the defensive benefits of indexation.

This dynamic demonstrates how terminal congestion and freight rate tailwinds rise in tandem. Consequently, terminal gridlock serves as a leading indicator of broader group profitability.

Other side of the story: APM Terminals' elevated 2026 earnings are fundamentally distorted by temporary, congestion-related storage fees that mask underlying cost inflation. Consequently, the segment's high 2026 margins are distorted by temporary, congestion-related storage income and do not represent sustainable structural growth.
