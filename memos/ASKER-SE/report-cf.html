EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Scale of change

Asker Healthcare Group has experienced material expansion over the last twelve months, growing from more than 50 companies and 4,500 employees in 2025 to more than 5,000 employees across 19 countries by mid- 2026, while completing more than 20 acquisitions during 2024–2025 that added approximately SEK 3.8 billion of revenue. The company preserved continuity in its Chief Executive Officer, Chief Financial Officer and Chief Operating Officer roles while refreshing acquisition and people leadership, and successfully completed a 2025 initial public offering that raised approximately SEK 1.5 billion and increased the share count from approximately 321 million to 383 million weighted-average shares.

The strategic direction remains consistent around a twinengine model of organic growth and acquisitions, but the enlarged group now operates a more complex platform requiring greater integration discipline, working-capital management and balance-sheet capacity. The company moved reported net debt to adjusted EBITDA from approximately 2.1 times in 2024 to 2.37 times by June 2026, approaching its stated ceiling of below 2.5 times, while management maintained its more-than- 15% annual adjusted EBITA growth target and medium-term margin ambition of above 10%.

Ranked future EPS drivers

Acquisition pace and acquired earnings is the largest numerical driver, because management targets transactions contributing 10–12% of annual adjusted EBITA growth and first-half 2026 acquisitions contributed approximately 18% of adjusted EBITA growth, making this driver essential but medium-plausibility dependent on financing capacity, target availability and disciplined execution. Organic healthcare demand and market-share gains delivered 5% organic sales growth in the first half of 2026 against an estimated 3–4% European medical technology market growth rate, supporting medium plausibility for 4–5% organic growth over the next eighteen months, although confidence remains medium because 2025 organic growth was only 3% and some earlier growth benefited from exceptional defence-related demand.

Acquired margin, mix and synergy realisation lifted the adjusted EBITA margin to 10.0% in the second quarter of 2026 from 9.5% a year earlier, but DNB Carnegie's alternative calculation suggests the sustainable cash margin may be closer to 8.1%, making further expansion medium-plausibility and dependent on productivity gains and accounting treatment. Working capital and freecash-flow conversion remains critical because Asker's acquisition model depends on internally generated cash, yet inventory days increased from approximately 59–65 days in 2022 to 82 days in the second quarter of 2026, while DNB Carnegie assumes forward free-cash-flow conversion of only 55% of adjusted EBITA after including annual intangible investment of SEK 160–170 million, lowering plausibility to medium.

Leverage and financing costs directly constrain future acquisitions because reported net debt reached SEK 4.5 billion with leverage at 2.37 times by June 2026, while DNB Carnegie's broader definition including contingent consideration and obligations suggests effective leverage of 3.1–3.3 times, making meaningful deleveraging medium-plausibility and dependent on cash conversion improving while earnings continue to grow.

Gothenburg warehouse savings and cost efficiency should deliver SEK 50–60 million of annual savings from the second half of 2027, but the project has not yet demonstrated realised savings and carries medium plausibility because implementation costs and temporary inventory duplication could delay the benefit. Share count and incentive-plan dilution is a lowerimpact but visible driver because the average share count increased approximately 8.4% to 383 million in the first half of 2026, while employee incentive programmes create only modest prospective dilution of approximately 0.81% combined maximum.

Latest results

The second quarter of 2026 delivered solid reported growth, with net sales rising 18% to SEK 4.7 billion and adjusted EBITA increasing 24% to SEK 471 million, producing a 10.0% margin and adjusted EPS of SEK 0.57, although the consensus beat was modest at approximately 1% against company-compiled consensus and DNB Carnegie's own estimate was in line. Organic sales growth reached 5% without the large defence and preparedness orders that distorted prior comparisons, while acquired growth contributed 13% and currency effects were negative 2%, confirming the twin-engine model rather than proving that underlying demand alone can sustain mid-teens earnings growth.

Business Area North showed genuine organic recovery with 10% organic sales growth and a 13.5% margin, while West and Central accelerated with margins rising to 9.6% in both areas, but acquired growth dominated the strongest segments and management attributed most gross-margin improvement to the acquired portfolio's higher margins. Adjusted cash flow from operating activities improved to SEK 330 million in the second quarter from SEK 211 million, but the quarter was affected by SEK 463 million of earn-out payments including SEK 238 million through operating cash flow, alongside a SEK 149 million dividend and higher working capital, meaning statutory cash conversion provides less support for the headline earnings increase than adjusted measures suggest.

Net debt reached SEK 4.5 billion from SEK 2.9 billion a year earlier, lifting reported leverage to 2.37 times from 1.84 times, while first-half investing cash flow was negative SEK 422 million including SEK 354 million for subsidiary acquisitions and SEK 112 million of tangible and intangible investment.

Guidance reliability and beat-or-miss history

Management's guidance deserves medium trust because the company consistently delivers its stated more-than- 15% annual adjusted EBITA growth target, having exceeded it in every reported rolling period from the first quarter of 2025 through the second quarter of 2026 with outcomes ranging from 17% to 22%, but provides no formal revenue, EBIT, EPS or free-cash-flow forecast and excludes material cash costs from its preferred adjusted measure.

The medium-term adjusted EBITA margin target above 10% has been approached but not consistently achieved on a rolling basis, rising from 9.1% in the first quarter of 2025 to 9.8% in the second quarter of 2026, with quarterly margins reaching 10.0% in the fourth quarter of 2025 and second quarter of 2026 but the full-year 2025 margin remaining at 9.5%, showing credible progress rather than completed delivery. Asker's available broker-comparison record shows adjusted EBITA beats in the first quarter of 2025, third quarter of 2025, fourth quarter of 2025 and first quarter of 2026, while the second quarter of 2025 was broadly in line and the second quarter of 2026 beat company-compiled consensus by approximately 1%, supporting a pattern of meeting or modestly beating expectations rather than consistently producing large surprises.

Reported leverage reached 2.37 times at June 2026 against a target below 2.5 times, leaving narrow headroom, while DNB Carnegie's alternative ratio including contingent consideration and obligations suggests effective leverage of approximately 3.1–3.3 times during the period assessed, materially reducing confidence in the amount of acquisition capacity available. Trust would rise materially if Asker delivered several consecutive periods of statutory free cash flow close to adjusted EBITA, reduced inventory days, lowered recurring integration costs and brought leverage down while continuing acquisitions, but would fall if reported leverage exceeded 2.5 times, organic growth returned toward the 3–4% market rate, or provisions and capitalised investment produced repeated downward adjustments.

Broker positioning

Coverage is unusually concentrated, with AlphaSense containing direct Asker research from one broker, DNB Carnegie, while the 2025 annual report separately lists analysts at BNP Paribas, Citi, Danske Bank, Nordea and SB1 Markets whose current ratings, targets and estimates cannot be verified in the supplied evidence. DNB Carnegie experienced a material recommendation reversal, moving from BUY with a target price of SEK 105 on 5 August 2026 to SELL with a target of SEK 60 on 10 September 2026 after a forensic accounting review and benchmarking against AddLife, before upgrading to HOLD with a target of SEK 66.5 on 1 October 2026 after the shares underperformed AddLife by approximately 20% since the downgrade.

The central concern in the September downgrade was that capitalised intangible investment, acquisition and integration costs, depreciation and provisions may have made reported adjusted EBITA materially stronger than sustainable cash earnings, with DNB Carnegie estimating that more conservative treatment could reduce 2025 adjusted EBITA from SEK 1.594 billion to just above SEK 1.2 billion. The broker cut its 2027–2028 earnings per share estimates by 2–5% and reduced equity free-cash-flow estimates by 15–17% after including annual intangible investment of SEK 160–170 million, while placing its revised 2027–2028 adjusted EBITA estimates 4–9% below Bloomberg consensus.

The October HOLD reflects a balanced valuation after share-price underperformance rather than a resolution of the September earnings-quality dispute, meaning the most important broker signal is the wide gap between operating-growth optimism and confidence in the quality of the earnings base used to compound that growth.

Fundamental peer position

Against AddLife, Asker has faster historical revenue growth, lower working-capital intensity and similar cash EBITA relative to working capital, but materially lower cash EBITA margins of 8.1% versus AddLife's 11.7% and higher leverage after broader adjustments including contingent consideration and obligations, which places Asker last on absolute margin and earnings quality among the named peers despite its stronger growth potential.

Asker's 2025 reported adjusted EBITA margin of 9.5% was below AddLife's 12.1%, while its 2025 net working capital represented approximately 8.4% of sales versus 12.3% for AddLife, creating a genuine working-capital advantage that does not eliminate the balance-sheet disadvantage because Asker's adjusted EBITA-to-workingcapital ratio of 112.7% compares with AddLife's 97.7% but reported leverage remains higher. Against the proven compounders Addtech, Indutrade, Lagercrantz and Lifco, the evidence supports Asker's stronger growth potential but does not establish that its current margin, leverage or accounting quality is at the peer median, while Lagercrantz delivered 18% earnings-per-share growth in financial year 2025/26 with an 18.1% full-year EBITA margin and Lifco reported a 23.1% EBITDA margin in the second quarter of 2026.

Asker ranks first on latest reported revenue-growth observation with second-quarter sales up 18% and first-half sales up 15%, comprising 5% organic growth, 12% acquired growth and a negative 2% currency effect, exceeding the estimated 3–4% European medical technology market growth, but the majority of reported expansion remains acquisition-driven rather than purely organic and therefore more dependent on continued financing capacity than peers with stronger organic momentum.

Key outside forces

Structural demand is favourable because the European MedTech market was estimated at approximately SEK 1,355 billion in 2024 and is expected to grow by roughly 3–4% annually through 2028, driven by ageing, chronic disease, homecare and efficiency requirements, but recent organic growth should not automatically be treated as structural because public procurement represents approximately 70% of European medical-product purchasing while 55% of procurement processes focus mainly on price, creating a difficult environment for distributors that cannot demonstrate service, quality or lower total care costs.

Competition remains intense because European medical-product distribution contains approximately 37,000 companies, many of them small, owner-managed businesses, while Asker's market share remains below 10% in every country in which it operates and approximately 1% overall, implying that local distributors, family businesses, private-equity-backed platforms and suppliers themselves can still contest contracts and increase acquisition prices.

Regulation is a competitive advantage for scaled distributors because the European Union Medical Device Regulation and In Vitro Diagnostic Regulation require more documentation, clinical evidence, traceability and quality controls, which can exclude smaller competitors and support Asker's tender position, but compliance increases regulatory staff, training, audit and product-onboarding costs while data regulation under the General Data Protection Regulation creates potential fines of up to EUR 20 million or 4% of worldwide annual turnover for serious breaches.

Supply is relatively diversified because the group sells products from more than 2,000 suppliers, sources approximately 80% of products from Europe and approximately 20% mainly from Asia, with no individual supplier representing more than 7% of cost of goods sold, but rising inventory days to 82 days in the second quarter of 2026 tie up cash and increase obsolescence risk while geopolitical logistics exposure including the Red Sea, Gulf of Aden, trade tensions and possible tariffs could raise transportation costs and extend transit times. The business cycle is less cyclical than industrial distribution because healthcare demand is defensive, but equipment, capital projects, elective procedures and preparedness orders introduce meaningful volatility, as demonstrated by North's 2024 growth benefiting from large defence and preparedness deliveries followed by negative 1% organic growth in the fourth quarter of 2025 after those comparisons weakened.

Interest rates are a direct EPS sensitivity because Asker carries acquisition-related debt and floating-rate exposure, with the 2025 annual report estimating that a one-percentage-point change in interest rates affects net financial items over the following 12 months by approximately SEK 35 million, while currency movements affect reported earnings with the company estimating that a 1% strengthening or weakening of the Swedish krona against other currencies changes group earnings by approximately SEK 3.7 million.

Activist-investor status

No activist investor, activist stake, public campaign or documented demand for strategic change was identified in the supplied research findings for Asker, with the ownership materials instead showing a significant long-term principal owner and institutional shareholders participating in the nomination process rather than an externally documented activist campaign.

The absence of an activist means there is no identifiable near-term EPS impact from activist proposals, although if an activist later demanded slower acquisitions and faster deleveraging, nearterm EPS growth could fall through lower acquired revenue but financing costs might decline, while a demand for more aggressive acquisitions could lift short-term EPS but increase leverage and execution risk.

Principal risks

Earnings-quality risk is that reported adjusted EBITA materially overstates sustainable cash earnings because DNB Carnegie estimated that more conservative treatment of capitalised intangible investment, acquisition costs, provisions and depreciation could reduce 2025 adjusted EBITA from SEK 1.594 billion to just above SEK 1.2 billion, implying a 20–25% earnings-base difference that would materially lower the compounding rate for future EPS even if acquisition activity continues [Cross-ref: Section 4 + Section 5 + Section 8].

Leverage and acquisition-capacity constraint arises because reported net debt to adjusted EBITDA reached 2.37 times by June 2026 against a target below 2.5 times, while DNB Carnegie's broader definition including contingent consideration and obligations suggests effective leverage of 3.1–3.3 times, meaning weaker cash conversion or higher earn-out payments could force slower acquisitions precisely when organic growth normalises toward the 3–4% market rate and the company loses both engines of its growth model simultaneously [Cross-ref: Section 4 + Section 9 + Section 10].

Interaction and joint-failure risk reflects the linked nature of the drivers, because organic growth and acquisitions reinforce one another through a broader product portfolio and stronger customer relationships, but weaker organic growth can make acquisitions more important just as lower cash generation reduces the ability to fund them, while debt-funded acquisitions can increase EPS when the acquired EBITA return exceeds the incremental financing cost but higher leverage raises interest expense and reduces future acquisition capacity, creating a plausible scenario where acquisitions contribute only 7% rather than 10–12% of annual EBITA growth and organic growth falls from 5% to 3% while margin remains around 9.7% rather than reaching 10.3%, producing EPS growth approximately 5–10 percentage points below the bullish case over 2027–2028 [Cross-ref: Section 9].

Section 2 – Changes in Strategy, Structure and Ambition

Overall strategic direction Asker has not changed its fundamental strategy during the last 12 months, but it has increased the intensity and geographic breadth of execution around its established “twin engine” of organic growth and acquisitions. The strategic message remains consistent across the 2025 annual report, the first-half 2026 report and investor presentations, with management prioritising above-market organic growth, regular acquisitions, decentralised accountability, scale benefits, cash flow and capital efficiency. This consistency supports confidence in the direction of travel, although the earnings outcome increasingly depends on executing several linked initiatives at once.

The strategy is becoming more acquisition-led in absolute terms because the enlarged group now has more capital, more geographic reach and a broader platform from which to source targets. Asker reported more than 50 companies in 19 countries and more than 5,000 employees in 2026, compared with more than 50 companies and over 4,500 employees in the 2025 annual report. That expansion should lift revenue and adjusted earnings before interest, tax and amortisation, but it also increases integration, working-capital, reporting and financing requirements.

The strategic ambition is therefore still high rather than defensive, despite the more difficult financing and valuation backdrop for acquisition compounders. DNB Carnegie’s October 2026 assessment continues to view Asker as capable of growing earnings rapidly without increasing leverage over time, but it identifies failure to expand through acquisitions as a risk that could cause a substantial valuation de-rating. That observation matters for EPS because the company’s growth model requires continued reinvestment of cash flow, leaving less room for dividends or deleveraging while the acquisition programme remains active.

Serial-acquisition model Asker’s acquisition model remains the primary strategic engine for the next three years, with management targeting acquisitions contributing 10–12% of annual adjusted EBITA growth. In the first six months of 2026, management said it was already more than halfway toward that annual acquisition target and continued to see good pipeline visibility. If achieved through 2027–2029, this would increase revenue and EBITA before financing costs, although the effect on reported EPS would depend on purchase multiples, funding costs, contingent consideration and share issuance.

The German GHC and Medicalprodukte Vertrieb Flensburg acquisition added businesses with approximately SEK 300 million of annual revenue and 140 employees, with private-label incontinence and urology products that can potentially be distributed through other Asker companies. The strategic rationale is therefore more than simple consolidation because cross-selling and private-label expansion could raise organic growth and gross margin after the acquisition.

However, the disclosed material does not quantify expected synergies, purchase price, acquired EBITA or the timing of EPS accretion, so the earnings case remains directionally positive but numerically uncertain. 40 expands Asker’s Belgian and Benelux presence in endoscopy, radiology, urology, digital health, artificial intelligence and minimally invasive procedures. Its approximately SEK 170 million of 2025 revenue is small relative to Asker’s group scale, but the strategic value could be greater if the company provides specialist knowledge and access to higher-value hospital technologies.

The EPS effect depends on whether these capabilities generate incremental product sales and service revenue, rather than simply adding acquired revenue at an unchanged margin. 42 illustrates the continuing preference for bolt-ons that can be attached to established platforms rather than operated as entirely new businesses. The Irish distributor, with approximately SEK 73 million of annual revenue and nine employees, is being integrated into HSL Group, where Asker expects cross-selling and geographic expansion opportunities.

The likely financial effect is modest at group level but potentially attractive if shared sales channels and product breadth produce an EBITA margin above the acquired company’s standalone level. 44 45 M2 Pharma Ambercell Solutions adds a proprietary supply-chain management platform for therapy and procedure-specific packs across Lithuania, Latvia and Estonia. This is strategically different from a conventional product distributor because software-enabled logistics and clinical solutions may deepen customer relationships and improve recurring or repeat sales.

The available evidence does not quantify software revenue, implementation costs or profitability, so investors should not capitalise the strategic narrative into EPS until the company reports contribution and cash conversion. 46 extends Asker’s position in Poland through specialised neurosurgical microscopes, operating-room products, hospital-pharmacy workflows and technical maintenance. Management presents the acquisition as a route to exclusive supplier relationships, regional cooperation and a stronger Polish platform. Its approximately SEK 100 million of annual revenue is not material alone, but the platform effect could support further bolt-ons and procurement scale, provided Asker does not overpay for the option value. 48 acquisition programme is not uniformly relentless, because Asker discontinued the proposed Kirstine Hardam transaction by mutual agreement with the owner after a prolonged closing process.

Management described the decision as evidence that it will reject a transaction when the process or terms are not satisfactory, rather than completing deals simply to meet a numerical target. That is strategically constructive and could protect future EPS, although one discontinued deal does not prove that acquisition discipline is consistently superior to competitors.

DNB Carnegie’s October 2026 analysis treats acquisitions as the central determinant of future earnings revisions and models acquired EBITA growth of approximately 7% annually during 2027–2031, below the company’s historical and earlier broker assumptions. The lower assumption reflects the risk that the acquisition pace gradually slows, even though the market remains fragmented. This is a useful test of management’s ambition because the investment case can tolerate slower growth only if organic growth, margins or cash conversion compensate. 52 A former head of mergers and acquisitions at a European healthcare distributor said in an expert interview dated 21 March 2025 that Asker could sustain its acquisition journey for the next two years but warned that the longerterm pool of attractive, scalable targets may be limited.

The expert also questioned whether market share and margins could rise materially beyond the near term because of structural market limitations and competitive dynamics. This view supports the near-term acquisition case but weakens the assumption that the same acquisition intensity can continue unchanged through the entire three-year forecast period. 54 structure and integration The decentralised operating model remains a core strategic choice rather than a temporary response to rapid growth.

Local companies retain responsibility for daily operations, customer relationships and market-specific decisions, while group functions provide common governance, procurement, logistics, information technology, quality and sustainability frameworks. The model can support organic revenue growth because decisions remain close to customers, but it also limits the certainty of cost synergies and makes control quality more dependent on local management.

The new distribution centre in Gothenburg is an important structural project within the operating model. Management said the move and start-up were proceeding according to plan in the second quarter of 2026, with the facility expected to be fully operational by year-end. The investment should eventually improve logistics efficiency and capacity, but start-up expenses and remaining capital expenditure can depress free cash flow before savings appear in EBITA.

DNB Carnegie expects additional start-up costs from the Gothenburg warehouse to pressure Business Area North in the third quarter of 2026, while also identifying potentially greater cost savings as an upside risk. The strategic outcome is therefore asymmetric: successful implementation could improve margins and cash conversion, whereas delays could create costs without the expected operating benefit. Evidence that would change the view would include disclosed warehouse savings, lower distribution costs, improved inventory turns and a sustained margin improvement after the facility becomes fully operational.

Organic growth and operational initiatives Management has not abandoned organic growth in favour of acquisitions, and the first-half 2026 results support a continuing emphasis on existing-company performance. Asker reported 5% organic sales growth in the first quarter and 8% organic adjusted EBITA growth, with management attributing the performance to more customers, a broader product range, efficiency improvements, scale economies and acquisitions made in prior years. This matters because organic growth carries less financing and dilution risk than acquisition-led growth.

The company’s stated ambition is to grow sales slightly faster than the underlying market, which management estimates at approximately 3–4%, and to grow adjusted EBITA faster than sales. Achieving this would require a combination of market-share gains, product expansion, pricing, operating leverage and mix improvement rather than healthcare-market growth alone. The strategic claim is plausible in the near term but should be treated as partly cyclical or execution-dependent until organic growth remains above market through more difficult comparisons.

DNB Carnegie estimates that Asker averaged approximately 6% organic sales growth during 2022–2025, with growth slowing to 3% in 2025 before recovering to approximately 5% in the first half of 2026. The broker argues that future organic growth estimates should be slightly below the historical average and assumes no margin expansion beyond 2026–2027 levels. This provides a more conservative strategic baseline than management’s ambition and implies that future EPS growth must rely significantly on acquisitions.

Asker is also shifting its portfolio toward advanced products, devices and equipment, which management said represented approximately 70% of MedTech activity by September 2026. The shift can support margins because specialist products and clinical support may have greater value-added content than basic distribution, but it may also require more sales specialists, training and working capital. The EPS effect is favourable only if higher gross margins exceed the additional commercial and technical costs.

The 2025 annual report presents partnerships as a route to organic growth because suppliers and healthcare customers can use Asker’s broader product range, local relationships and scale. The report also highlights initiatives involving digital healthcare, automatic monitoring and solutions intended to free time for care providers. These initiatives could increase revenue per customer and improve retention, but the findings do not provide enough financial data to distinguish material growth drivers from strategic case studies.

Aspironix, digital healthcare and solution expansion Aspironix represents an effort to build a more specialised platform within medical products and solutions rather than relying only on country-level distribution companies. The acquisition of Dartin in the Czech Republic and Slovakia was described as a bolt-on to Aspironix, adding neonatal intensive-care equipment, elderly-care patientlifting devices and related service capabilities.

The strategy could raise revenue and margin through specialisation and cross-selling, but the disclosed annual revenue of approximately SEK 46 million makes the direct near-term EPS contribution small. 78 added healthcare software for digital diagnostic imaging, ultrasound reporting and secure data exchange to the HSL platform in the United Kingdom. The acquisition expands Asker’s offering from physical products toward implementation, maintenance and workflow software, potentially increasing customer stickiness and service revenue.

The evidence does not disclose recurring revenue, software margins or integration costs, so the strategic value is clearer than the immediate EPS value. 80 provides another digital and technology-oriented route through its activities in digital health, artificial intelligence and minimally invasive procedures. Management’s rationale is to combine RMS’s clinical expertise with Asker’s broader European offering and support the company’s continued growth.

The potential earnings benefit depends on converting technical capability into higher-value products, new hospital contracts and repeat service income rather than merely broadening the portfolio. 82 ’s own strategic materials also describe automatic monitoring and digital solutions that can improve bedside safety and release care-provider time. Such solutions may support structural demand because healthcare systems face labour shortages, rising care needs and pressure to reduce total costs. Nevertheless, the research does not quantify sales or margins from these initiatives, so they should be treated as optional upside rather than a central EPS assumption over the next 18 months.

Capital allocation, leverage and share count The post-listing capital structure initially increased Asker’s capacity to pursue acquisitions, because the 2025 primary issuance raised approximately SEK 1.5 billion and about SEK 1.2 billion was used to repay debt. The transaction reduced reported leverage and created room for further acquisitions, but it also increased the common share count from approximately 321 million in 2024 to more than 383 million after the listing-related transactions. Consequently, EBITA growth does not translate one-for-one into EPS growth because earnings are spread across a larger number of shares.

The directed set-off issue issued 20,552,600 new shares at SEK 70 per share to repay shareholder loans with accrued interest of approximately SEK 1.439 billion. This reduced shareholder-loan interest and therefore supports profit after financing costs, but the associated dilution reduces the per-share benefit unless the acquired earnings and financing savings exceed the increase in the denominator. The relevant strategic test is therefore adjusted EPS growth, not only adjusted EBITA growth.

Asker has prioritised acquisitions over a high payout while the acquisition runway remains active. DNB Carnegie assumes a payout ratio of approximately 35% during 2026–2031, rising only after acquisition growth slows. Retaining cash can maximise future EPS growth if acquisitions earn attractive returns, but it also increases the cost of a poor acquisition because shareholders forgo distributions while funding integration and contingent consideration.

Leverage is no longer as clearly conservative as management’s headline figures suggest. DNB Carnegie estimated that contingent consideration of SEK 645 million to SEK 1.05 billion and put or call options of SEK 127 million to SEK 134 million could make effective net debt 17–31% higher than reported net debt. If this adjustment is valid, the company has less financial flexibility to fund acquisitions without higher interest expense, which could reduce EPS even if EBITA continues to grow.

Management’s strategic response has been to balance continued acquisitions against leverage discipline rather than accelerate transactions to meet an acquisition expectation. That is a more cautious stance than simply maximising the number of deals and should protect long-term EPS if acquisition returns are uncertain. The key evidence to monitor is whether leverage declines after the Gothenburg investment and earn-out payments, or remains elevated because acquisitions absorb operating cash flow.
