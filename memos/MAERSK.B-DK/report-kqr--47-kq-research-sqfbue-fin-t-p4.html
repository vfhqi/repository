Section 2 – Vessel Depreciation and Useful-Life Extension

Section summary
  1. Maersk's vessel useful-life extension to 25 years provides a USD 700m depreciation holiday in 2026. Net read: The depreciation benefit will decay as incoming dual-fuel newbuilds and terminal capex add fresh charges.
  2. Most major shipping peers already use a 25-year or longer useful-life accounting baseline.
  3. Maersk faces rising goodwill impairment risks in its Logistics segment due to expensive acquisitions.
  4. Vessel book values are temporarily shielded by high secondhand prices but remain vulnerable to rate normalization.
  5. Diminishing Ocean segment impairment headroom increases the risk of abrupt write- downs.
  6. Maersk's impairment testing metrics are sector-compliant, making future impairments dependent on operational execution.

Verification of the accounting adjustment

1.
Verified accounting benefit: Maersk's useful-life extension for vessels from 20 to 25 years took effect on 1 January 2026. This accounting adjustment reduces the annual depreciation expense by approximately USD 700m in 2026. In its interim reporting, Maersk confirmed that this change yielded a USD 175m reduction in Q2 2026 and USD 351m for the first half of the year. This change mechanically inflates operating profit without generating any cash.
2.
Broker consensus view: Bernstein Research notes that Maersk's 2026 EBIT guidance of USD -1.5bn to USD 1.0bn is highly dependent on this accounting change. Without the useful-life extension, the guided EBIT range would have been lower at USD -2.2bn to USD 0.3bn. This means that underlying operating losses would appear far more severe to the market. Consequently, the depreciation holiday serves as a vital cosmetic buffer during the cyclical shipping downturn.

Peer direct comparison

3.
Peer alignment analysis: Deutsche Bank Research notes that Hapag-Lloyd has long depreciated its vessels over an economic useful life of 25 to 35 years. This shows that Maersk's transition to a 25-year policy merely aligns it with standard industry practices. Therefore, Maersk's historical 20-year depreciation policy was unusually conservative compared to its primary alliance partner. This adjustment brings peer accounting methods closer together ahead of their Gemini Cooperation.
4.
Global industry standards: Other major global shipping lines also utilize a 25-year baseline or longer for vessel useful lives. COSCO Shipping Holdings depreciates its container vessels over an anticipated life of 25 years. Similarly, ZIM Integrated Shipping Services typically utilizes a 25-year useful life for its newly built vessels. This uniform industry behavior highlights that Maersk was an outlier prior to 2026.

Consequently, the influx of these advanced vessels will completely reverse the current depreciation benefits by the turn of the decade.

These investments in high-cost alternative fuel technologies will drive up future D&A expenses. Unless green premium pricing is achieved, these investments could drag down future return on invested capital.

This indicates that the depreciation benefit will be quickly overwhelmed by new asset additions.

Obsolescence and contrary views

10.
Contrary evidence: DNB Carnegie argues that vessels aged 20 years and above represent only 13% of the active container fleet. This means the physical opportunity to extend the life of existing older vessels is highly restricted. Furthermore, stricter environmental regulations will heavily penalize older, fuel-inefficient vessels. Therefore, these older ships are highly likely to face functional obsolescence and early scrapping, rendering the 25-year useful life assumption unsustainable.

Consequently, Maersk may be forced to write down or scrap vessels before they reach the new 25-year target.

However, this non-cash buffer will rapidly decay as massive capex additions of USD 10bn to 11bn in 2026-2027 come online. This means that while EPS appears supported in the short term, the underlying cash flows remain severely constrained by capital requirements.

This would allow Maersk to sustain the lower depreciation run-rate without facing premature asset write-downs.

Section 3 – Asset Impairment Risks in Ocean and Logistics

Ocean vessel book values and secondhand markets

1.
Recoverable vessel headroom: Maersk's Ocean segment faces diminishing impairment headroom as vessel book values remain elevated while the shipping cycle peaks. The company reported in its 2025 Annual Report that the Ocean cash-generating unit's impairment headroom dropped to USD 1.1bn. This compares poorly to the headroom of USD 4.1bn recorded in the prior year. Because the major part of this segment's carrying value is comprised of vessels, any severe rate correction in 2027 will immediately threaten reported earnings per share.
2.
Inflated secondhand valuations: Current secondhand vessel values are trading at historically high levels that may quickly correct during an industry downturn. Jefferies Research reported on 18 September 2026 that average prices for ten-year-old 1,100 to 2,800 TEU feeder vessels stood at more than 200% above their average 2015 to 2019 levels. This elevated secondhand pricing is unsustainable unless global shipping capacity constraints remain permanent. Consequently, a sudden normalization of trade routes will deflate asset values and force heavy book-value write-downs, directly harming EPS.
3.
Record-high secondhand indices: Maritime transactional data confirms that container vessel valuations are peaking and could fall sharply if demand softens. MPC Container Ships reported in its Q1 2026 briefing that Clarksons' Secondhand Price Index rose by 2% quarter-on-quarter to reach 84 index points in April 2026. This peak level is the highest recorded since October 2022, indicating a tight vessel market. However, as charter fixtures expire and newbuilds enter the global fleet, these asset values are highly vulnerable to rapid downward adjustments.
4.
Historical precedent warning: Historical container shipping downturns demonstrate that asset write-downs can be sudden and massive. During the previous normalization cycle, China Shipping Development announced a substantial impairment loss on old vessels amounting to Rmb 841m in its 2020 financial results, as reported by Morgan Stanley on 31 January 2021. This precedent shows that when freight rates collapse, shipping lines are forced to adjust their asset carrying values. If Maersk faces a similar rate decline in 2027, the non-cash write-downs will heavily weigh on reported net profit.

Goodwill concentration from logistics acquisitions

5.
Massive acquisition spend: Maersk's aggressive vertical integration strategy has accumulated a massive goodwill balance that is vulnerable to future impairment. Bernstein Research noted on 14 August 2026 that the company has deployed over USD 8bn since 2019 on logistics acquisitions. These acquisitions were intended to build an asset-light integrator, but they also added immense intangible assets to the group's balance sheet. If these businesses fail to cover their high cost of capital during a downturn, substantial goodwill writedowns will severely hit reported earnings.
6.
Concentrated balance sheets: Comparing asset-light logistics players reveals that an acquisitive growth strategy inevitably results in massive goodwill concentration. Bernstein Research notes that DSV's highly acquisitive history has resulted in goodwill accounting for c. DKK 150bn of its DKK 200bn fixed assets. This shows that Maersk's goodwill accumulation is not unique, but rather a structural characteristic of scaled logistics networks. However, unlike DSV, Maersk's lower logistics operating margins make its goodwill balance far more vulnerable to impairment tests.
7.
Peak valuation multiples: High purchase price multiples paid at the top of the shipping cycle increase Maersk's goodwill impairment risks. Regulatory news reports from 14 February 2024 highlight that Maersk acquired LF Logistics for USD 3.6bn in 2022, Pilot Freight Services for USD 1.7bn, and Senator International for USD 644m. Paying peak multiples when freight rates were abnormally high means these assets carry highly inflated book values. Unless these units generate superior organic growth, their carrying value will exceed their cash-generating capability, resulting in mandatory write-downs.
8.
Integration execution failures: Expert insights reveal that Maersk has struggled to successfully integrate its newly acquired logistics businesses. A former head of supply chain development at Maersk stated on 2 January 2026 that acquired entities like Pilot Freight Services, LF Logistics, and Senator International mostly operated as independent businesses post-acquisition. This lack of operational and technological integration has prevented the realization of expected commercial synergies. Consequently, the underperformance of these standalone units increases the probability of goodwill impairment tests failing.
9.
Peer integration strategy: Competitors have chosen different structures for their logistics acquisitions to avoid brand dilution and operational friction. According to CEVA Logistics expert commentary from 28 January 2026, CMA CGM kept CEVA as a neutral, standalone logistics brand rather than rebranding it under the parent container line. Maersk's choice to unify all brands under the single Maersk banner has created integration delays and client friction. This ongoing friction drags down the operating margin of the Logistics segment, heightening the risk of asset write-offs.

Evaluating impairment testing metrics and assumptions

10.
Optimistic Ocean testing: Maersk applies highly sensitive assumptions to justify the carrying value of its Ocean segment assets. The company disclosed in its 2024 Annual Report that its Ocean segment impairment test used a pre-tax discount rate of 9.3% and a terminal growth rate of 2.1%. These parameters are highly sensitive to even minor changes in long-term freight rates and fuel costs. If long-term rates normalize below these assumptions, the headroom will disappear and trigger massive vessel impairments.
11.
Weak logistics returns: The Logistics segment's financial performance continues to run below its cost of capital, threatening goodwill viability. Morgan Stanley reported on 9 September 2025 that Maersk's Logistics segment has not covered its cost of capital because its return on invested capital consistently falls below the group's 7.5% target. In its 2024 Annual Report, Maersk disclosed using an 8.9% pre-tax discount rate and a 2.1% terminal growth rate for this segment's impairment testing. These weak returns indicate that the segment's massive goodwill balance is highly sensitive to further margin compression.
12.
Peer discount rates: Other freight forwarders utilize comparable capital costs and growth rates to test their balance sheet asset values. Nordic Transport Group stated in its 2025 Annual Report that it determines recoverable amounts using discounted cash flow models based on historical data and assumptions of future expected market developments. This alignment indicates that Maersk's discount rate selection is consistent with the broader logistics sector. Therefore, any impairment trigger will be driven by operational earnings failures rather than aggressive accounting assumptions.
13.
Precedent brand write-downs: Recent accounting actions demonstrate Maersk's willingness to execute abrupt write-downs when business models shift. In its 2024 Annual Report, Maersk disclosed that it recognized USD 299m of impairment losses in 2023 due to the retirement of historical brands during its corporate rebranding initiative. This write-down included USD 297m within the Ocean segment and USD 2m in Logistics. This precedent proves that management will adjust book values downward when legacy assets lose their strategic utility.
14.
Peer testing benchmarks: Comparison with logistics peers highlights that Maersk's testing parameters are broadly in line with industry standards. According to its 2024 Annual Report, DSV's goodwill impairment tests assume a stable global economic development with average annual GDP growth of at least 3% and terminal growth in line with GDP. Morgan Stanley's report on 19 June 2026 notes that DSV uses an estimated WACC of 8% and a terminal growth rate of 2.5% for its DCF models. Because Maersk uses comparable assumptions, its impairment risk is driven by operational execution rather than aggressive accounting tricks.
