APM Mini-Memo: Kerry

The one-line view. Kerry is the volume winner of food ingredients priced as a margin loser, and the case depends on margin and cash now following volume.

What the company does

Kerry is a pure taste and nutrition supplier. Kerry is an Irish company that designs and makes flavours, enzymes and nutrition ingredients for food, drink and restaurant companies, and nothing else since the dairy business was sold on 31 December 2024. Continuing revenue was 6,758 million euros in 2025, with the Americas 55% of sales, retail customers 68% and foodservice 32%. Taste technologies are 81% of sales and biotechnology, meaning enzymes, fermentation and cultures, 19%. Kerry holds about 9% of a 70 billion dollar specialty ingredients market, just behind Givaudan at 10%, and leads the world in smoke, grill and dairy tastes. The market value was 13,330 million euros at 98.35 euros on 30 October 2026.

A plausible investment thesis

The story is volume accelerating on real demand. Volume grew 4.1% in the third quarter of 2026, up from 3.5% in the second and above the consensus of 3.6%, with the Americas up 4.6%, while Givaudan's North American taste sales fell 7% in the same quarter. The engine is American recipe renovation: synthetic dyes out, sodium and sugar down, with renovation now over half of Kerry's North American pipeline, more than double a few years earlier, as management said on 8 October 2026. A recipe change sold as a package of ingredients earns far more than a single flavour: a former commercial director puts single flavours below 20% margin and flavour with sugar reduction above 45%.

Volume reaches earnings per share through a fixed cost base and a cost programme. Jefferies says each extra unit of volume earns about twice a company's average margin, and the Accelerate 2.0 programme adds 100 million euros of annual savings by 2028 for 140 million euros of cost. Management raised the 2030 EBITDA margin target, meaning earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, to 20 to 21% from 17.9% in 2025, and consensus has earnings growing 10.3% a year. The market has not paid for this yet: the shares trade at 16.2 times earnings, the 24th percentile of their own ten-year range, and the discount to Givaudan and Symrise of 23 to 32%, depending on the broker, sits at a five-year low.

Four drivers to track: foodservice volume, which is 32% of sales and grew 5% in the second quarter; Americas volume staying above 2%; the margin reaching 18 to 19% in 2026; and cash conversion recovering above 80% from 76% in the first half of 2026.

The Target Setup

No memo exists, so this read is my own. The Target Setup is 'Major CfC Clearing in MQ non-cyclical or better business on Q1 multiple, leading to reversion to normal'. Kerry is a medium-quality business, with margin and return on capital below Givaudan and Symrise, in a non-cyclical end market, and the multiple sits in the cheapest quarter of its own history. The cause for concern is that Kerry wins volume and loses margin, and the first half of 2026 began to clear it: the margin rose 60 basis points to 16.7% and management raised its profitability targets. The American recipe wave adds a demand-driven element to the re-rating.

Case Riskiness and simplicity

The case is simple to state but has two legs, and the second is unproven. Volume is already delivering; what needs to go right is that margin steps up to 18 to 19% in 2026 and cash conversion recovers above 80%, so that earnings per share compound at the high single digits management promises. There are no Bottom-up/Stock-driven ratings, and the weekly Lists judgement of 30 October noted that Kerry's score rests on the environment alone.

The main risk is that the margin gain is a false friend. The research on file finds Kerry the weaker of its peer set on every quality measure, and the sceptical reading is that margin rises by under-investing in research, at 4.6% of sales against 7 to 8% at Givaudan and Symrise, by destocking for cash and by restructuring, rather than by a better product mix. Over half the product mix is judged at severe risk of commoditisation, and the first half of 2026 showed the cash side of the doubt: free cash flow of 262 million euros missed the consensus of 371 million. The trigger to watch is foodservice volume growth below 2% for two consecutive quarters; Morgan Stanley's bear case of 64 euros, at 12 times 2026 earnings of 5.05 euros, is about a third below today's price. The reverse test is just as clear: sustained volume outperformance in North American retail and snack reformulation, with cash conversion recovering, would retire the doubt.

Business quality

Medium quality with real moats. Co-created recipes create switching costs, because changing an ingredient supplier risks the customer's product consistency, and Kerry's ingredients are only 1 to 2% of a customer's cost of goods. Kerry holds over 1,200 patents and patent applications, about 40% of the taste portfolio rests on proprietary fermentation, no customer is more than 5% of revenue, and Kerry won new business with 19 of the top 20 foodservice chains in two years.

The gaps are margin, returns and pricing power. The EBITDA margin of 17.9% in 2025 compares with 23.8% at Givaudan and 21.7% at Symrise, and return on capital of 10.6% with 20 to 25% at Givaudan. Contracts pass raw material costs through automatically, which protects gross margin in inflation but forces price cuts in deflation, as with pricing of minus 1.3% in the first quarter of 2026. Staff turnover of 29% against 10 to 13% at the peers is the under-priced weakness. For predictability, judge Kerry at constant currency: translation is over 95% of earnings sensitivity, and reported earnings per share grew 3% in 2025 against 7.5% at constant currency.

Plausible returns and valuation

Cheap against peers and against its own history. Kerry trades at 16.2 times earnings on 24-month forward earnings per share of 6.07 euros, the 24th percentile of its ten-year range, against Givaudan at 29.1 times and Novonesis at 25.4 times. The weekly TSR/Valuation rating of 30 October is B, a valuation reading only. The weekly routine's plausible return of 46.9% a year rests on the multiple moving to 28 times, the 90th percentile of its history, and I do not lean on it.

My own read is a C to B return. Earnings growth of about 10% a year alone is a D against Richard's bar. A multiple moving to about 20 times over three years, still far below Givaudan, adds about 7 points a year, giving high teens to 20% with dividends, a C to B. An A needs the peer discount to close most of the way, which a large staple on a cheap reading can take years to do. The weekly Sell-side Momentum rating of 28 October is A, as estimates and target prices rose after the first half, and the weekly Technical Momentum rating of 30 October is B, with the shares within 2% of their 52-week high.

Value chain and thematics: the pros and the cons

The pros are two Big sector stories with Kerry at their centre. The Sector note of 25 October judges Kerry the purest way to own American recipe changes and the second purest, after Symrise, way to own the real return of volume in food ingredients. Brokers raised Kerry's 2027 organic growth forecast to 4.3% from 3.8% on volume alone, over 40 countries now mandate front-of-pack nutrition labels, and synthetic Red Dye 3 must be out of American food by January 2027. The weekly Thematic Fit/Momentum rating of 30 October is B, from the environment only.

The cons are timing and price pressure. The American notice rule is not yet binding, with the comment period closing on 9 December 2026 and no final date set, and a Coca-Cola specialist says no major food company is going all in on replacing synthetic colours, which can cost up to ten times as much. Food companies are pressing suppliers on price, the October 2026 rise in oil prices reaches contract prices with a six to twelve month lag, and the proposed Unilever-McCormick merger could bring flavour work in house. The food ingredient suppliers rose 41% over six months and then slowed to 9% over three, so the easy part of the move is done.

In short

  • Interesting because volume is accelerating on real demand: 4.1% in the third quarter of 2026, with the Americas up 4.6% while Givaudan's North American taste sales fell 7%
  • Cheap because the market still prices a margin loser: 16.2 times earnings, the 24th percentile of its own range, and a 23 to 32% discount to peers at a five-year low
  • The case depends on margin and cash following volume: watch the margin reach 18 to 19% in 2026 and cash conversion recover above 80%
  • The main risk is a false friend, margin gained by under-investing in research and destocking rather than mix; foodservice volume below 2% for two quarters is the exit signal
  • The plausible return is high teens to 20% a year, a C to B, from 10% earnings growth and a modest re-rating; my own read, as no memo exists
