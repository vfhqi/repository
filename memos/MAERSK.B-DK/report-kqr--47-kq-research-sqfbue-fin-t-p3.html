Section 1 – The Key Question and Why It Matters

Section summary
  1. Understanding the feedback loops of container shipping determines Maersk's earnings volatility and capital returns over the next three years.
1.
Central question: "which reinforcing or balancing feedback loops are at work in this business model, such that dynamics at play today create additional scope for further positive or negative dynamics in the near and medium term (reinforcing feedback loops), or the opposite (balancing loops)?"
2.
Significant earnings volatility: Maersk's underlying financial performance remains highly sensitive to ocean container freight rates because a small change in rates dramatically shifts profitability. According to Maersk's Q2 2026 press release, a variance of just USD 100 per FFE in container freight rates has an estimated full-year EBIT impact of USD 0.7bn. This immense leverage means that understanding the timing and triggers of feedback loops directly determines whether Maersk's earnings per share can sustain current peaks or collapse into net losses.
3.
Escalating capital commitments: Maersk's feedback loops influence capital allocation decisions because peak- cycle cash flows have historically driven massive, sticky investments in fleet renewal and terminal expansion. While Morgan Stanley notes that Maersk generated over USD 40bn in cumulative free cash flow during the FY21- 22 boom, its annual average free cash flow over ten years is closer to USD 6bn. If supply-side loops prolong the upcoming downturn, Maersk's planned green capex of USD 10bn to 11bn per year will severely strain its medium-term free cash flow.
4.
Constrained capital returns: The resolution of cash buffer and orderbook dynamics will dictate the sustainability of Maersk's shareholder return policies over the next three years. Analysts at DNB Carnegie argue that a betterthan-expected year in 2026 does not automatically translate into expanded share buyback programs for investors. Because Maersk is prioritizing land-based logistics acquisitions and fleet modernization, prolonged downcycle pressure from peer capacity expansion will likely keep buybacks suspended.
5.
Alliance network restructuring: A closely related critical question is whether the newly launched Gemini Cooperation with Hapag-Lloyd can successfully deliver structural cost advantages. Bernstein reports that Gemini aims to achieve over 90% schedule reliability by anchoring its network around strategically located, partner-controlled hub terminals. If successful, this hub-and-spoke model will increase terminal utilization and insulate Maersk from generic freight volatility.
6.
Divergent peer strategies: Maersk's end-to-end integrator strategy stands in stark contrast to the volume-first and asset-light approaches of its primary competitors. A former global freight forwarder president noted that while Maersk integrates vertically, peers like MSC aggressively expand standalone capacity, and Hapag-Lloyd targets regional scale. This strategic divide means that if ocean capacity remains heavily oversupplied, Maersk's highcost integrated model faces severe utilization and margin pressure.
7.
Mounting supply-side risks: The primary threat to Maersk's risk profile over the next 18 months to 3 years is the massive wave of global fleet deliveries. Bernstein warns that global container fleet capacity is projected to grow by 7.4% in 2027 and 13.2% in 2028. Unless operational loops like port congestion and routing disruptions permanently absorb this tonnage, the industry faces a severe and prolonged downcycle.
8.
Other side of the story: Terminal integration could mitigate the negative impacts of a broader container shipping downturn. Morgan Stanley points out that APM Terminals provides a highly stable, inflation-linked cash flow stream that represents approximately 20% of Maersk's group EBITDA. If the Gemini Cooperation successfully concentrates volumes in these owned hubs, Maersk may maintain comfortable profitability even if global ocean rates collapse completely.

Section 3 – Operational Loops: Loss, Withdrawal, and Capacity

Section summary
  1. Global liner operators utilize a structured sequence of operational capacity adjustments to mitigate structural oversupply during downcycles.
  2. Financial resilience and long-term charter backlogs delay aggressive fleet scrapping and prolong market-wide freight rate depressions.

Sequential Capacity Withdrawal Mechanics

1.
Sequential capacity withdrawal: Sequential capacity withdrawal begins with tactical adjustments before moving to permanent fleet reductions. According to Bernstein Research's report of 23 October 2026, carriers first implement blank sailings to manage shortfalls, followed by slow steaming. If rate erosion persists, operators redeliver chartered tonnage, idle owned vessels, and ultimately scrap older ships. This cascading sequence defends spot rates during temporary lulls, but it fails to address structural overcapacity unless permanent ship demolition is accelerated.
2.
Tactical blank sailings: Blank sailings serve as the immediate, short-term mechanism to match weekly slot capacity with fluctuating cargo volumes. An expert call with a former global freight forwarder president on 26 May 2026 explains that alliances publish schedules and then blank specific dates to artificially tighten space. Jefferies Research's note of 27 October 2025 highlights that carriers blanked 11% of Transpacific sailings in October to defend rates, before moderating blanks to 7% in November. This tactical flexibility stabilizes freight rates, which directly protects Maersk's medium-term earnings per share from collapsing.
3.
Systemic slow steaming: Slow steaming absorbs substantial nominal fleet capacity by extending round-trip voyage times across major trade lanes. Carnegie's report of 4 November 2024 notes that global average vessel speeds were reduced to 15 knots in 2023, down from almost 17 knots during the pandemic peak. This speed reduction represents an approximate 10% decline in operational productivity over two years, effectively removing slot capacity from the water. If market conditions worsen, carriers can reduce speeds further to mitigate oversupply, although rising bunker fuel costs limit the efficiency of extreme speed reductions.
4.
Idling and layups: When short-term blanks and speed reductions fail to stabilize rates, carriers physically idle vessels and place them in storage. Deutsche Bank Research's report of 29 September 2026 notes that global idle capacity remained low at 2.2% of the active fleet in September 2026, compared to a peak of 6.0% in mid- 2020. This suggests that carriers keep almost all tonnage active due to temporary route disruptions. Once these disruptions resolve, idle capacity will likely spike as carriers attempt to avert severe cash burns by placing excess tonnage in storage.
5.
Vessel demolition dynamics: Fleet scrapping represents the final, permanent safety valve to correct a structural supply-demand imbalance. DNB Carnegie's report of 4 May 2026 highlights that the historical peak for container vessel recycling occurred in 2016, when approximately 200 vessels were demolished. This historical precedent demonstrates that meaningful fleet demolition only occurs when carriers face severe, prolonged operating deficits. Because the modern fleet has grown 43% since 2019, modern larger vessels will eventually need to be retired to rebalance long-haul trade lanes.

This financial pain was driven by unsustainable freight rate declines, which eventually forced liners to implement strict capacity discipline. This indicates that capacity withdrawals are rarely preemptive and are only triggered once cash balances are depleted.
7.
State-backed price wars: State-supported container lines often exhibit different operational behaviors during downcycles, prioritizing trade flows and market share over immediate profitability. A news report of 23 December 2025 notes that COSCO Shipping is a key maritime arm of China's state-led trade policy, managing nearly 3 million TEU of capacity across 450 vessels. During past price wars, state backing has allowed COSCO to withstand prolonged operational losses without immediately idling tonnage. This state- backed endurance dilutes the effectiveness of industry capacity discipline because private carriers are forced to compete against non-commercial market share preservation.

Because these vessels are leased under long-term, fixed-rate time charters, Seaspan maintains high utilization and stable cash flows even as liner spot rates collapse. Consequently, the operational risk of oversupply is transferred from the shipowners directly to the leasing liner companies.

This deep backlog ensures that GSL's near-term operating cash flows remain robust, even if its liner customers face substantial downturns.

This tonnage redelivery serves as a primary operational transmission channel through which overcapacity pressures are forced back.

12.

Deutsche Bank Research's report of 29 September 2026 projects that global capacity additions will reach 10% in 2027 and 17% in 2028. This massive supply expansion stands in stark contrast to normalized annual demand growth, which is estimated at only 3% to 4%. If stricter emissions rules force the entire global fleet to reduce speeds further, this operational slowdown will effectively destroy enough capacity to insulate liners from severe collapses, altering the expected freight rate trajectory.

Route Disruption Loop

6.
Suez transit recovery: The routing of vessels around the Cape of Good Hope has begun to reverse as major carriers resume Suez Canal transits. Morgan Stanley's report of 5 October 2026 reveals that containership Suez transits surged by 61.9% week-on-week to 752k TEU. This volume represents approximately 54% of the weekly transit levels recorded immediately before the Red Sea disruptions began in late 2023. Unless geopolitical tensions re-escalate, this rapid route normalization will drastically reduce average voyage times and undermine global rate stability.
7.
Fuel burn dynamics: Shorter routing options through the Suez Canal dramatically alter liner operating cost structures and vessel speeds. An executive at a global logistics provider explained in a transcript of 22 September 2026 that Cape of Good Hope diversions previously absorbed 10% of global fleet capacity. The reopening of the Suez Canal allows operators to slow down their vessels while maintaining identical service schedules. Because fuel consumption scales logarithmically with speed, this slow-steaming transition allows liners to slash bunker fuel costs.
8.
Gemini network transition: Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd have initiated a significant structural shift in their alliance operations by routing core services back to Suez. A Maersk press release on 14 September 2026 announced that the Gemini Cooperation is transitioning four major services, including the AE5 and AE11 loops, back to the trans-Suez corridor. These loops were previously diverted around the Cape of Good Hope to avoid security threats. This routing change improves transit times for shippers but also unleashes significant effective capacity back into the Asia-Europe trade lane.
9.
Precedent of persistent disruption: Historical chokepoint disruptions indicate that shipping routing patterns can remain altered long after the initial crisis. Jefferies Research's report of 20 October 2026 reviews the aftermath of the late 2023 Houthi attacks near the Bab el-Mandeb Strait. Despite international military intervention, crude tanker transits through the Suez Canal remained well below pre-attack baselines nearly three years later. This historical precedent suggests that some operators will maintain conservative routing policies for several years, which would cushion the freight market against a rapid capacity glut.
10.
Contrary evidence: Some container lines continue to completely avoid the Red Sea, which limits the speed of global capacity re-entry. Consequently, nearly half of the normal Asia-Europe capacity remains diverted around Africa, preventing a total collapse in freight rates. This split industry behavior suggests that effective capacity will return slower than some bearish forecasts imply.

This strategic integration enables Maersk to synchronize mainliner and shuttle services far more effectively. Bernstein Research's report of 7 May 2026 highlights that Gemini's network structure places reliance on a small number of transshipment gateways. 14.

This margin discount suggests that Maersk may operate these assets at subsidized rates to accommodate its Ocean segment fleet. Consequently, the embedded value of these terminals remains locked and heavily discounted by public equity markets.

This trust deficit will restrict Maersk's logistics segment margins unless the company can provide completely carrierneutral transport services.
