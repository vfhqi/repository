Core Drivers of Investor Returns

2.
Upgraded midterm guidance: Vodafone is raising its targets for the newly integrated UK division to drive mid-term growth. According to Deutsche Bank's report of 8 October 2026, the company upgraded its UK cost-saving target to £1.0 billion by FY32 and expects operating free cash flow to more than triple. This operational tailwind should support Group-level cash flow expansion, which means that adjusted EPS has strong support unless execution failure or intense price competition offsets these integration benefits.
3.
Consolidated growth drivers: African expansion is transforming Vodafone's growth profile through the full consolidation of Safaricom. Vodafone's trading update on 27 July 2026 noted that Safaricom's consolidation will add €1.1 billion to FY27 Adjusted EBITDAaL with an updated Group guidance of €13.0 to €13.3 billion. This consolidation enhances the group's emerging markets presence, but it also elevates currency volatility risk which could negatively affect consolidated adjusted EPS if the Kenyan shilling or South African rand devalues against the euro.
4.
Stable financing profiles: Vodafone maintains a robust debt profile with high fixed-rate exposure to mitigate interest rate volatility. Morgan Stanley's report of 8 October 2026 points out that over 95% of Vodafone Group's debt stack is fixed-rate, reducing interest rate risks. This protection is critical because Vodafone expects leverage to remain managed, so stable interest costs are essential to protect adjusted EPS from refinancing pressures.
5.
Persistent European pressure: European operations face near-term price reversal pressures that could impact adjusted cash flows. Morgan Stanley's note of 14 September 2026 reports that an European Court of Justice ruling reversing the 2023 German fixed price increase is expected to reduce Group free cash flow by 1.5%. This structural headwind complicates the German turnaround story, meaning that adjusted EPS growth could slow if retail cable losses continue to weigh on profitability.
6.
Other side of the story: While management projects double-digit free cash flow growth, structural and regulatory risks in core markets remain prominent. A former marketing director of Vodafone expressed skepticism in an interview on 9 September 2026, arguing that the exit from Italy and Spain reflects structural failures rather than proactive strategic right-sizing. These historic missteps and high German cable competition indicate that valuation multiples could compress if structural headwinds outpace synergies.

Robust operating performance: Vodafone Group reported an adjusted EBITDAaL of €11,351 million for FY26, establishing a strong operational baseline for the year. To reconcile this to adjusted operating profit, Vodafone deducted adjusted depreciation and amortisation on owned assets of €7,796 million. It then added a positive adjusted share of results of equity-accounted associates and joint ventures of €218 million. This solid starting point demonstrates that underlying EBITDAaL remains the primary driver of the group's adjusted earnings profile.

The group recorded interest on lease liabilities of €615 million, which represents a significant ongoing financing commitment. Additionally, a gain of €199 million on the disposal of property, plant, and equipment and intangible assets was recognized in the adjusted reconciliation. These adjustments culminate in an adjusted operating profit of €4,587 million for FY26.
3.

This rising leverage highlights that cash outflows from acquisitions, dividends, and spectrum licences outweighed disposal inflows, meaning that balance sheet deleveraging is progressing slower than management anticipated.

Substantial financing expenses: Vodafone's financing and investment lines significantly shaped the transition from operating profit to pretax income. The group reported adjusted net financing costs of €819 million, which included adjusted gross financing costs of €2,214 million. This was partially offset by a substantial investment and other income of €1,395 million. Consequently, the group successfully generated an adjusted profit before taxation of €3,768 million for the fiscal year.

Manageable tax burden: Vodafone applied an adjusted income tax expense of €998 million to its pre-tax earnings to determine continuing profits. This adjusted tax expense reflects a reported income tax charge of €1,805 million offset by €807 million in adjustments. This calculation resulted in an adjusted profit of €2,770 million from continuing operations. This tax level implies that tax optimization was crucial in defending the group's bottom-line profitability during a transitional year.

Lower minority claims: Vodafone reduced its continuing adjusted profit by deducting adjusted non-controlling interests of €194 million to isolate parent earnings. This deduction led to an adjusted profit attributable to owners of the parent of €2,576 million for the total group. When divided by the basic weighted average share count of 24,033 million, the group reported adjusted basic EPS of 10.72 eurocents. Non-Controlling Interest Dynamics

7.
Structural asset changes: Vodafone's adjusted non-controlling interests fell from €423 million in FY25 to €194 million in FY26, primarily driven by portfolio restructuring. The complete deconsolidation of Vodafone Spain and Vodafone Italy structurally removed the minority profit shares associated with these underperforming European assets. Because these operations were historically highly capital-intensive, their removal helped simplify the group's minority exposure profile, which sustainably lowers the annual NCI drag on parent earnings.
8.
Temporary transaction effects: Vodafone's reported non-controlling interests included massive non-recurring accounting impacts from the UK merger that did not affect adjusted NCI. The group recognized €1,208 million in reported non-controlling interests and a €603 million net gain in retained earnings from giving up a 49% interest in Vodafone UK. An additional €348 million was recognized for Hutchison's proportionate equity funding contribution. These one-off accounting items are isolated as non-recurring elements to ensure adjusted NCI accurately reflects core operations.
9.
Incremental partner funding: Vodafone experienced minor offsetting movements in its non-controlling interest lines through strategic commercial partnerships. The group received €125 million from Accenture Holdings B.V. for a non-controlling interest in Vodafone Shared Operations Limited in FY26, up from €25 million in FY25. While this commercialization adds a steady cash inflow, it represents a non-recurring equity-level transaction that does not fundamentally alter the long-term halving of adjusted NCI.
10.
Strategic debt reductions: Vodafone's cash inflows from discontinued operations further supported its balance sheet and reduced financing pressures. Net debt rose by €3,014 million to €25,411 million, but was offset by €1,212 million in other movements. This offset was principally driven by €974 million in cash from the settlement of redeemable preference shares provided by Zegona Communications as part of the Spain disposal. This capital return will sustainably reduce net financing costs by providing immediate liquidity.
11.
Unique minority structures: Vodafone's adjusted NCI halving stands out as a unique structural event because peer operators have more stable ownership setups. A direct comparison of adjusted NCI development with named peers like Deutsche Telekom or Orange is not available in the cited broker reports. However, Vodafone's aggressive asset deconsolidation in Spain and Italy remains unmatched in scale within the sector. This means Vodafone’s minority drag reduction is highly idiosyncratic rather than a reflection of broader European telecom trends.

Cash Flow and Capital Allocation Impact

12.
Favorable cash turnaround: Morgan Stanley reports that Vodafone generated an adjusted free cash flow of €2,621 million in FY26, representing 3% year-on-year growth. This recovery follows nearly zero growth over the previous two years and a cumulative 50% decline over the prior four years. The broker expects adjusted free cash flow to grow by 8% annually on average over the next three years. This positive trajectory suggests that adjusted EPS growth is well-supported by structural improvements.
13.
Delayed shareholder rewards: Deutsche Bank Research notes that the deconsolidation of Spain and Italy triggered a cut to Vodafone's free cash flow and dividend. However, the broker highlights that €4 billion of planned buybacks will be delayed until May 2026 or May 2027. This delay has temporarily limited the accretion to Vodafone's EPS and cash flow per share. Consequently, the full impact on Vodafone's EPS remains delayed despite the execution of these major asset sales.
14.
Other side of the story: While the line-by-line reconciliation shows a clean adjusted EPS of 10.72 eurocents, the underlying quality of these earnings is masked by continuous restructuring and adjustive adjustments. If we analyze the reported total Group loss attributable to owners of €397 million, the true financial health appears much weaker than the adjusted figures suggest. This discrepancy implies that adjusted EPS significantly overstates sustainable profitability unless Vodafone can rapidly halt restructuring costs.

German Broadband and Fixed-Line Turnaround

1.
Persistent broadband attrition: Vodafone Germany faces substantial fixed-line customer losses despite maintaining its position as the largest gigabit connectivity provider in the country. The company reported a net loss of 202,000 fixed-line customers in FY26, which includes a loss of 136,000 customers on its gigabit network. This persistent decline is driven by management's focus on value optimization rather than promotional volume, which means that earnings per share will remain under pressure unless customer losses stabilize.
2.
Accelerating fiber partnerships: Vodafone Germany is expanding its high-speed footprint beyond its traditional cable network through joint ventures and wholesale agreements. The group's 50% owned joint venture, OXG Glasfaser, successfully passed 840,000 fiber households in Q1 FY27. In May 2026, Vodafone further expanded this reach by signing a strategic wholesale deal with Deutsche GigaNetz to market speeds to an additional 500,000 homes, aiming for up to one million homes by 2030. This hybrid network strategy should mitigate legacy cable churn and support long-term broadband ARPU.
3.
Contrary evidence: Deutsche Bank Research's report of 19 October 2026 highlights that alternative network operators stepped up fiber deployment, passing 2.8 million German homes in 2025 compared to Deutsche Telekom's 2.5 million. This rapid alt-net build-out has steadily eroded the infrastructure market share of both incumbents, which means that Vodafone's retail market position faces threat as alternative networks achieve higher take-up rates.

The National Roaming Agreement with 1&1

4.
Lucrative wholesale revenues: Vodafone's long-term national roaming agreement with 1&1 provides an important cash cushion during its European transition. This 18-year partnership generates approximately €400 million in annual wholesale revenues for carrying 1&1's mobile traffic. Deutsche Bank's note of 4 August 2023 estimated this agreement delivers a high-margin EBITDAaL contribution of €300 million initially, which means that wholesale cash flows will protect EPS before 1&1 transitions fully to its own network.
5.
Elevated wholesale friction: The capacity-based pricing model under the national roaming agreement has introduced financial volatility for both partners. In its interim report of 6 August 2026, 1&1 noted that its wholesale costs rose because Vodafone's network expansion was slower than anticipated. This slower growth increased 1&1's capacity usage share, meaning that EBITDA volatility could rise for Vodafone if traffic patterns deviate from forecasts.
6.
Imminent migration risks: Vodafone faces the potential loss of 1&1 as a wholesale customer to competitor networks. Deutsche Bank Research's note of 21 April 2026 suggests that a full migration of 1&1 to Telefónica would eliminate €400 million in wholesale revenues, though this would be offset by a €100 million reduction in capital expenditure. This transition would take up to two years, which means that near-term earnings are relatively protected despite the structural headwind.
