Management and Business Quality Memo · Xvivo Perfusion (XVIVO-SE) · Investment Analyst · 4 November 2026 · price SEK 201.0 (close 4-Nov-26), market value about SEK 6.3 billion · written from the Stock-specific Investment Case A&J Memo of 26-Oct-26, the four Family-Query reports, the Earnings Reviews, 21 Key Question reports, Richard's own notes and calls (Nov-25 to Oct-26) and public registries; four further Key Question reports were commissioned today and will be folded into a dated second version

Xvivo Perfusion: a good scientific company run by a careful steward, with a commercial organisation that is not yet good enough for the strategy it has chosen

Richard's brief (4-Nov-26), word for word: "I want to see a deep dive on the quality of the management, goals, strategic priorities, strategy, choices, execution, standards, culture, records, delivery, and the company's competitive position and competitive advantages in lung, heart, liver and kidney"; the purpose: "me better understanding the subtle nuances regarding whether *this* management team and *this* business is likely to deliver a better-than-expected performance and forward guidance over the coming ~12 months than consensus expects, or vice versa; to be able to try to intuit the subtleties between an okay, a good, and a great management/business combination". His steer on the answers: "Dont over quantify it. This is a qualitative judgement exercise"; "I only want the topic I asked about covered - the management's actions/choices etc etc and the interlinkage with the business's strengths/weaknesses". His own prior, from his results notes of 30-Oct-26: "It's a sub-scale business, with mediocre quality leadership. With good product potential. It needs better management, better investment. It has neither"; and from January: "legacy moat, not reinvestment moat". The memo tests that prior rather than assuming it.

1. The answer

  • The placing: an okay combination, made of a good scientific and regulatory organisation and a weak commercial and organisational one, run by a careful, honest steward who is not a builder. Judged against the organ-perfusion peers and the Nordic growth medtechs the memo uses, Xvivo's management is above the middle on what it does with evidence, regulators and cash (the heart trials, the CE mark, the FDA filing, the liver re-route, the 2025 cost programme) and below the middle on what it does with people, customers and dates in the United States (the services unit, the kidney slip, the empty finance seat, the lost surgeons, a decade of being out-sold by TransMedics). Richard's "mediocre quality leadership" is right about the second half and too harsh on the first; the subtlety is that the business's value over the next three years is created by the first half and harvested by the second.
  • The business's strengths are inherited and scientific; its weaknesses are organisational and chosen. The lung franchise (Perfadex Plus at about 90% of static lung preservation, patents to 2035, thoracic product margins of 80% to 85%), the clinical record (the only randomised heart trial linked to survival; the United States heart trial enrolled five months early) and the balance sheet (net cash about SEK 193 million, no dividend, no buyback) were built by the founder's generation and the scientists; this management has kept them intact and added the heart's evidence base. The weaknesses are the products of this management's choices: a services unit bought in 2021 that has lost money for six quarters and whose gross margin is now minus 19%; a United States kidney launch that slipped two years on a component; a chief financial officer moved to run the United States turnaround while his own seat stays empty; employee commitment that halved in the year the strategy was most ambitious.
  • The interlinkage that matters most: the lung franchise pays for everything, so nothing forces pace. A near-monopoly consumable with 80%-plus margins funds three launches, a loss-making services node and about SEK 150 million a year of capitalised development without a capital raise, and it makes the company's culture (consensus, evidence first, "we are not a logistics company") affordable. The same culture produces the clinical quality that wins approvals and the commercial slowness that loses customers. The one customer that left (Lung Bioengineering, building its own platform) is the first time the market has charged the company for that slowness inside the lung franchise itself.
  • Goals and ambition are large in words and modest in resources, and the targets are not managed. "Nobody should die waiting for an organ", a market "100 times larger", "standard of care for all donor hearts", the 2027 targets of a 30% EBITDA margin and 20% EBIT margin: all were set or restated while the company said it "cannot invest the cost base much" (Nov-25), then "will aggressively invest" (Oct-26). The 2027 targets were reaffirmed in July 2026 after a margin miss and again in October 2026 with both brokers who model the company below them. This is an honest company that does not manage expectations, which is a different thing from a conservative one.
  • Competitive position by organ: dominant and defended in lung fluids; strong in evidence and unproven in the market in heart; leading and being commoditised in European liver; a challenger in kidney; structurally weak in services. The advantage widens where Xvivo's science is the product (fluids, the heart's data) and narrows wherever a device, a service or a price is the product (devices in lung and liver, logistics everywhere, abdominal pricing). The European heart rollout is the test of whether this management can turn evidence into customers at pace: on the registries, Tier 2's "about 30 centres" in France, the Netherlands, Austria and the United Kingdom is about 38 programmes and about 790 transplants a year, and France, the one reimbursed market, machine-perfused 13 of its 414 hearts in 2024.
  • Over the next twelve months the management-controlled items point to the company doing what it says operationally and not what it targets financially: the clinical and regulatory dates are now in others' hands (the FDA; four reimbursement authorities), the commercial dates are in management's hands and have slipped before, and the 2027 targets are more likely to be re-dated than met. The counter-case, given in full in section 10, is that a company which has just landed its two hardest milestones on the second attempt, restored cash generation on command and refreshed its board with two United States commercial operators is a good management mid-transition rather than an okay one, and that the twelve months to November 2027 are when that shows.

2. The people

The chief executive

Christoffer Rosenblad is a ten-year insider promoted in a crisis, with the profile of an operator-steward rather than a founder or a commercial builder. He joined Xvivo in 2012 after corporate finance and controlling roles (Ciba Vision Nordic, LG Electronics, Novartis), was chief financial officer for eight years, ran the United States operations from 2015 to 2017 as chief operating officer and business development director, and was deputy chief executive from 2017; he had presented on 33 quarterly calls before his first as chief executive. He became acting chief executive on 27 September 2022, the day the founder-era chief executive Dag Andersson died suddenly, and permanent chief executive on 30 November 2022 after a structured search. His first act was to confirm that the 2023 to 2027 strategy stayed intact (Leadership report, July 2026).

What the record shows him doing well. Revenue doubled from SEK 415 million in 2022 to SEK 822 million in 2024; the United States heart trial (PRESERVE, 141 patients) enrolled its last patient five months ahead of schedule and met its endpoints; the CE mark and the FDA's receipt of the premarket approval application both landed in 2026; the liver route was re-cut from a 215-patient trial to a 510(k) submission; a cost and cash programme in the second half of 2025 produced the company's first positive quarterly cash flow and a record 25% EBITDA margin in the fourth quarter of 2025; rolling forecasts replaced annual budgets. His communication is honest: he announced the heart delay before he had to, warned investors not to extrapolate the 25% quarter ("guided to a 20% baseline"), and disclosed the SEK 19 million of timing items in the third quarter of 2026 unprompted (the memo, 26-Oct-26; Earnings Review, 30-Oct-26). Experts describe him as "highly competent", "thoughtful and cautious", collaborative with surgeons where TransMedics' founder is "exceptionally pushy" (Leadership report).

What the record shows him not doing. He has no chief executive or turnaround record before this one; former employees say he "lacks the deep visionary pull of the founder" and acts as "an operational steward" (Business Foundation report, June 2026; Leadership report). Former directors say his team "was late to scale US perfusion services", and United States clinicians say he approaches that market "too slowly compared to domestic medical technology rivals" (Leadership report). The decisions that define his tenure's weak side are his: the halt of new United States service contracts for six months to fix logistics; keeping the services unit as "launch infrastructure" rather than exiting it; accepting a two-year slip in United States kidney on a component change; and moving his chief financial officer into the United States turnaround with no successor named. Richard's own impression after two calls is blunter than the reports': "CEO not much better" than a chief financial officer he rated "bottom quartile", a "poorly executed conference call", "confusing guidance + commentary, as normal" (his case file, 17-Jan-26). The sell-side has restored its confidence in him since the 2025 slowdown; the Leadership report's own counter-view is that this is "late-stage consensus tracking".

Skin in the game is thin. He holds 53,523 shares (about SEK 11 million at today's price) and 39,000 performance share rights; the chief financial officer holds 3,700 shares; the chairman 8,000. Pay is modest and moved the right way: total remuneration fell from SEK 7.38 million in 2024 to SEK 6.01 million in 2025 as variable pay fell 47% on missed targets, and the 2022 to 2025 warrant programme expired with nothing exercised. Alignment through downside, not through ownership.

The finance seat, and who runs the United States

The most revealing single decision of 2026 is the chief financial officer's move. Kristoffer Nordström (chief financial officer since 2020, ten years at KPMG, "a very good, highly knowledgeable guy" to experts, "exceptionally clear financial presentations") became senior vice president for corporate strategy and business development on 1 April 2026 and took direct charge of the United States services organisation, while keeping the chief financial officer's duties until a successor is found; the external search has run since February 2026 and, nine months on, nobody has been named (Leadership report; Earnings Review, 30-Oct-26). Read charitably, the board put its most trusted executive on its worst problem. Read sceptically, a 207-person company launching three organs in its most capital-intensive year chose to run finance, strategy, business development and a United States turnaround through one person, and its bench was too thin to do otherwise. Richard's reading of the same man in January, "CFO is poor, bottom quartile", "Not performing his job well" (7-Nov-25), sits oddly with the experts' praise; the reconciliation I find most likely is that he is a good controller and presenter and an unproven operator, which is exactly the job he has now been given. The Leadership report calls the permanent succession "the single most important execution risk over the next 12 months"; the Risks report (July 2026) files it as a failure mode for cost control; RedEye calls it "a succession vacancy during a critical commercial ramp" and DNB Carnegie "a minor governance issue", which is the sell-side's usual split on this company: one house reads the organisation, the other reads the products.

The United States has no named commercial head in any file. The chief executive told Richard in November 2025 that "the USA is sufficiently complicated that the US needs a CEO", that the board was "on board with that judgement", and that "some of the salesforce were not good + not working well. We changed that"; the January call confirmed the board "agreed need to change US leadership" but had not done it (Richard's notes, 7-Nov-25 and 17-Jan-26). The Leadership report lists Mark Reade as North American commercial lead and says former employees report that "the North American commercial leadership underperformed and was subsequently let go". The field force was reorganised toward the South and West in 2025 and 2026, with benefits "over five quarters". The Earnings Review of 30-Oct-26 still records "the absence of a disclosed US commercial successor". For a company whose strategy is now "US-led", that is the organisation chart's largest hole, and it has been open for a year.

The wider team, the board and the owners

  • The senior team is long-tenured and clinically credible: Lena Hagman (deputy chief executive and chief operating officer, ex-Getinge, joined 2022, deputy since November 2024, the natural internal successor); Johan Holmström (Europe and rest-of-world commercial, ex-Permobil); Jaya Tiwari (clinical and regulatory affairs, North America, since 2015); Charlotte Walldal (research and development, ex-Essity, since 2020); Andreas Wallinder (chief medical officer, PhD). The team "underwent minimal structural disruption over the last 18 months" (Leadership report). The departure that mattered was earlier: the former head of research left after the founder-era chief executive's death "because Andersson's visionary pull was lost". Richard's January concern, "Concerned re. NPD given Steen left + no NPD roadmap knowledge", is not answered in any file; the commissioned management report asks for it.
  • The board is clean, long-serving at the top and newly commercial at the edges. Seven members, six independent of the company; chairman Gösta Johannesson since 2013, dependent on Bure Equity (14.27%, the largest owner; the Fourth Swedish National Pension Fund (AP4) 9.05%; Eccenovo 5.70%); audit committee chaired by a former chief financial officer (Camilla Öberg, Yubico) with a professor of cardiothoracic surgery beside her; no internal audit function; a whistleblower system with zero cases in four years, which the Leadership report calls "statistically improbable". The April 2026 annual general meeting brought in Emil Billbäck (former chief executive of Bonesupport, the Nordic medtech that is currently beating and raising) and Anne-Karen Hunt (GE Healthcare, Demant), and two long-serving directors left. The board's guidance to the chief executive is "partnerships not M&A" (Richard's notes, 7-Nov-25), and the chief executive sends takeover approaches to the chairman.
  • Incentives point at volume and shareholder return, with a recent loosening. Variable cash is tied to annual sales and EBITDA; the long-term plans pay 37.5% at 8% annual total shareholder return, 75% at 12%, and the remaining 25% on perfusion-volume growth of at least 12% a year (added in 2026, "twice the market"); the cash cap was raised to 60% of base salary for the chief executive on a benchmark study, which the Leadership report reads as a risk of short-term revenue-chasing. The 2026 programme is built to retain about 40 managers and key staff. There is no return-on-capital condition anywhere in the plans.

3. Goals and ambition

The stated goals are a purpose, a market claim, three product promises and three financial targets. The purpose is "nobody should die waiting for a new organ", repeated in every report and call. The market claim is that demand is about ten times supply and that converting static storage to machine perfusion is "a market opportunity 100 times larger than current levels" (Leadership report). The product promises are the heart ("standard of care for all donor hearts"; a European launch, then a United States launch "targeted" for the end of 2027), the liver (a United States 510(k) submission in the first half of 2027) and the kidney (a broad United States launch in 2027, after the 2025 and 2026 dates moved). The financial targets are the 2027 targets.

The ambition is real and the resourcing is not sized to it. On the November 2025 call the chief executive told Richard the company "cannot invest the cost base much", "will not do that", would "re-allocate resources" and be "cost conscious", while describing "the US huge investment need". Eleven months later the third-quarter 2026 report says the company "will aggressively invest in US commercial capabilities for heart and liver", with about 10 to 13 United States commercial hires, a surgical roster hired ahead of volume and headcount up from 183 to 207 in a year (Earnings Review, 30-Oct-26; Key Question report on operating leverage, 23-Oct-26). Both statements were sincere; what changed in between was the CE mark and the FDA filing. The reading I take is that this management sets ambition by the science's timetable and resources by the cash flow's, and reconciles the two only when a milestone forces it. That is why the company could run a cost programme in late 2025 and reverse it in 2026 without either being a change of mind.

The 2027 targets show how goals are handled. An EBITDA margin above 30%, an EBIT margin above 20% and a 70% abdominal gross margin were in place, as far as the files show, by 2024 (the abdominal target is on the January 2024 call and DNB Carnegie cites the set in April 2024), before the services unit turned negative, before the largest lung customer's platform was approved and before the CE-mark amortisation (SEK 39 million a year) was known. They were reaffirmed in July 2026 after a 6% EBITDA miss and restated at the third quarter with the abdominal margin at 61% and services at minus 19%; the two brokers whose models are available (DNB Carnegie and RedEye) are at 25.0% and 23.4% for 2027, and RedEye "explicitly projects that Xvivo will fall short" because the launch infrastructure depresses profitability until United States heart and liver scale in late 2028 (Key Question report memo-own-3, 25-Oct-26). The company publishes a consensus compilation before each report, so it can see the gap. Management's own framing has quietly moved: 2027 is now "a financial springboard for a highly profitable and exciting fiscal year 2028" (third-quarter report), which is the language of a target being re-dated without being withdrawn. The memo put the probability of a miss above 90% and of a re-dating by July 2027 at about 85%; I would not quarrel with either. A company that gives no numerical guidance "and never has" (the memo) has only its targets as a promise, and it has chosen to keep a promise it cannot keep rather than reset it. That is the single clearest management-quality signal in the file, and it is a negative one: not dishonesty, but a reluctance to disappoint that ends up disappointing.

Richard's test on the heart ambitions. His note that "25 heart centres by 2027 in Europe and 90 in US by 2029 are madness" refers to the Financial Analyst's model assumptions behind the memo, not to company targets; the company's own plan is the three tiers (section 8). On the European count the model and the company's Tier 2 are of the same order (25 live against "about 30 prioritised"); what the registries say about the pace is in section 8, and it supports Richard's instinct on timing more than on the count. The 90 United States centres in 2029 is a model assumption for a launch year and is not something management has said; it belongs to the Financial Analyst's next update, not to this memo.

4. Strategic priorities and strategy

The strategy has one spine and three bets. The spine is to be the only company with a perfusion product for every transplantable organ ("98% of all transplantable organs", DNB Carnegie), built by acquisition (Organ Assist in 2020 for liver and kidney; STAR Teams in November 2021 for United States recovery services; Avionord in 2022 for Italy; FlowHawk in late 2024 for workflow software) and by the company's own clinical development (the heart). The three bets are: first, that evidence wins adoption (fund the trials, publish in The Lancet and the European Heart Journal, price at a third of the incumbent, let surgeons choose); second, that an asset-light route to market beats TransMedics' owned fleet (five aviation partners, 175 perfusionists through Perfusion Solution Inc, OPO hubs rather than hospital-by-hospital selling, "we are not a logistics company"); third, that the United States is where the value is and that it must be led from there ("US-led transplant platform", Earnings Review 30-Oct-26).

How the priorities moved, by year. 2023: integrate the acquisitions and run the trials. 2024: scale lung in the United States (revenue +39% organic, the EBITDA margin from 14% to 22%). 2025: survive the customer's destocking and the heart delay with a cost and cash programme, restructure the United States field force, sign the perfusionist partnership, start the hub model. 2026: launch the heart in Europe, file in the United States, re-route the liver, build the United States commercial organisation, consolidate abdominal manufacturing in Sweden, finish the tenfold capacity project, and keep the services unit as the carrier of the hubs and the heart. The third-quarter 2026 strategy slide added "building for scale and a robust supply chain" as a named focus area, which the Earnings Review reads as ambition "ahead of demonstrated productivity".

Judgement on the strategy itself. The spine is right for a company of this size: an all-organ consumables platform is what an acquirer pays 11 to 20 times sales for (Getinge for Paragonix, Terumo for OrganOx) and what a hospital's procurement prefers over four vendors. The evidence bet is right and is the company's real competence. The asset-light bet is unproven and is the one experts doubt most: a former head of sales for the Americas says that "unless Xvivo builds an equivalent transport infrastructure, it may struggle to capture market share" (17-Mar-26), and the Leadership report notes TransMedics' "bundled concierge logistics model remains an un-replicated competitive threat"; the hub model is the company's answer and its throughput is undisclosed. The United States bet is right in direction and is the one the organisation has been least able to execute. The strategy, in short, is better than the organisation that has to deliver it, and the gap between the two is the subject of this memo.

What the pattern of choices says. Every choice that depends on evidence, regulators or cash has been made well or at least sensibly. Every choice that depends on building or running a commercial organisation in the United States has been made late, kept too long, or staffed too thinly. The board's guidance ("partnerships not M&A") and the chief executive's temperament point the same way: this is a company that would rather partner, license and persuade than hire, own and push. Against TransMedics that is a disadvantage; against a surgeon choosing a preservation fluid it is not.

7. Standards and culture

  • Honesty is high; the standard of promise-keeping is not. The reports agree on an "honest-tracker" archetype (Change Forces report, Sep-26): the company took the 2025 miss "cleanly without obfuscation", "has not been found to artificially massage numbers", discloses timing items unprompted and warns against extrapolation. The same archetype "refuses to provide hard numerical guidance" and keeps targets it will not meet. DNB Carnegie's line that "regulatory optimism frequently outpaces actual clearance timelines" (6-Oct-25) describes the standard: truthful about the present, optimistic about dates. One number cuts against the honesty record: the chief executive put the CE-mark amortisation at "around SEK 25 million annually" on the July 2026 call and the audited figure three months later was SEK 39 million, a third higher, which the brokers had copied into their models (Key Question report on amortisation and tax, Oct-26). Carelessness with a disclosed figure, not concealment, but it is the kind of carelessness a strong finance function does not allow, and the finance function was by then one person doing two jobs.
  • Employee commitment halved in the year the strategy was most stretched. The employee net promoter score fell from 51 to 27 in 2025 and the commitment index from 4.16 to 4.04, with participation steady at 84%; the Change Forces report calls a 47% single-year fall "outside the 10 to 15 point band typical for Nordic medtech" and "historically a leading indicator of senior departures". Twenty-seven is still inside the favourable Swedish band, and no 2026 figure exists yet; the 2026 incentive programme for about 40 key people is the company's answer. The score fell in the year of the destocking, the heart delay, the cost programme and the United States field-force changes, which is to say in the year the culture was asked to do something other than what it is good at.
  • A Swedish consensus culture selling into an American market. Experts describe "trust, participation, and individual responsibility", a chief executive who is "user-friendly" with surgeons, and a company "widely seen as more user-friendly than TransMedics' notoriously aggressive culture"; the same experts say the consensus model "may prevent rapid operational course-correction, allowing aggressive US competitors to poach clinical staff" (Leadership report), and that the company "lost key transplant surgeons to rivals because it failed to support regional logistics". The culture is the moat in Europe and the gap in the United States.
  • Governance standards are high on paper and untested in practice. One share class, clean audits, independent directors, pay that fell on missed targets, warrants that expired worthless, no related-party dealings, a code of conduct signed by every senior manager; against that, no internal audit, a chairman dependent on the largest owner for thirteen years, thin insider ownership, and a zero-incident whistleblower record that may mean reporting friction rather than virtue.
  • Disclosure is above average for a Swedish small cap and stops exactly where it would be most useful. Segment revenue, procedure volumes and geographic splits are given; hub throughput, the customer's contract terms, the cost of each launch, the amortisation each approval triggers and the tax rate are not (the memo lists the last four as the numbers that have moved earnings per share more than the milestones in each of the last three years).

8. Competitive position and advantage, organ by organ

Judged on evidence, product, price, installed base, route to market and where share is moving; lung and heart carry the depth because they carry the value.

Lung: dominant in fluids, contested in devices, with the first crack showing

Position. Perfadex Plus holds about 90% of static lung preservation worldwide and is used by over 90% of lung programmes, with patent cover to 2035; STEEN Solution and the XPS are the only established warm lung-perfusion platform; thoracic product gross margins of 80% to 85% held through 2023's inflation and are 80% in the third quarter of 2026 after write-downs (85% without them). A chief fellow calls STEEN and Perfadex "the unrivaled leader"; a Duke surgical director calls STEEN "surprisingly expensive and lacks clinical competition" (Key Question reports, 20-May-26 and 9-Apr-26). This is the franchise Richard called a "legacy moat", and he is right that it was built by the scientists; it is also the one place where the company's pricing has held (the November 2025 increase passed tariffs through) and where its clinical culture is the sales force.

Where it is contested. EVLP penetration in the United States has been stuck at 10% to 20% of lung transplants for a decade on a USD 40,000 run cost; hospitals break the XPS into pumps and ventilators and build their own circuits (UPMC, Mass General) "but still buy Xvivo solutions"; Traferox's TorEx and X°Port target the XPS's usability; a Canadian surgeon claims TorEx achieves "a 70% clinical success rate compared to only 20% to 30% for Xvivo's XPS" (a single expert, 4-Sep-26); Toronto General has its own version of Perfadex; TransMedics' CHOPS cold system and its lung trial attack the category from above. The largest customer, Lung Bioengineering, won approval for its own EVLP platform on 29 June 2026 after 1,100 procedures on Xvivo's, built about SEK 10 million of inventory in the third quarter, and "will decline" from SEK 132 million of 2025 revenue by an amount the company has not disclosed; it keeps buying the fluids. The hub model is the company's answer and is working on the one measure disclosed (disposables +183% in the third quarter; OneLegacy's hub open to over 70 centres); its throughput is not disclosed, one OPO that bought an XPS could not sustain a programme (LifeShare Oklahoma, which "could not hire dedicated personnel and lacked sufficient procedure volume"), and a former general manager of Xvivo North America said in March 2026 that the perfusionist agreement covered "only" Indiana, so each new hub is a new negotiation. The company "doubled the surgeon roster" in 2025 and has kept it through the volume dips "to preserve clinical readiness" (third-quarter 2024 report; fourth-quarter 2025 call), which is the cost side of the same choice.

Advantage trend. Stable in fluids; widening in the hubs if they deliver; narrowing in devices and in the customer relationship. The management link: the fluids need no commercial excellence and get none; the hubs are the first commercial design the company has produced that fits its culture (a partnership, a protocol, a clinical specialist on site), which is why they work and services did not.

Heart: the best evidence in the category, the weakest commercial machine, and a rollout whose pace the registries can test

Position. Heart Assist Transport carries the only randomised heart preservation trial linked to survival (NIHP2019: 204 patients, 15 institutions, 8 countries; 92% against 86% one-year survival; a 76% reduction in severe primary graft dysfunction) and a single-arm United States trial that met its endpoints (PRESERVE; 92.1% 30-day success); the premarket approval application was received on 28 September 2026 with Breakthrough designation and a Continued Access Protocol of 120 patients with cost recovery from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS); the price per case is about USD 40,000 against TransMedics' USD 85,000 and up (Jefferies, 24-Jul-26). Surgeons describe the box as easier to prepare and carry. The sceptics are specific: the earlier European cold-heart trial did not meet its endpoints; a surgical research director argues the ice comparator inflated the benefit; long-term head-to-head data do not exist.

The European rollout, tested on the registries. The company's plan is three tiers: keep the eight derogation and compassionate-use centres active; activate "about 30" centres in the funded countries (France, the Netherlands, Austria, the United Kingdom); then others as country listings allow (third-quarter 2026 presentation). The registries say the following. Europe performed about 2,900 heart transplants in 2024 (the World Health Organization region, 33 countries); the Western and Central European core about 2,500. France has 24 transplant teams and did 414 hearts in 2024, of which 13 were placed on a perfusion machine (8 in 2023, 23 in 2022); the Agence de la biomédecine says that low number is why it prepared a funding request for routine use, which became the national reimbursement of May 2026; only five French teams do more than 20 hearts a year and the largest, Pitié-Salpêtrière, did 80 (and 11 of them under Xvivo's derogation in its first months). Germany has 18 to 19 active centres and did 330 hearts in 2023, with the top five doing about two-thirds of them; it has no DCD programme and no reimbursement, which is why it is in the third tier. The United Kingdom has seven centres and did 237 in 2023/24, 65 of them DCD; TransMedics' system was used at most centres for "a small proportion" of cases and at Harefield for all brain-dead donor hearts in earlier years. Spain did 347 in 2024 (29% DCD) across about 22 historical programmes; Italy 413, with Bari at 73 in 2024 and 94 by October 2025, the largest centre in Europe. Adding the four Tier 2 countries gives about 38 programmes and about 790 transplants a year; "about 30 centres" is therefore most of the funded market by count, and perhaps half by volume once the small programmes are left out. Australia, the precedent the company cites, reached 40% to 50% of brain-dead donor hearts under special access.

What the test says about Richard's instinct. The count in the model (25 live centres by end-2027) is not mad: it is the company's Tier 2, and Tier 2 is a real list of about 38 programmes in four funded countries. The pace is the question. France, reimbursed since May, machine-perfused 3% of its hearts in 2024 and has eight derogation centres across Europe as the base; EVLP, the company's own precedent in lung, took a decade to reach 10% to 20% of United States lung transplants; a former chief executive of the company says reimbursement and guideline delays "could double penetration timeline" (9-Jun-26); DNB Carnegie's own precedent is "about one year for national funding after launch". My reading is that the funded four can plausibly have a dozen to twenty centres active by end-2027, concentrated in France and the derogation base, with the thirty reached in 2028 if the Netherlands, Austria and the United Kingdom fund it in the first half of 2027; the model's 25 by end-2027 sits at the optimistic end of that range, and the company has not published a count of its own. The commissioned report on European centres will replace this reading with centre-level evidence.

The United States, where the advantage is on paper. TransMedics' Organ Care System has over 1,000 heart cases, Medicare and private reimbursement, 22 aircraft and about 26% of United States heart, lung and liver transplants; Paragonix's SherpaPak has over 50% of heart and lung transport (Bernstein, 28-Jan-26) at about USD 20,000 a case; Traferox's cold heart system was cleared in September 2026. Xvivo has the evidence, a third of the incumbent's price, a Continued Access cohort (expanded to 120 patients in August 2026, of which DNB Carnegie counted only three enrolled by 23-Oct-26), and no launch organisation that anyone can name. The management link is direct: the evidence took five years and was delivered; the organisation to sell it has not been built, and the chief executive's own words to Richard a year ago ("the US needs a CEO") remain the best description of the gap.

Liver: leading in Europe, being commoditised from below, absent in the United States until 2028

Position. Liver Assist has over half of European liver perfusion, over 100 publications, and a dual hypothermic and normothermic capability that OrganOx's metra lacks (a former director, 25-Mar-26); in Italy the Avionord service model has 15 of 22 centres on a pay-per-procedure basis, the one services model in the group that works. Where it is contested. OrganOx, now Terumo's with Terumo's balance sheet, has over 10,000 transplants on the metra, leases the device, is growing about 50% and plans a lighter model; one centre runs "95% of its normothermic liver cases on OrganOx on cost"; Bridge to Life's VitaSmart cleared in the United States in January 2026, won NICE funding in August, raised USD 110 million and sells at USD 15,000 to 20,000 a case on one expert's count and a few thousand euros on another's (and is "super cheap" to an Emory surgeon); TransMedics holds 40% to 75% of United States liver perfusion. Abdominal gross margin fell from 68% to 54% and has recovered to 61%; real price runs minus 1% to 2% a year. The Change Forces report's phrase is that cheaper adequate systems "threaten the margin expansion thesis across the abdominal value chain".

Advantage trend. Narrowing in Europe on price, held by clinical relationships and the Italian service model; zero in the United States until the 510(k) lands, and then entering against two incumbents and a cheap entrant. The management link: the 510(k) re-route is a good decision made two years after the original plan became unaffordable; the European position is being defended by the same evidence-first culture that will not match a cheaper device on price.

Kidney: a challenger with a slipping clock

Position. Organ Recovery Systems' LifePort has about 95% of United States kidney perfusion; Xvivo's Kidney Assist Transport grew 63% and 72% in the first two quarters of 2026 from a small base (disposables above SEK 10 million a quarter for the first time) and 19% in the third on a component replacement and the manufacturing transfer; Canada is approved; the broad United States launch has moved from 2025 to 2027; Paragonix's KidneyVault and Terumo's planned kidney programme are arriving. TransMedics has no kidney product, which Jefferies calls "a massive strategic gap fully dominated by XVIVO". Advantage trend. Widening in portfolio terms (the all-organ offer), narrowing in time: each year the launch slips, the field fills. The management link: this is the organ Richard flagged in January as "continually delayed" and it has been delayed again since; the company's explanations are each true (the chief executive's own rule, Apr-25: launching kidney "without US-specific clinical data would not succeed"; then a component; then a transfer) and the pattern is the point: evidence first, dates second, every time.

Services and the route to market: the structural weakness

United States organ recovery services, at about 5% of sales, took SEK 44.6 million of revenue in the first nine months of 2026 against SEK 59.7 million a year earlier and ran a minus 19% gross margin in the third quarter; TransMedics' National OCS Program, with 20 hubs, 22 aircraft and 250 clinical specialists, is the thing surgeons buy when they buy a bundle, and smaller programmes "depend on it". Xvivo's answer is partnership (perfusionists, aviation) and the hubs. The honest statement of the position is that Xvivo is a product company whose products increasingly need a service wrapped around them in the United States, and that it has twice tried to supply that wrapper (STAR Teams; its own recovery roster) and twice run it at a loss.

9. The interlinkage: how the management and the business shape each other

The lung franchise removes the pressure that would otherwise force the management to change. A consumable with 80%-plus margins and no real competitor funds three launches, a loss-making unit and SEK 150 million a year of capitalised development, and leaves net cash at the end. No covenant, no dividend, no activist and no cash need has ever required this management to cut the services unit, name a chief financial officer, or set a target it could meet. The strength is real; its effect on management is that patience is never punished internally, only by the market.

The culture that produces the clinical quality produces the commercial slowness. Evidence first, consensus, partnership with surgeons, "we are not a logistics company": these give the company the only randomised heart data in its category and a reputation with clinicians that TransMedics lacks. The same traits make it late to services, reluctant to own logistics, slow to replace underperformers (the United States leadership change was "agreed" in January and is still undisclosed), and unwilling to reset a target. One cannot be bought without the other; the question for an owner is which one the next three years pay for.

The ambition has outrun the organisation, and the capitalised development hides the cost of the gap. Three organs launching at once, in two continents, with 207 people and a part-time chief financial officer, is a plan sized to the science. Reported EBITDA at 20% looks like a business that can afford it; the same business fully expensed would be at breakeven, and the amortisation that now starts (SEK 39 million a year for the CE mark alone, more for the PMA and the 510(k)) will make the reported numbers tell the truth over 2027 and 2028 whether or not the launches succeed. Management's choice to spend the operating leverage through 2027 is defensible if the United States heart launch arrives in 2028; it is a cost without a product if the FDA asks for more.

The weaknesses are concentrated where the next value is. Lung's value is secure and in the price; the heart's, the United States liver's and the kidney's are not, and all three run through the commercial organisation the company has not yet built. The strategy's spine (all organs) is therefore carried by its weakest limb. This is the precise sense in which Richard's "it needs better management, better investment; it has neither" is right: better management here means a United States commercial leader and a chief financial officer, and better investment means a sales organisation sized to the launch, and neither exists yet.

Where the interlinkage runs the other way. The hubs show that when the company designs a commercial model in its own idiom (a protocol, a partner, a clinical specialist on site), it executes it early and well. The board's refresh (a Bonesupport chief executive; a GE Healthcare and Demant operator) is the first time in this tenure that the oversight has been built for the commercial half rather than the scientific one. The incentive plan now pays on perfusion volume. Each of these is a management action aimed at the weakness, taken in the last twelve months. None has yet produced a result.

10. Okay, good or great

Against the named set (the organ-perfusion peers TransMedics, Terumo's OrganOx, Getinge's Paragonix, Bridge to Life and Organ Recovery Systems; and the Nordic growth medtechs Bonesupport, Vitrolife, Elekta, Getinge and Ambu), using the Viewforth words for the letters where a grade helps and the Investment Analyst's judgement where it does not:

  • Scientific, clinical and regulatory execution: very good (B). Only TransMedics has a comparable evidence programme and it is older; nobody in the set has a randomised survival-linked heart trial. Held off A by the dates: every regulatory date before 2026 slipped, and the first dates assumed they would not.
  • Commercial and organisational execution: poor (D). A services unit at minus 19%, a decade behind TransMedics in the United States, a two-year kidney slip, no United States leader, no chief financial officer, surgeons lost to rivals. Held off F by the hubs, the European heart derogation base and the Italian model, which are working.
  • Capital allocation and financial discipline: solid (C). Prudent balance sheet, a well-timed raise, a cost programme when needed, a cheaper liver route; against that, an acquisition that created the loss-making unit, a second that stalled, capitalisation that flatters, no hedging, and returns on capital near 4%.
  • Honesty and governance: very good (B). Candid, clean, pay that falls when targets are missed. Held off A by the thin ownership, the absent internal audit and the chairman's dependence.
  • Expectations management and promise-keeping: poor (D). Targets left standing; no guidance; dates narrowed in wording rather than reset.
  • Strategy and ambition: very good (B) on the strategy, poor (D) on sizing the organisation to it.

The placing. Taken together, and weighted to what decides value over the next three years (the commercial harvest of a scientific lead), this is an okay management and business combination: a good business core (lung; the heart's evidence; the balance sheet) with a weak commercial edge, led by a steward who protects the core well and has not yet built the edge. It is not good, because a good combination in this set would have named its United States leader and chief financial officer before its largest launch, would have exited or fixed services inside six quarters, would set targets it beats, and would have a commercial organisation whose throughput it was willing to disclose. It is not bad, because a bad one would have missed the science, raised equity, lost the lung franchise's pricing or hidden the timing items, and this management has done none of those.

What a good combination would look like here, in the company's own terms: a permanent chief financial officer with a stated margin floor; a named United States heart launch leader with a budget; services at breakeven with named contract conversions; the 2027 targets re-dated to a year the company can beat; hub throughput and the customer's run-rate disclosed; two Tier 2 reimbursements won on the company's timetable. What a great one would look like: all of that, plus a United States heart launch in 2028 that takes named centres from TransMedics on evidence and price, a beat-and-raise habit against numerical guidance, and a return on the SEK 150 million a year of development that shows in the margin by 2029. Nothing in the record says the second is likely; several things in the last twelve months (the board, the incentive plan, the hubs, the two milestones) say the first is possible.

The counter-case to "okay", in its strongest form. This management inherited a company whose founder-era chief executive died without a successor, kept the strategy and the team intact, doubled revenue in two years, absorbed a 15% revenue fall and a 64% share-price fall in 2025 without a raise, delivered the two hardest regulatory milestones in the company's history within eight weeks of each other, restored cash generation on command, and has now refreshed its board with the operators it lacked and tied its own pay to volume. On that reading the commercial weaknesses are the cost of doing the science first with 207 people (the Key Question report on plausibility puts the opposite view in one sentence: Xvivo "has never demonstrated the commercial scaling speed of peers like Bonesupport ... nor completed a complex services turnaround"), the services unit is launch infrastructure that will be judged in 2028 not 2026, and the chief financial officer's move is the board using its best executive where it matters. For this counter-case to win, the next four quarters have to show the commercial half catching up: a named chief financial officer and United States leader, services at or above breakeven by the first quarter of 2027, hub throughput disclosed, and the heart's Tier 2 centres activating on the company's own count. If they do, the placing moves to good; if the targets are simply reaffirmed again in January while services and the finance seat stand where they are, it stays okay and Richard's "mediocre" becomes the better word.

11. What this means for delivery against expectations

Kept short and qualitative, as the brief asks; the memo of 26-Oct-26 carries the arithmetic.

  • On the things management controls, expect what it says and not what it targets. The qualitative steers (services slow to convert; kidney constrained until early 2027; the customer declining; investment continuing; margins partly seasonal) have been the accurate half of its communication and are more cautious than consensus for the next twelve months. The 2027 targets are the inaccurate half and are more likely to be re-dated than met (the Key Question report on guidance expects management to be "forced to re-date or lower its targets" by the 28 January report); a reaffirmation in January would be a worse signal about expectation management than a reset.
  • On the things it does not control, its record is of honest optimism. The FDA's handling of the heart file and four countries' reimbursement decisions decide the next twelve months' narrative; the company's first dates on such things have slipped in every case before 2026, and its wording has already narrowed ("targeted", "subject to review").
  • The sell-side's cuts are mostly not a verdict on management. Most of the twelve-month fall in financial year 2027 consensus earnings per share is the brokers aligning to disclosed amortisation, a normal tax rate and the end of currency gains, and to one customer; the part that is management is the choice to spend through 2027 and the targets. Judging the team by estimate revisions would convict it of the brokers' modelling. On Richard's question of 30-Oct-26 ("Is SS EPS reported excluding amortisation?"): DNB Carnegie's figures are labelled adjusted and, on the memo's reading of its model, include development amortisation at a lower level than the company has disclosed (about SEK 38 million of total development amortisation for 2027 against SEK 39 million for the CE-mark asset alone), so the gap to the Financial Analyst's model is one of level, not of definition; I have not seen the broker's definition page and flag this as unconfirmed.
  • The management-quality indicators to watch over the next two reports (28 January and late April 2027), each one a management action rather than a market outcome: the chief financial officer named; a United States commercial leader named; what is said about the 2027 targets; services' gross margin and any named conversions; hub throughput or a count of European heart centres activated; the customer's contract terms, if given; the fourth-quarter depreciation and amortisation and tax charges (whether the company helps the market align); whether the capacity project completed in the fourth quarter as promised for two years.
