EXECUTIVE SUMMARY (BLUF) 1 finding

Business Model and Strategic Positioning

SES is integrating Intelsat to scale its global multi-orbit operations and counter structural declines in broadcasting, with management targeting EUR 370 million in run-rate synergies to protect group profitability through this consolidation.

The revenue mix is shifting to high-growth data connectivity segments like government and aviation, while video distribution remains highly cash generative but faces terminal headwinds from cord-cutting.

Strategic capital allocation relies on significant spectrum clearing payments totaling USD 5.6 billion from the FCC, providing critical deleveraging firepower.

New leadership under Adel Al-Saleh is implementing aggressive restructuring and cost reductions to deliver guidance.

SES operates a multi-orbit satellite network combining 43 geostationary and 28 medium Earth orbit satellites, allowing the company to deliver low-latency data alongside wide-beam broadcast coverage.

The business is organized into two primary divisions comprising Networks and Media, with Networks growing to represent 54% of total group revenue by fiscal year 2024.

The video revenue model is driven by recurring transponder leases on long-term capacity contracts, whereas Networks increasingly relies on end-to-end managed contracts rather than raw bandwidth sales.

Total group gross backlog stands at EUR 6.4 billion as of mid-year 2026 reporting, helping insulate the near-term top-line from spot market pricing pressure.

The acquisition of Intelsat scales the combined group to a pro forma revenue of EUR 3.8 billion, with management expecting to deliver EUR 370 million in annual run-rate synergies by year three.

Financial Profile and Capital Structure Pressures

SES generated pro forma revenue of EUR 2,627 million in fiscal 2025, outscaling regional satellite peers, yet experienced a pro forma revenue decline of -1.6% at constant foreign exchange in fiscal 2025.

SES adjusted EBITDA margin dropped to 45.5% in 2025 due to video segment contraction, while the company reported a 0.5% ROCE in fiscal 2025, reflecting the competitive drag on capital employed.

Adjusted free cash flow turned negative EUR -130 million in H1 2026 as massive LEO constellation capex limits free cash flow generation across all geostationary operators.

SES adjusted net leverage reached 4.4x in the first half of fiscal 2026, prompting management to target net leverage below 3.0x but remove the 18-month deadline.

Moody's downgraded SES to Ba1 in late 2025, removing its investment-grade credit rating, followed by Fitch downgrading SES credit rating to BBB- in early 2026, citing acquisition debt loads.

Management secured USD 5.6 billion in gross compensation for clearing upper C-band spectrum, with proceeds primarily directed to accelerate postacquisition balance sheet deleveraging toward a 3.0x target, compared to peer operator Eutelsat securing only USD 504 million from the same spectrum clearing program.

Severe pricing pressure from LEO constellations like Starlink may neutralize pro forma integration synergies and accelerate Media decline.

Industry Dynamics and Competitive Erosion

The global satcom market was valued at USD 4.5 billion in 2021 and is projected to reach USD 5.7 billion by 2029, with high-growth LEO markets forecast to expand up to 14% annually out to 2033.

Starlink has reached an estimated 13.5 million subscribers exiting September 2026, demonstrating commercial traffic migrating aggressively from GEO to LEO which now captures 95% to 98% of annual satellite deployments.

Traditional GEO incumbents are pursuing defensive multi-orbit M&A to build scale against lower-latency low Earth orbit operators, with scale-driven transactions including the Viasat acquisition of Inmarsat and the merger of SES and Intelsat.

SpaceX represents roughly half of all global launches, establishing an insurmountable cost and cadence advantage over rivals.

Starlink has deployed disruptive monthly pricing strategies, undercutting local fiber and home internet providers globally, setting prices USD 10 below incumbent plans in Australia to rapidly capture retail and mobile subscribers.

This aggressive pricing forced traditional consumer satellite provider HughesNet into a recent bankruptcy filing.

Key success factors have shifted from raw transponder bandwidth leasing to integrated multi-orbit managed service delivery, with competitive advantage now relying on highly standardized software-defined satellite payloads enabling dynamic real-time beam routing.

Unchecked capacity expansion by LEO operators may trigger severe terminal price wars that destroy geostationary operator profitability.

Value Chain Position and Margin Compression

Downstream integration features rapidly thinning retail margins as operators transition to LEO services, with legacy inflight systems historically capturing 80% to 90% operating margins before low Earth orbit consolidation squeezed returns to 30% to 40% margin levels.

Wholesale satellite operations yield typical 70% to 80% gross margins and 10% to 20% EBIT margins, though revenue mix shifts toward lower-margin connectivity services dilute operator profitability, with Eutelsat reporting EBITDA margin contraction to 51.2% because of increased pass-through equipment costs and LEO connectivity applications estimated well below legacy 70% levels.

SpaceX achieves superior economics by controlling its entire launch platform and its consumer communications network, having captured approximately 13.5 million global subscribers exiting the late September 2026 reporting period.

European operators mitigate vertical pressure by securing long-term sovereign contracts including the IRIS² constellation project, with French military connectivity agreements providing EUR 138 million in initial commitments over a four-year contract.

Severe price erosion from Starlink may negate any multiorbit premium as downstream clients gain total pricing leverage.

Customer Concentration and Switching Dynamics

No single customer accounted for 10% or more of group revenue during the 2026 fiscal year, though United States defense and civilian agencies generate 64% of total segment revenue inside the key Government vertical.

The group boasts a EUR 6.4 billion gross backlog supporting medium-term operational visibility, with the Networks backlog growing to EUR 3.6 billion while Media backlog stands at EUR 3.0 billion.

Average customer contract length has historically remained over eight years, standing at 8.4 years historically, but only 20% of customer contracts contain active inflation indexation clauses to offset cost escalation.

Customer switching costs are high in aviation and government, evidenced by specialized electronically steered antenna hardware integrated directly into aircraft tails and highly customized sovereign architectures which underpin decades-long multi-contract iterations.

However, rising churn to LEO operators threatens legacy connectivity accounts, with notable accounts including Air France switching directly to Starlink's LEO network and Delta Air Lines representing another contract roll-off as they transition their fleet to Viasat.

Rapid commercial aviation defection to Starlink could threaten to erode Networks' high-margin backlog.

Supplier Dependencies and Launch Bottlenecks

SES relies heavily on a small pool of satellite manufacturers including Boeing, Airbus, and Thales Alenia Space, reducing the group's negotiating leverage and increasing core satellite procurement risk over time.

Power module malfunctions on Boeing-built O3b mPOWER satellites significantly reduced the operational lifetimes of initial spacecraft, requiring SES and Boeing to upgrade the remaining seven satellites to mitigate early fleet capacity constraints.

The group depends on a highly limited selection of launch providers to place its assets in orbit, having contracted SpaceX to launch its entire initial 13-satellite MEO broadband constellation on Falcon 9 rockets.

SpaceX Falcon 9 availability is ending for commercial operators as the industry faces launch shortages through 2028.

Downstream distribution relies on complex indirect channel partnerships that face long-term disintermediation risks from direct LEO operators, with rapid expansion by vertically integrated LEO networks threatening to bypass traditional wholesale and managed-service satellite distribution models.

Unresolved launch manifest shortages and manufacturer delays may stall the next-generation meoSphere rollout, letting Starlink permanently disintermediate key enterprise channels.

Operator Quality and Management Execution

Under CEO Adel Al-Saleh, SES is executing a transformational integration of Intelsat to counter structural declines in legacy video broadcasting, with the Chief Executive restructuring the executive committee by appointing six new members focused on execution.

Executive long-term equity grants are linked to delivering synergies from the Intelsat acquisition, with short-term executive bonuses measured against achieving specific budgeted targets for revenue and EBITDA.

Balance sheet deleveraging is prioritized, supported by USD 5.6 billion in gross spectrum clearing windfalls to offset post-acquisition leverage, with spectrum proceeds primarily directed to accelerate post-acquisition balance sheet deleveraging toward a 3.0x target.

Combined pro forma EBITDA guidance was reset to approximately EUR 1.5 billion post-merger, and dividend growth has been paused as management prioritizes deleveraging back to investment grade.

Depressed capital return metrics reflect heavy satellite constellation investment cycles that yield low single-digit return on capital employed, with return on invested capital for geostationary operators declining to low single-digit levels and standalone return on capital employed registering a low 0.5% in 2025, with Bernstein forecasting that the return on invested capital will remain below a 10% WACC through 2030.

The structural moat is migrating from high-margin video transponder leasing toward highly competitive multi-orbit networks with lower-margin profiles, as the core legacy moat of geostationary video orbital slots faces structural video cord-cutting headwinds.

Accelerated commercial customer defections could permanently depress multi-orbit network pricing and asset utilization, with confidence in foundation quality remaining low to medium due to intense capital intensity and competition.

Structural Disruption and Long-Term Market Evolution

Industry entry barriers are shifting from orbital slots to massive constellation capital and highly constrained launch access, with deploying a competitive global LEO network requiring billions of dollars in upfront constellation capital and commercial satellite operators facing an entrenched launch services oligopoly that delays competitive constellation rollouts.

Technological disruption from LEO constellations is accelerating the structural obsolescence of bespoke geostationary payloads, with latency-sensitive commercial traffic migrating aggressively from geostationary to LEO satellite orbits and manufacturing transitioning from bespoke high-cost geostationary platforms to mass-produced modular satellites.

Long-term market expansion is anchored in sovereign and mobility verticals outstripping sluggish global GDP growth rates, as traditional satellite video broadcasting faces terminal structural declines from cord-cutting and OTT platforms while commercial aviation services represent a high-growth satcom vertical projected to expand steadily through 2045 and sovereign defense budgets are accelerating material satellite communications spend amid worsening geopolitical tensions.

The European IRIS² project provides a crucial defensive backlog that protects SES from commercial pricing erosion.

Unmitigated LEO capacity expansion could permanently depress multi-orbit connectivity pricing and destroy geostationary asset values.

Outliers worth noting 1 finding

Based on the comprehensive financial and operational analysis across all sections, SES S.A. exhibits three notable statistical outliers that position the company in the extreme tails of its peer distribution. First, spectrum clearing windfall concentration represents the most pronounced positive outlier in the satellite operator universe. SES secured $5.6 billion in gross compensation for clearing upper C-band spectrum, while peer operator Eutelsat secured only $504 million from the same spectrum clearing program.

This represents an 11-fold advantage in non-operating cash windfalls, placing SES in the top ~2% of all global satellite operators for one-time regulatory proceeds. The magnitude of this windfall–equivalent to approximately twice SES's annual revenue –provides extraordinary balance sheet flexibility unavailable to traditional geostationary peers. This structural capital advantage is being deployed toward accelerated post-acquisition balance sheet deleveraging toward a 3.0x target, offering a refinancing cushion that competitors cannot replicate. [Cross-ref: Section 2, Section 9] Second, return on capital employed collapse marks SES as a bottom-tier outlier among capital-intensive infrastructure operators.

Return on capital employed was a low 0.5% in 2025, and Bernstein forecasts that the return on invested capital will remain below a 10% WACC through 2030. This 0.5% ROCE places SES in the bottom ~5% of all European industrial companies with comparable asset bases, reflecting the severe competitive displacement facing geostationary satellite operators. The metric is particularly alarming when compared to historical infrastructure norms where regulated utilities and transport operators typically generate 8-12% ROCE even in mature markets.

The depressed returns stem from return on invested capital for geostationary operators declining to low single-digit levels due to aggressive LEO constellation pricing pressure. This structural profitability erosion signals a fundamental paradigm shift in satellite economics that management's integration synergies may not fully offset. [Cross-ref: Section 2, Section 9] - Earnings Calls Third, government revenue concentration within the Networks segment represents an extreme customer dependency outlier.

United States defense and civilian agencies generate 64% of total segment revenue inside the key Government vertical, creating a single-sovereign exposure that far exceeds typical aerospace and defense diversification norms. While SES Space and Defense actively services over 60 global government clients including European and NATO partners, the 64% U.S. concentration within a segment that now comprises over half of total group revenue places SES in the top ~5% of non-U.S. domiciled defense contractors for American budget dependency.

This creates acute geopolitical and budgetary risk, as even modest shifts in Pentagon procurement priorities or transatlantic political tensions could materially impact the company's fastestgrowing revenue pillar. The concentration is partially mitigated by the United States DoD now comprising less than 50% of total government-segment revenues, but the sovereign customer lumpiness remains an extreme statistical outlier relative to diversified satellite operator norms.
