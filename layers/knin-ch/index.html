External change forces

Layer 1 · External change forces

Written last by the Investment Analyst for its group. The rating above stands for it, so it carries no number of its own.

The external environment is at or near its most favourable point for Kuehne + Nagel and is scheduled to deteriorate. Over the last six months demand, share and yields all helped: a cyclical volume rebound (partly restocking and front-loading), a disrupted main competitor, disruption-supported ocean yields and a technology-driven air boom. Over the next six to eighteen months the forces that delivered this weaken in a predictable order: DSV stabilises and fights to recover volume (from late 2026), Suez routing returns and congestion clears (already under way), the container delivery wave arrives (2027-2028) and air capacity normalises. The durable positives, supply-chain complexity and the scale advantage in automation, are real but smaller in EBIT terms. The key modelling consequence: separate volume (which can stay at or above market growth) from unit gross profit (which should fall in Sea and Air in 2027-2028 from 2026 levels), and assume the external contribution to EBIT growth turns negative in 2027, leaving self-help to carry earnings.

C-02

To what extent, and in which direction, is competitive intensity changing?

F429th of 13

Layer 1 · External change forces

Judgement: competitive intensity is temporarily lower for Kuehne + Nagel in 2026 because its largest rival is distracted, but it is set to rise again from 2027, and the structural direction is up, not down. DSV's integration of DB Schenker (enterprise value EUR 14.3bn) has disrupted DSV: its Q3 2026 sea and air volumes fell 3% while Kuehne + Nagel's grew 4.5% and 10%; its Road on-time delivery fell into the 80% range and Kuehne + Nagel took road share; experts expect 5% to 20% Schenker customer churn. This windfall is time-limited: DSV restored road on-time delivery to 94% by early October 2026 and expects to win back lost volume over three to four quarters, it will emerge with a lower cost base (Air and Sea conversion already 46% against Kuehne + Nagel's 32%), a DKK 9bn synergy target, DKK 6bn of artificial-intelligence savings by 2030 and a share buyback from 2027. Two further forces raise intensity structurally: artificial-intelligence cost savings are likely to be competed away as scale players "price aggressively to win volumes" (Bernstein; management itself conceded some early gains will be competed away), and experts report DSV holds a 5% to 10% price advantage in large multinational tenders. The CEO's claim that "only three large competitors left" has raised pricing power is not yet supported by unit gross profit data (Sea gross profit per TEU fell 9% year on year in Q2 2026). For the model: assume part of the 2026 share gain is retained (customers are splitting allocations to diversify away from DSV, which is sticky) but that unit gross profit pressure rises through 2027 as DSV seeks to recapture volume.

C-05

How are input costs, input availability and supplier concentration changing, and is the net effect a headwind or a tailwind?

D428th of 13

Layer 1 · External change forces

Judgement: the forwarder's main input, carrier capacity, is becoming abundant, which lowers buying rates, but abundance historically compresses forwarders' unit gross profit as volatility and complexity fall, so the net effect over the next 6 to 18 months is a mild headwind that grows into a material one in 2027-2028. Ocean: the container orderbook is 42% of the active fleet; brokers expect fleet growth of about 7% to 7.9% in 2027 and 12% to 13% in 2028 against demand growth of 3% to 4%; Maersk ordered about 500,000 TEU in Q3 2026; Suez transits are back to 47% to 54% of 2023 levels and Gemini has moved six services back to Suez; only port congestion (49.9% schedule reliability in August 2026, more than 4.3m TEU tied up) and the Cape diversion still absorb the surplus. Air: Middle Eastern carriers have restored capacity and passenger belly space is recovering, easing tightness on China-Europe lanes. Supplier concentration (top ten liners hold 86% of capacity) gives carriers no pricing power in oversupply. Labour, the other input (60% to 70% of opex), sees moderate wage inflation, offset by automation. The first-order effect of cheaper capacity is positive (buy rates fall before sell rates in contracted business), the second-order effect negative (transparency and calm markets let shippers demand rate concessions; the CEO's "complexity" premium shrinks). Fuel spikes (oil at USD 111) are passed through with a lag and mainly swell revenue and working capital.

D408th of 13

Layer 1 · External change forces

Judgement: macro forces are acting at all three levels, and they bite hardest at the industry level, through the shipping capacity cycle and geopolitics that set freight yields, rather than through end-demand; at company level the Swiss franc is a persistent second bite. C-03

To what extent is a disruption force strengthening or being disproved, and in which direction does it act on the company?

D587th of 13

Layer 1 · External change forces

Judgement: the disruption force that matters is artificial intelligence in white-collar forwarding work, and it is strengthening; it acts in Kuehne + Nagel's favour against the long tail of smaller forwarders, but against the industry's unit profitability, so its net effect on Kuehne + Nagel's margin is smaller than management's gross figures suggest. C-04

To what extent is political or regulatory change under way, and how does it bear on the company?

C565th of 13

Layer 1 · External change forces

Judgement: heavy political and regulatory change is under way and, on balance, it modestly helps a scaled, customs-capable forwarder by adding complexity that customers pay to outsource, while adding volatility to volumes. The main channels: (1) Tariffs and trade policy (US Section 301 duties, the end of US de minimis treatment for Chinese goods, a Section 301 port-fee suspension expiring 10-Nov-26, the EU's removal of the EUR 150 de minimis threshold with a EUR 3 duty) re-route trade and increase customs work; Road Logistics EBIT rose 29% in Q2 2026 on customs-clearance demand, and the Farrow acquisition (Canadian customs broker) fits this. The downside is volume shocks: the risk report's trigger is a 20% to 25% drop in bookings out of China. (2) Environmental rules (EU ETS at 70% of shipping emissions in 2026 and 100% in 2027; FuelEU Maritime; CO2-based road tolls) raise carriers' costs, which are passed through to forwarders and then to shippers; they inflate net turnover and working capital more than they change gross profit, and they create demand for Kuehne + Nagel's book-and-claim sustainable-fuel products. (3) A May 2026 US Supreme Court broker-liability ruling raises the cost to serve and hurts small brokers, which favours scale. (4) Geopolitics (Red Sea, Strait of Hormuz) is the largest single regulatory-political variable because it sets effective shipping capacity (see C-07 and C-09). The complexity tailwind is real but small in EBIT; the volume and rate volatility is large.

The Investment Analyst's opening notes

Read this first

Bases. As in the second set: EBIT is the company's recurring EBIT (the guidance basis); gross profit is the company's line (net turnover less transport costs), the denominator of the conversion rate; free cash flow is the company's definition (after capital spending and lease principal, before dividends and acquisitions); net debt excludes leases unless stated. FactSet's historical free cash flow (after dividends) and FactSet's net working capital (receivables plus inventories less payables) are not used as cash measures.

The model's cross-check gaps, explained. The one-page summary shows the model's EPS CHF 0.16 to 0.20 above the second set's cross-check in every year (9.46 against 9.30 in FY2026, 9.11 against 8.94, 9.05 against 8.86, 9.59 against 9.40, 10.35 against 10.15) and the model's free cash flow CHF 55m above in FY2026 (995 against 940) and CHF 10m to 12m above in FY2027 to FY2030. Both gaps are explained and neither shows a wrong input. (1) EPS: the model has no line for minority interests. The difference between the model's net income before minorities and the cross-check's attributable net income is CHF 24m (FY2026), 30m, 32m, 33m and 35m, which is the minority charge the second set deducts (the IMC 49% minority and smaller ones, after the November 2025 Apex buyout). The memo should quote the cross-check EPS (CHF 9.30, 8.94, 8.86, 9.40, 10.15) as the Viewforth EPS and treat the model's figure as EPS before minorities. (2) Free cash flow: the FY2026 gap of CHF 55m is the cash use of the FY2025 restructuring provisions (about CHF 60m paid out in 2026) and dividends paid to minorities, for which the model has no input; the CHF 10m to 12m a year after that is the minority dividend. No forecast input is changed for these gaps.

Where this set differs from the first and second sets. Four points, each explained where it matters below.

  1. The CHF 4m Sea Logistics depreciation credit in Q3 2026 was not a flattering one-off. The CFO explained on the Q3 call that it came from a cumulative CHF 23m reclassification of real-estate costs between operating expenses and depreciation with zero net effect on EBIT (operating expenses were raised by the same amount). Both earlier sets treated it as a CHF 14m to 15m benefit to Q3 EBIT, and the second set deducts CHF 15m from FY2027 EBIT for its non-recurrence. That deduction should not be there, so the second set's FY2027 EBIT is about CHF 15m (1%) too low on this item; and the Q3 2026 operating cost growth of 7.1% that the second set used as evidence that savings are being absorbed is overstated by the reclassification (about 6% underlying).
  2. The Amazon customer incentive is probably larger than the second set's CHF 40m a year. The Q3 2026 charge against net turnover was CHF 17m; Morgan Stanley treats this as a persistent quarterly run-rate (about CHF 68m a year), while straight-line amortisation of the CHF 160m over seven years would be about CHF 23m a year; the charge follows commercial milestones (the Zip Co and Plug Power precedents), so it will fluctuate. At the Q3 run-rate FY2027 EBIT is about CHF 28m (1.8%) lower than the second set's. Points 1 and 2 offset to within about CHF 15m, inside the rounding of a CHF 1.5bn forecast, so I leave the forecast inputs unchanged and do not write an inputs-changed file.
  3. The 2016 precedent is a weaker analogue than the earlier sets implied. Jefferies' contemporaneous notes (1-Mar-17 and 14-Mar-17) attribute the 10% fall in Sea gross profit per TEU in Q4 2016 to a sudden carrier rate increase that could not be passed on (the squeeze after Hanjin's collapse), not to abundant capacity. The clean precedents for unit gross profit falling as capacity loosens are therefore 2023-2024 (the unwinding of an extreme premium: Sea gross profit per TEU -30% in 2023 and -28% year on year in Q1 2024) and, in the other direction, 2019 (held at about CHF 317 per TEU in a demand slowdown without a supply wave). The direction of the second set's unit gross profit assumption stands, but the historical base rate for its size is thinner and more contested than presented (D-03 and D-04).
  4. The DSV pricing evidence reconciles rather than contradicts. The first set relied on experts saying DSV is 5% to 10% cheaper in large tenders; the second set found Kuehne + Nagel pricing 6% to 12% below DSV in several 2026 tenders. The DSV recapture report adds that DSV's European road pricing historically sat 5% to 10% above competitors, that DSV's scale secures carrier rates 8% to 10% below Kuehne + Nagel's, and that DSV's culture is to let low-margin business go. Read together: DSV has a procurement cost advantage it has not used in price, Kuehne + Nagel bought part of its 2026 share with price, and DSV has the room to respond when it chooses. That is a latent, not an active, pricing threat, which supports the second set's placing of the risk in European road rather than in a general price war.
