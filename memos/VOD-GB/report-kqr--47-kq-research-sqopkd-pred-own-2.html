Vodafone's adjusted free cash flow definition, its bridge from adjusted EBITDAaL and the reason for the Safaricom disconnect are all documented, and the overall confidence level in the compiled evidence on the definitions and bridges is high. The one part that cannot be closed is Safaricom's longer-term cash contribution. Key gaps remain on the legal timeline of Safaricom's consolidation, the resolution of restricted dividends and future restructuring cash spending.
  2. The exact definition of adjusted free cash flow and the consolidation of Safaricom heavily dictate Vodafone's post-merger dividend coverage and leverage trajectory.
  3. Strategic developments in UK consolidation and tower monetization provide major upside to cash generation, though emerging market volatility remains a persistent concern.

Dividends paid to non-controlling shareholders are explicitly deducted in both the standard and adjusted measures. These minority payments rose to €398 million in H1 FY27 from €141 million a year earlier.

FY26 bridge: Adjusted EBITDAaL was €11,351 million, and cash capital additions of €7,291 million were the largest single outflow. Standard free cash flow of €1,795 million was bridged to adjusted free cash flow of €2,621 million by adding back discrete items.
4.
H1 FY27 bridge: Adjusted EBITDAaL rose to €6,451 million from €5,728 million, yet standard free cash flow was an €845 million outflow on seasonal working capital. The working capital outflow was €2,276 million. Adjusted free cash flow was an outflow of €440 million, improved from an outflow of €583 million. Full-year FY27 guidance was upgraded to €2.8 billion to €3.0 billion.

Safaricom disconnect: Consolidating Safaricom adds €1.1 billion to FY27 adjusted EBITDAaL but €0.0 billion to adjusted free cash flow. The operating cash flows are offset by capital additions and taxes sitting within Safaricom. Minority leakage and lost associate income add to the gap. MTN's R4.1 billion minority leakage consumed 36.9% of its R11.1 billion free cash flow, leaving equity free cash flow at R7.0 billion. Vodafone's own FY22 Egypt reorganization involved a €194 million special dividend to minorities. Together these show the mismatch is a recurring feature of emerging market consolidation.

Deutsche Bank expects leverage to fall below the 2.25 to 2.75 times target range in fiscal 2028. The minority dividend figures (FY26 €245 million against H1 FY27 €398 million) cover different periods and scopes. The Deutsche Bank near-term accretion view and the zero FY27 guidance differ mainly in time horizon, as the FY27 result is characterized as a temporary timing mismatch from the July consolidation date.

Section 1 – The Key Question and Why It Matters

Section summary
  1. Vodafone’s adjusted free cash flow represents a highly customized performance metric designed to measure underlying cash generation after accounting for leases and minority dividends.
  2. The metric systematically excludes volatile cash outflows such as spectrum licenses, discrete restructuring costs, and integration capital additions to isolate operational performance.

The Key Question

1.
Key Question: "Vodafone Group Plc (VOD-GB), a European and African telecommunications operator reporting in euros, with segments Germany (cable, fixed and mobile), the United Kingdom (VodafoneThree, wholly owned since July 2026), Other Europe (Portugal, Ireland, Greece, Romania, Albania, Czech Republic), Turkey, Africa (Vodacom, including Egypt and, since July 2026, a 55% effective stake in Safaricom) and Vodafone Business (connectivity, cloud, security and IoT), plus non-controlled stakes in Vantage Towers (Oak Holdings), Vodafone Idea, TPG Telecom and Ziggo Group. For Vodafone Group Plc (VOD-GB), how is adjusted free cash flow defined in the FY26 annual report and the H1 FY27 results: are dividends paid to non-controlling shareholders (Vodacom, Safaricom, Vodafone Egypt), lease payments, interest and tax deducted, and spectrum, restructuring and integration payments excluded; what was the bridge from adjusted EBITDAaL to adjusted free cash flow in FY26 and H1 FY27, item by item; and why did management state that the Safaricom consolidation adds about EUR 1.1bn to FY27 EBITDAaL but nothing to FY27 adjusted free cash flow?"

Impact on Valuation and Dividend

2.
Strategic dividend coverage: Vodafone's progressive dividend policy relies entirely on the organic growth of its adjusted free cash flow after completing its portfolio transformation. The group upgraded its FY27 adjusted free cash flow guidance range to EUR 2.8bn to EUR 3.0bn from EUR 2.6bn to EUR 2.9bn in November 2026. This cash generation ensures dividend sustainability and supports future earnings per share growth.
3.
Elevated cash conversion: Morgan Stanley highlighted that the group's adjusted free cash flow is projected to grow 8% annually over the coming 36 months, which should support valuation re-rating. Any incremental cost savings will have an amplified positive impact on bottom-line equity free cash flow given the historically low base. This suggests that cash conversion is the primary driver of shareholder returns and long-term valuation upside.
4.
Valuation mismatch: Deutsche Bank noted that Vodafone's European operations trade at a major discount to peers, valued at approximately 4 times EBITDAaL. Strong emerging market growth and the post-merger integration of the UK business are expected to drive significant cash flow generation. This high free cash flow yield provides a strong valuation floor and supports potential share buybacks.
5.
Contrary evidence: A legal challenge in Kenya recently declared the government's 15% stake sale to Vodacom null and void, creating uncertainty around Vodafone's ultimate economic ownership. This legal dispute could reduce the expected consolidated economic benefit of Safaricom from 55% to 40% if the adverse outcome holds. Such a reduction would impact attributable earnings and transaction funding, though it does not invalidate the underlying diversification thesis.

Consolidation and Related Stakes

6.
UK merger synergies: Vodafone's integration of VodafoneThree in the United Kingdom is a key related driver, targeting 1.0bn pounds of annual cost and capex savings by FY32. The company plans to invest 11bn pounds over 10 years to build the country's best network. This integration is projected to more than triple UK operating free cash flow by FY32.
7.
Strategic tower monetization: Morgan Stanley estimates that Vodafone's 45% equity stake in Vantage Towers is worth approximately EUR 7bn, representing 34% of the group's market capitalization. This massive asset is held off-balance sheet and is highly sensitive to falling interest rates. The stake represents significant optionality for future portfolio simplification and balance sheet repair.
8.
Simplified associate stakes: Deutsche Bank expects further balance sheet simplification to assist deleveraging, with Vodafone's equity stake in Vodafone Idea valued at EUR 1.7bn. Additionally, the group's 50% stake in Vantage Towers parent Oak Holdings is worth more than EUR 6bn. Simplifying these non-core holdings will allow the company to organically drop its leverage ratio below the target range of 2.25 to 2.75 times by FY28.
9.
Other side of the story: German operations continue to drag on group performance due to regulatory headwinds and a German impairment of EUR 4.9bn reported in the first half of FY27. While the ex-Africa equity stub has de-rated, European margins must absorb a EUR 100m headwind from a recent ECJ ruling. These domestic pressures could delay the group's leverage reduction and restrict future cash returns unless German broadband trends stabilize.

Standard Free Cash Flow Foundation

10.
Standardized reporting: Vodafone defines its standard free cash flow as adjusted EBITDAaL after accounting for capital additions, working capital movements, and asset disposals. This baseline metric also incorporates cash flows from taxation, net interest, and dividends received from associates. It provides the foundational calculation from which the group derives its adjusted free cash flow metric to evaluate liquidity.
11.
Consistent treatment: Vodafone handles lease liabilities by deducting lease payments directly from its cash flow calculations. These lease payments encompass both the principal repayment and the interest expense under IFRS 16 guidelines. Standard interest payments exclude the cash portion of lease interest of €341 million in H1 FY27 because it is already captured within EBITDAaL adjustments.
12.
Rigorous adjustments: Vodafone strictly deducts both taxation and net financing costs to arrive at its standard free cash flow. In H1 FY27, the group recorded cash taxation outflows of €706 million beside net interest payments of €393 million. Deducting these unavoidable cash outlays ensures that the resulting figure reflects actual cash available to equity holders.
13.
Inherent complexity: Vodafone’s treatment of associate and joint venture dividends adds further complexity to its baseline cash flow definition. In standard free cash flow, dividends received from associates are added, while dividends paid to minorities are subtracted. This structure isolates the cash that is directly controlled by the parent company rather than locked in joint structures.

Adjusted Cash Flow Metrics

14.
Enhanced clarity: Vodafone defines adjusted free cash flow by adding back specific volatile and non-operational cash expenditures to standard free cash flow. These add-backs include spectrum licenses, integration capital additions, and restructuring payments. This adjusted metric is a key performance measure for remuneration used to determine executive incentives and evaluate group liquidity.

15.
Disciplinary exclusion: Vodafone excludes licenses, spectrum, and integration capital additions from its adjusted free cash flow. For the first half of FY27, licenses and spectrum payments of €21 million were excluded. Integration capital additions of €64 million were also added back to the metric during the same period.
16.
Systematic addbacks: Vodafone excludes discrete restructuring costs and their related working capital movements from its adjusted cash flow metrics. Restructuring costs reached €320 million in the first half of FY27 compared to €122 million in the prior period. Management adjusts for these costs because they stem from discrete, non-operational restructuring plans across Europe.
17.
Temporary distortions: Vodafone’s definition of adjusted free cash flow can be distorted by the timing of working capital related to capital additions. For example, working capital movements in respect of capital additions are excluded if they relate directly to integration projects. This accounting treatment ensures that shortterm payables changes do not obscure the underlying operational cash performance.
18.
Contrary evidence: Deutsche Bank noted that while the Safaricom consolidation adds massive paper EBITDAaL to Vodafone's reports, the transaction has a negligible near-term impact on consolidated adjusted free cash flow. This disconnect arises because heavy capital intensity and immediate minority dividend requirements offset Safaricom's operational cash contributions. Consequently, the adjusted cash metric may overstate the group's real operational cash flexibility from its new African footprint.

Treatment of Non-Controlling Dividends

19.
Explicit deductions: Vodafone explicitly deducts dividends paid to non-controlling shareholders in subsidiaries to calculate both standard and adjusted free cash flow. Minority dividend payments rose to €398 million in H1 FY27 compared to €141 million in H1 FY26. These cash outlays reflect dividends paid to minority shareholders in Vodacom and Safaricom following recent portfolio changes.
20.
Elevated leakage: Vodafone’s African operations through Vodacom experience significant cash leakage due to high minority ownership stakes. Morgan Stanley emphasizes that whilst Safaricom consolidation boosts operating earnings, minorities and capital expenditure limit conversion to attributable cash. This structural constraint means that a substantial portion of consolidated cash cannot be upstreamed directly to Vodafone.
21.
Upstreaming friction: Safaricom’s dividend payout policy of 80% represents a high cash return that must be shared with Kenyan minorities. Vodacom's management notes that because the acquisition debt sits at the group level, managing this debt is a priority. This friction highlights how minority leakages restrict cash flow growth at the parent Vodafone level.
22.
Comparable framework: Vodacom Group calculates its own group free cash flow by subtracting capital additions, lease payments, cash interest, and tax. This subsidiary framework also deducts net dividends paid to noncontrolling shareholders, which reached R5.4 billion in the six months ended September 2026. This treatment aligns closely with Vodafone’s consolidated methodology of deducting minority cash distributions from final cash metrics.

Historical Context and Future Guidance

23.
Sector alignment: Orange SA and other European telecommunications peers similarly calculate adjusted free cash flow after accounting for minority interest payments and lease costs. Deutsche Bank notes that Vodafone's post-minority adjusted free cash flow remains highly sensitive to emerging market dividend cash timings. This structural sensitivity means both Vodafone and its peers experience temporary cash flow mismatches when consolidating high-growth subsidiaries.

Historical precedent: Vodafone faced similar cash flow adjustments in FY22 when it paid a special dividend of €194 million to minority shareholders in Egypt. This target excludes restructuring and integration costs which are expected to peak at €0.7 billion. This guidance upgrade reflects strong H1 performance but remains highly dependent on German turnaround stabilization trends.

Other side of the story: While the exclusion of spectrum, restructuring, and integration costs creates a cleaner representation of ongoing operational liquidity, it risks masking the heavy cash drag of structural transformations. Investors must monitor standard free cash flow because it represents the actual cash available for equity dividends and debt reduction.

Section 4 – The Safaricom Consolidation and the Free Cash Flow Disconnect

Minority Shareholder Leakage and Ownership Structure

6.
Diluted economic ownership: Vodafone's complex corporate structure results in massive cash leakage to minority shareholders at multiple levels. 7.
Severe dividend leakage: The high minority interest in Safaricom creates substantial cash outflows when dividends are declared. Because Safaricom was only consolidated from the second quarter, these massive payments outweighed the newly consolidated operational cash contribution. This leakage directly limits the cash that reaches Vodafone's balance sheet.
8.
Lost associate dividends: Consolidating Safaricom forced Vodafone to eliminate the high-margin associate dividends it previously received directly. Morgan Stanley noted that Vodafone would lose over €0.1 billion of direct dividend inflows from Kenya because the stake is no longer accounted for as an associate. While consolidation increases paper EBITDAaL, it extinguishes these highly valuable cash inflows that previously went straight to parent free cash flow.
9.
Persistent currency depreciation: Safaricom's cash flows are denominated in Kenyan shillings, which have experienced historical volatility against major currencies. The currency has depreciated by an average of 7% annually against the euro over the past three years. This ongoing depreciation dilutes the consolidated euro value of Safaricom's cash distributions to Vodafone.
