EXECUTIVE SUMMARY (BLUF) 31 findings

Christoph Aeschlimann brings a deep technical foundation from software engineering and IT consultancy, holding a Dipl. Ing. degree in Computer Science from EPFL and an MBA from McGill University, culminating in six years as Group CEO at ERNI Group before joining Swisscom as Head of IT, Network & Infrastructure in February 2019. He was appointed CEO of Swisscom Ltd effective June 1, 2022, replacing long-standing chief executive Urs Schaeppi after his 23-year career at the company. The CEO's background reflects an operator-track chief executive with deep technical roots and software leadership credentials, though his experience is heavily weighted toward IT software and network architecture rather than direct B2C consumer commercial operations in legacy telecommunications networks. Management executed a transformational €8.0bn acquisition of Vodafone Italia on December 31, 2024, combining it with Fastweb to create Italy's second-largest convergent operator while outlining €600 million in runrate EBITDAaL synergies by 2029. Group revenue expanded from CHF 11,112 million in FY22 to CHF 15,048 million in FY25, primarily driven by the full consolidation of Vodafone Italia, while reported EBITDAaL increased from CHF 4,120 million in FY22 to CHF 4,984 million in FY25, reflecting lower post-merger margins in Italy, and reported EPS dropped from CHF 30.93 in FY22 to CHF 24.54 in FY25 due to debt-financing costs and front-loaded integration expenses. Swisscom generated a 19% Total Shareholder Return in 2025, outperforming the European telco sector median return of 14%, demonstrating peer-relative outperformance during a period of substantial strategic transformation.

Aeschlimann communicates with plain-spoken clarity, avoiding corporate obfuscation and addressing competitive headwinds in Swiss B2C and B2B telco directly, while demonstrating exceptional decision velocity by executing the transformational €8.0bn acquisition of Vodafone Italia. Demonstrating strong owner mindset alignment, management raised the annual dividend from CHF 22 to CHF 26 for FY25 and guided to CHF 27 for FY26 upon meeting targets, while displaying significant strategic risk appetite by expanding Swisscom's balance sheet exposure to the volatile Italian market, taking Net Debt/EBITDA to 2.4x while preserving an A2 credit rating.

However, the aggressive M&A execution in Italy exposes Swisscom shareholders to severe long-term capital destruction risk if Italian competitive dynamics erode integration synergies and the dividend growth model collapses under the weight of higher leverage. analysts view Chief Executive Christoph Aeschlimann as a credible operator executing well on cost transformation and Italian M&A, but debate whether Swiss price hikes can offset structural domestic erosion, with equity analyst Harrison Williams at Morgan Stanley downgrading Swisscom to Underweight with a CHF 600 target price in August 2026, citing a negative risk-reward profile following stock price strength.

Analyst Robert Grindle at Deutsche Bank Research holds a Hold rating with a CHF 590 target price, viewing the stock as expensive relative to European peers, while research analyst Max Findlay at Rothschild & Co Redburn maintains a Sell recommendation with a CHF 353 target price, highlighting leverage concerns and Italian market headwinds, with the sell-side consensus across the full coverage pool standing at 11% Overweight, 42% Equal-weight/Hold, and 47% Underweight.

Sceptical analysts argue that CEO Christoph Aeschlimann's price increases are merely a temporary fix as customer downtrading accelerates and Italian synergy gains get priced in, threatening a structural top-line decline and valuation de-rating. CFO Eugen Stermetz has served as Group CFO since March 2021, following nine years directing Swisscom's M&A, participations, and treasury operations, and maintained net debt/EBITDA at exactly the 2.4x target limit prescribed by the Swiss Federal Council at YE25.

Management preserved an A2 rating with Moody's and A- with S&P, maintaining one of the highest credit ratings among European telecommunications operators, while Stermetz locked in a 1.84% average interest rate across Group financial liabilities, with a 5.5-year average maturity and 95% fixed-rate coverage. Fastweb + Vodafone CEO Walter Renna took charge of the combined Italian entity on December 31, 2024, after leading Fastweb as CEO since October 2023, and Renna's team delivered €166 million in Italian integration synergies in 1H26, exceeding the run-rate trajectory required for the €300 million FY26 target.

The dual management structure across Switzerland and Italy creates corporate complexity, and if Italian cost integration stalls under Walter Renna, CFO Eugen Stermetz will face rising leverage that threatens the guided dividend increase to CHF 27 per share.

Swisscom's Board of Directors comprises ten non-executive members as of YE25, structured in compliance with the Swiss Code of Best Practice for Corporate Governance, with all ten board members meeting the independence criteria set out in the Swiss Code of Best Practice, having no prior executive roles at Swisscom within three years or material commercial ties. Pursuant to the Federal Telecommunications Enterprises Act, the Swiss Confederation holds 51% of capital and voting rights and appoints a dedicated federal representative, with former Swiss National Bank Vice Chairman Fritz Zurbrügg serving as the designated representative of the Swiss Confederation on the board, and Sandra Lathion-Zweifel and Daniel Münger serving as employee representatives nominated by transfair and syndicom unions. The Swiss Confederation's statutory fifty-one percent controlling stake and direct board appointment right restrict true board independence, as state political priorities regarding universal service and infrastructure employment could override minority shareholder value creation during structural downturns.

Total executive compensation sits in the lowest quartile relative to European telecommunications peers and comparable Swiss Market Index companies, with variable performance-related pay capped at 100% of base salary, preventing excessive risk-taking or outsized short-term bonus payouts. Executives must take at least 25% of variable pay in blocked shares or RSUs, with an option to increase equity settlement up to 50%, while the CEO is required to hold shares worth 2x base salary and Group Executive Committee members must hold 1x base salary. CEO Christoph Aeschlimann's total compensation rose from CHF 1.85 million in 2023 to CHF 2.23 million in 2025, tracking expanded international responsibilities, though the Compensation Committee acknowledged shareholder concerns regarding the absence of a dedicated performance-share LTI framework due to statutory structural constraints. The absence of a multi-year performance share plan based on relative TSR or ROIC creates an overly defensive compensation model, as executives face minimal financial penalties if debt-funded Italian expansion yields subpar long-term returns.

The Swiss Confederation holds a 50.95% majority stake in Swisscom Ltd pursuant to the statutory requirement under the Federal Telecommunications Enterprise Act, with the Swiss Federal Council establishing binding fouryear strategic goals for Swisscom, governing enterprise parameters and capital allocation, and strictly enforcing a maximum net debt/EBITDA limit of 2.4x for the Group. The Swiss Federal Court confirmed a final CHF 72 million COMCO antitrust penalty against Swisscom regarding sports broadcasting rights, Swisscom resolved a multi-year COMCO fiber network investigation by agreeing to transition from P2MP to Point-to-Point architecture, and Swisscom's Fastweb + Vodafone subsidiary issued notice terminating its Master Service Agreement with INWIT effective March 2028. The Swiss Confederation's fifty-one percent stake exposes Swisscom to political interference and potential parliamentary sell-down proposals, and if state re-armament demands trigger a stake reduction, loss of implicit state credit backing could expand borrowing costs.

Swisscom maintains strong internal employee recommendation scores of 90% and 95% talent retention, offsetting friction from customer care and IT headcount rationalization, with a structured social plan via Worklink Ltd and agile operational practices preventing legacy public-monopoly bureaucratic stagnation. However, ongoing headcount reductions in customer care and IT risk eroding service quality and staff morale, and if social plan placement success continues declining below seventy percent, union resistance and brand reputational damage could slow cost transformation initiatives.

Insider activity reflects predictable mandatory equity fee allocations and RSU grants with zero open-market sales, though total executive skin-in-the-game remains low relative to European peers, with directors receiving 1,498 shares worth CHF 0.8 million in 2025 under mandatory fee share rules, and executive committee members receiving 2,842 shares worth CHF 1.5 million in March 2025 as variable compensation. Zero discretionary openmarket share purchases were executed by directors or officers over the L12M period, though no insider openmarket sales were recorded during 2025 or 1H26, confirming zero insider liquidation sentiment.

Complete reliance on mandatory fee allocations rather than voluntary open-market purchases signals passive insider alignment, and without direct personal capital commitments, executive financial skin-in-the-game remains low. Expert network insights reveal strong operational credibility and unmatched brand trust in Swiss enterprise ICT, with industry experts emphasizing that Swisscom's incumbent status and enterprise reliability generate exceptional customer trust across domestic corporate and B2B banking sectors, while former telecommunications executives confirm that Swisscom operates an impeccable network, maintaining strong brand loyalty that supports premium pricing relative to European peers.

However, competitor experts observe that aggressive price discounting by Sunrise and Salt forces cost-conscious consumers toward secondary brands like Wingo, former Italian telecom executives warn that Italy remains one of Europe's most competitive mobile markets where Iliad's low-cost posture caps broad ARPU expansion, and tower industry experts criticize Swisscom's unilateral MSA termination with INWIT as an aggressive cost-cutting tactic that creates ongoing legal and operational friction.

Expert consensus cautions that if Italian price aggression prevents B2C mobile ARPU recovery and INWIT legal disputes trigger costly contractual damages, Swisscom's debt-funded Italian expansion could drag overall group return on invested capital below domestic baseline levels. Christoph Aeschlimann demonstrates high drive and strategic push by executing the transformational €8.0bn acquisition of Vodafone Italia, while exhibiting high agency and decision velocity by restructuring the Group ExCo from nine members down to four within three years.

Aeschlimann shows partial dynamism across differing market environments, excelling in IT software transformation but unproven in retail mobile repair, while capital allocation discipline is moderate, balancing dividend growth with leverage reaching 2.4x Net Debt/EBITDA. Executive communication is plain-spoken and non-evasive, directly addressing domestic ARPU headwinds and customer downtrading, publicly admitting that back-book price increases accelerated customer migration to budget brand Wingo.

Confidence in operator capacity to deliver the 18M-3Y EPS trajectory is rated high plausibility, and if Italian synergies reach €300m in FY26, dividend growth to CHF 27 remains highly achievable. However, if Italian price wars erode mobile ARPU and INWIT litigation triggers costly contractual penalties, Swisscom's debt-funded expansion will compress group ROIC, forcing a dividend cut and de-rating.

Outliers worth noting

Swisscom exhibits three statistical outliers that position the company in the top or bottom ~5% of European telecommunications operators across capital structure, executive compensation philosophy, and state ownership constraints. These anomalies create both defensive moats and structural limitations that directly impact management's strategic flexibility and long-term shareholder value creation potential. The company maintains an A2 credit rating from Moody's, placing it among the highest-rated telecommunications operators in Europe despite executing an €8.0 billion debt-financed acquisition of Vodafone Italia.

This credit rating outlier reflects the company's sovereign backing and demonstrates exceptional balance sheet discipline under CFO Eugen Stermetz, who preserved investment-grade status while expanding leverage to the 2.4x Net Debt/EBITDA ceiling prescribed by federal mandate [Cross-ref: Section 6, Section 9]. The Swiss Federal Council enforces a strict maximum net debt/EBITDA limit of 2.4x for the Group, creating a hard governance constraint that prevents unconstrained managerial expansion but simultaneously limits capital allocation flexibility during strategic windows.

Peer operators including Vodafone Group (Baa2), Telefónica (Baa1), and Orange SA (Baa1) carry materially lower credit ratings, positioning Swisscom's A2 rating as a top-decile outlier within the European telecommunications universe [Cross-ref: Section 2].

salary. CEO Christoph Aeschlimann's total compensation of CHF 2.23 million in 2025 stands materially below peer chief executives at Deutsche Telekom, Orange SA, and Vodafone Group, where total compensation frequently exceeds €5-8 million annually including long-term incentive grants [Cross-ref: Section 8]. This ultraconservative compensation structure eliminates excessive risk-taking incentives but simultaneously reduces long-term equity alignment, particularly given the absence of a dedicated performance-share LTI framework based on relative TSR or ROIC that could drive multi-year value creation [Cross-ref: Section 8].

The compensation outlier directly influences insider ownership dynamics, where combined insider holdings account for less than 0.03% of outstanding share capital, representing a bottom-decile position among European telecommunications incumbents and signaling minimal personal financial skin-in-the-game [Cross-ref: Section 13]. State ownership concentration represents the third critical outlier, with the Swiss Confederation holding a 50.95% majority stake pursuant to the Federal Telecommunications Enterprise Act and the statutory mandate that 51% of capital and voting rights remain under federal control.

This sovereign ownership structure creates a bottom-5% outlier in terms of public float availability, where the 49% public float restricts index liquidity weightings and prevents hostile takeover activity [Cross-ref: Section 7, Section 9]. The federal controlling stake provides implicit credit backing that supports the company's A2 rating outlier but simultaneously exposes management to political interference risk, where the Swiss Federal Council establishes binding four-year strategic goals governing enterprise parameters and capital allocation [Cross-ref: Section 9].

Peer operators including Deutsche Telekom (71.4% float), Orange SA (68.85% float), and Telefónica (69% float) maintain materially higher public ownership, positioning Swisscom's 49% float as a structural outlier that constrains institutional investor participation and index inclusion eligibility [Cross-ref: Section 13].
