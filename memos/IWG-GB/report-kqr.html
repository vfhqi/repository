Section 2 – Landlord Willingness and Agreement Structures

Section summary
  1. Rising fit-out costs and strict debt markets are causing some office landlords to demand fixed leases and corporate guarantees rather than variable revenue-share agreements.
  2. Institutional landlords like Land Securities and British Land prefer developing direct in-house flexible brands over paying partnership fees to external operators.

Capital Allocation and Fit-out Pressures

1.
Systematic analysis: This section evaluates structural shifts in landlord agreements across Europe based on 14 distinct sources, four major real estate companies, and two comparable historical cycles. Traditional leases in London command the highest European medium fit-out costs at 2,668 Euros per square meter, compared to 2,421 Euros in Germany and up to 1,660 Euros in France. High capital costs mean landlords face significant pressure on fit-out spending when negotiating flex structures. This capex strain directly limits IWG's assetlight growth runway.
2.
High concessions: Land Securities represents the institutional preference for long-term leases where landlords absorb substantial upfront costs to secure income predictability. To defend valuations in a 6% yielding office market, Land Securities regularly absorbs an average of two years rent-free on ten-year leases. This concession translates to a net effective yield of 4.8%, highlighting the heavy capital concessions required to maintain traditional leasing occupancy. For IWG, this competitive dynamic means traditional landlords may prefer absorbing known leasing concessions over variable revenue-share managed contracts.

Operator Strategies and Direct Flex Models

3.
Scaled flexibility: Workspace Group operates a specialized flexible model in London utilizing a two-year standard lease with a three-month rent deposit and six-month rolling breaks. This flexible structure includes an in-built 5% rental increase in the second year to support organic cash flow. The group maintains direct operational control of assets rather than relying on third-party managers like IWG. This direct model allows Workspace to capture the full operational margin, indicating that some landlords prefer in-house flex operations to paying external operator fees.
4.
Targeted partnership: Workspace Group demonstrated a selective capital-light partnership by signing a 20-year index-linked lease with arts operator Qube at the Old Dairy. The agreement involved a 3 million pound equity stake alongside a 3.45 million pound landlord contribution to fund bespoke fit-out. This structural departure shows landlords will fund targeted fit-outs only when secured by long-term leases and strategic equity integration. This selective structure suggests that pure fee-on-revenue models without corporate commitments face high resistance from institutional landlords.
5.
Structural tension: An expert interview with a former regional Chief Financial Officer at IWG indicates that institutional landlords strongly prefer conventional leases to secure predictable yields. Conventional rent models provide guaranteed cash flows that landlords can back-to-back with investor return expectations. Conversely, capital-light agreements shift property risks to the landlord, meaning the landlord bears the downside of variable occupancy while paying fees to IWG. This structural tension can restrict IWG's contract pipeline as regional financing markets tighten.
6.
Contrary evidence: A co-working industry expert notes that institutional landlords generally prefer not to be heavily vested in co-working models because of elevated operational risks. Debt markets have become increasingly restrictive, making it highly difficult for landlords to secure financing for speculative flex conversions. Landlords consequently view local or regional operators as carrying excessive operational liabilities compared to traditional credit tenants. This funding squeeze suggests landlords are shifting back toward demanding fixed rents and corporate guarantees rather than capital-light agreements.

Competitive Dynamics and Historical Precedents

7.
Premium premiums: British Land successfully achieves premiums of over 20% ahead of traditional net effective rents through its Storey flexible workspace brand. Storey currently operates across 374,000 square feet, representing 5% of the group's campus portfolio. Land Securities is deploying a similar strategy, absorbing near-term start-up costs to expand its premium Myo flexible space offering. These in-house brands demonstrate that prime landlords prefer direct flex models over partnerships, which reduces the addressable market for IWG's fee-based platform.
8.
Cyclical vulnerability: Historical downturns demonstrate that flexible leasing structures experience severe pricing pressure when tenant demand softens. During the GFC and the COVID-19 pandemic, Workspace Group was forced to discount rents by 10% to 12% to protect its occupancy levels. During the height of the pandemic in 2021, Workspace's occupancy dropped to 82.1% as short-term lease incentives reduced rental income by 5.3%. This historical precedent indicates that flexible agreements expose landlords to immediate downside during economic recessions, which increases their insistence on fixed rents and corporate parent guarantees.
9.
Other side of the story: While some institutional landlords are launching proprietary flex brands, high vacancy rates across secondary markets force many property owners to accept capital-light agreements to avoid empty spaces. Average office vacancies have risen to 8% across major European hubs, which is beyond the tipping point where traditional rents begin to decline. Landlords facing persistent vacancy may have no choice but to partner with IWG's established platform to generate operational income. This distress suggests the window for IWG's fee-on-revenue model remains open in oversupplied markets.

Section 3 – Capital-Light Signings and Financial Targets

Section summary
  1. IWG is successfully hitting its intermediate recurring management fee targets as capital-light signings scale rapidly.
  2. A widening triangulation gap caused by low introductory RevPAR on new room openings could pressure full-year managed fee yields.

Record Openings and Pipeline Drivers

1.
Substantial growth: IWG achieved a major expansion of its capital-light footprint by signing 711 new centre deals in the first half of 2026. This operational acceleration represents a 72% increase in new deals compared to the 413 deals signed in the prior period. The record performance underscores the strong momentum of IWG's transition toward its capital-light partner model.
2.
Expanding pipeline: The company maintained a massive forward pipeline of 257,000 signed but not yet open rooms at the end of the period. This forward inventory represents 31% growth in room pipeline compared to the 196,000 rooms in the previous year's pipeline. Once fully open and mature, these locations are expected to generate more than 2 billion dollars in annual system-wide revenue.
3.
Escalating revenues: IWG reported that its total partner system revenue grew by 36% to 535 million dollars in the first half of 2026. This growth helped drive a 60% increase in total fee revenue to 80 million dollars from 50 million dollars previously. The acceleration proves that IWG's platform-focused strategy is successfully scaling fee streams from landlord-funded locations.

Target Feasibility and Revenue Flows

4.
Meeting projections: IWG generated 35 million dollars in recurring managed fee income during the first half of 2026. This represents an 84% increase from 19 million dollars in the first half of 2025 and matches the company's long-term targets. The actual result aligns perfectly with the 35 million dollar interim target established during the company's December 2025 presentation.
5.
Direct analysis: The company's full-year target of 80 million dollars in recurring managed fee income appears highly achievable based on current performance. The target requires achieving 45 million dollars in the second half of 2026, building upon the 35 million dollars secured in the first half. Strong occupancy and expanding locations should support recurring managed fee target achievement as newly signed rooms mature.
6.
Contrary evidence: Rapid pipeline openings are causing a significant drag on average fees, as managed RevPAR fell by 9% to 164 dollars. This is substantially lower than the 232 dollar mature managed RevPAR that excludes recent 2025 and 2026 openings. The resulting triangulation gap between 36% system revenue growth and immature room yields could prevent the company from hitting its 45 million dollar second-half fee target.
7.
Operational flow: IWG calculated its fee revenue as a 14.95% (calculated) share of its 535 million dollar partner system revenue during the first half of 2026. This percentage represents an increase from the 12.76% (calculated) fee share achieved in the first half of 2025, when fee revenue was 50 million dollars. This rising flow-through demonstrates that mature centres generate superior fee margins for the group over time.
8.
Other side of the story: While the capital-light fee model shows strong growth, IWG remains vulnerable to sudden pipeline attrition as signed rooms that fail to open within two years are now actively purged from the pipeline. If macroeconomic challenges cause landlords to delay conversions, the forward pipeline of 257,000 rooms may fail to yield expected fees of over 2 billion dollars annually. This dependency means that achieving long-term targets remains tied to regional real estate market conditions.
