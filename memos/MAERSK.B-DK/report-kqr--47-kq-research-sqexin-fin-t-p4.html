Section 2 – Tax: Tonnage Tax Mechanics and Loss Year Behavior

Section summary
  1. Tonnage tax regimes isolate shipping earnings from standard corporate tax rates, creating highly volatile effective tax rates as operating profits decline.
  2. Historical evidence shows that Maersk faces substantial absolute tax charges during loss years due to stable tonnage tax liabilities and land-based corporate taxes.
  3. Peer comparisons reveal that asset-heavy container carriers enjoy exceptionally low effective tax rates during peak cycles but lack the tax flexibility of asset-light logistics providers in downturns.

Maersk Historical Tax Dynamics

1.
Volatile tax outcomes: Maersk experienced a massive disconnect between tax charges and earnings during the severe shipping downturn of FY2016. According to the DNB Carnegie broker report dated 7 February 2024, Maersk posted a pre-tax loss of USD 714 mm but incurred an absolute tax charge of USD 1.05 bn. This mismatch was driven by fixed tonnage tax liabilities and means that loss years do not provide tax relief, resulting in a negative effective tax rate of 148% that heavily depressed earnings per share. Moderate tax rates: Maersk saw its tax charge stabilize in FY2019 as operating profits recovered from the previous downturn. The company reported a profit before tax of USD 967 mm for FY2019 while paying total taxes of USD 458 mm. This absolute tax charge represented an effective tax rate of 47% because of resolved historical tax disputes and higher tax liabilities in local jurisdictions.
2.
Low tax rates: Maersk paid a very low effective tax rate in FY2023 despite generating substantial profits across its shipping segments. The company's tax charge for the fiscal year ended 31 December 2023 was USD 454 mm against a pre-tax profit of USD 4.36 bn. According to the Maersk 2023 Tax Report, this absolute charge consisted of USD 215 mm in corporate income tax on land-based activities and USD 239 mm in Ocean tonnage taxes, demonstrating how tonnage taxation buffers peak earnings from high corporate tax rates.

Segment Tax Mechanics

3.
Inflexible ocean taxation: Maersk's Ocean segment remains subject to a fixed tax charge even when operating profits fall to zero or turn negative. During the historical shipping slump of 2017, Maersk posted an Ocean segment loss of USD 105 mm but still paid USD 11 mm in tax. This highlights how tonnage tax is calculated on fleet capacity rather than operating earnings, which increases EPS downside risk in low-profit years. 42 43 - Analyst/Investor Standard corporate taxation: Maersk's Terminals and Logistics segments are subject to ordinary corporate income taxes rather than the tonnage tax regime. The company notes in its FY2025 Annual Report that profits from land-based activities, including gateway ports and forwarding solutions, are taxed at standard national corporate rates. This segment mix means that as Ocean profits fall toward zero, ordinary tax charges remain sticky because the profitable land-based segments continue to face standard corporate tax rates.
4.
Contrary evidence: some tax regulations under OECD Pillar Two rules might actually increase the tax burden on shipping logistics operations during loss years. In its Annual Report 2025 published on 4 February 2026, Maersk warned that top-up taxes could be triggered by shipping classification under GloBE rules even in years where shipping net income is negative. This indicates that negative shipping results could trigger taxes, contradicting the assumption that tonnage taxes are the only fixed liability during downturns.

This extremely low rate was driven by international shipping profits that fall under tonnage taxation, meaning peak shipping profits escape high taxes but leave the company exposed to flat tax bills during downcycles. 51 52 / CSR reports Severe historical tax drag: Hapag-Lloyd experienced a massive tax drag during the historical shipping downcycle spanning from 2009 to 2020. The company's Sustainability Report 2023 discloses that Hapag-Lloyd paid EUR 383 mm in income taxes on cumulative pre-tax earnings of only EUR 959 mm. This resulted in a cumulative effective tax burden of 40%, demonstrating that tonnage taxation severely penalizes operators during prolonged low-profit periods because taxes are levied based on fleet capacity rather than actual earnings.
6.
Variable Chinese taxation: COSCO Shipping Holdings operates under a more variable tax regime compared to the tonnage tax schemes of European peers. The carrier reported an income tax expense of RMB 5.83 bn in its Q3 2026 report, resulting in an effective tax rate of 14.3% on pre-tax profits of RMB 40.77 bn. This highlights how COSCO's tax expense fluctuates with actual earnings because China's enterprise income tax rate of 25% is applied directly to taxable profits, rather than using a fixed fleet-capacity calculation.
7.
Direct minimum taxes: COSCO Shipping Holdings and Hapag-Lloyd face different structural impacts from the global implementation of OECD Pillar Two minimum tax rules. While COSCO notes in its 2025 Annual Report that international shipping income is generally exempt from the GloBE 15% minimum tax, the group still expects complex local filings to monitor ancillary revenues. Similarly, Hapag-Lloyd AG expects its core shipping activities to be shielded by international transport exemptions, meaning that Pillar Two will not significantly raise the tax burden of large ocean liner fleets.
8.
High logistics taxation: DSV operates under an asset-light forwarding model that results in a much higher and more stable effective tax rate than shipping peers. The logistics provider reported an effective tax rate of 28.8% for the first nine months of 2026, which was temporarily elevated due to non-deductible integration costs from the DB Schenker acquisition. This compares to Maersk's low peak-cycle tax rates and demonstrates that logistics segments face higher taxation than shipping segments, which will keep Maersk's group ETR elevated as Ocean earnings fall.

Section 4 – Depreciation: Vessel Life Policy and Impairment Headroom

Section summary
  1. Accounting modifications to vessel useful lives provide Maersk with a significant non-cash earnings buffer of USD 700 mm annually.
  2. We have high confidence in this depreciation reduction because it is fully documented in Maersk's 2026 financial reports.
  3. We have medium confidence regarding impairment risks as the Ocean segment headroom has contracted to USD 1.1 bn and remains highly sensitive to volatile freight rates.

Accounting Policy and Depreciation Benefits

1.
Positive accounting shift: Maersk adjusted its accounting estimates by increasing the estimated useful lives of its vessels from 20 to 25 years starting on 1 January 2026. This shift was implemented prospectively following an annual review of asset longevity completed at the end of 2025. The transition reflects the reality that modern vessels remain economically viable for longer periods than historically assumed. This policy change directly reduces the annual non-cash drag on profitability.
2.
Substantial depreciation relief: The extension of vessel useful lives is expected to reduce Maersk's depreciation expenses by approximately USD 700 mm in FY2026. During the first nine months of 2026, this policy change resulted in a cumulative depreciation reduction of USD 527 mm. This accounting relief lowers the operating breakeven threshold for the Ocean segment as freight rates normalize. The resulting boost directly protects Maersk's reported earnings per share during cyclical downswings.
3.
Analyst view on policy: Bernstein Research reports that Maersk's underlying financial guidance for FY2026 was significantly supported by this accounting policy adjustment. The broker notes that without the USD 700 mm depreciation benefit, the projected EBIT range would have been USD -2.2 bn to USD 300 mm instead of the guided USD -1.5 bn to USD 1 bn. This highlights that accounting modifications rescue reported profitability during periods of intense market overcapacity. Such non-cash adjustments do not, however, alter the underlying cash flow dynamics.
4.
Quarterly earnings buffer: Maersk's reported EBIT of USD 935 mm in Q2 2026 was buoyed by a USD 175 mm quarterly benefit from the extended vessel lives. A similar depreciation reduction of USD 176 mm was recorded in Q3 2026, maintaining the steady earnings buffer. These accounting gains offset rising operational costs associated with vessel reroutings around the Cape of Good Hope. This accounting support helps Maersk maintain positive operating momentum.

Ocean Segment Impairment Testing and Headroom

5.
Declining headroom safety: The Maersk FY2025 annual report revealed that the impairment headroom for its core Ocean cash-generating unit dropped to USD 1.1 bn. This safety buffer represents a steep decline from the USD 4.1 bn of headroom reported at the end of FY2024. This contraction indicates that recoverable asset values are narrowing toward their carrying book values. Any further deterioration in long-term freight rates could trigger non-cash write-downs.
6.
Stable discount parameters: The impairment testing for Maersk's Ocean segment in FY2025 applied a pre-tax discount rate of 9.8%. This discount rate is highly consistent with the 9.9% pre-tax rate utilized in the previous fiscal year. This stability means that discount rate adjustments are unlikely to be the primary catalyst for asset impairments. Instead, the risk remains heavily concentrated in operational cash flow assumptions.
7.
Red Sea routing impact: Maersk's impairment projections are highly sensitive to freight-rate developments, which depend on global supply-demand balances and routing disruptions. While the temporary rerouting of vessels around the Cape of Good Hope has absorbed excess capacity, an eventual return to normal Suez transits will lower rates. This risk is rising because shorter routings release vessel capacity and accelerate market oversupply. Consequently, a normalization of maritime routes could eliminate the remaining Ocean headroom.

Peer Comparisons and Fleet Dynamics

8.
Peer depreciation standards: Hapag-Lloyd similarly applies a standard economic useful life of 25 years for its owned container vessels. Furthermore, the company allows for individual vessels to be depreciated over an extended useful life of up to 35 years based on efficiency. This policy demonstrates that long vessel lifetimes are industry-standard among leading integrated ocean carriers. Maersk's transition to 25 years aligns its accounting with these competitive peers.
9.
Shared fleet operational profiles: Hapag-Lloyd maintained a total fleet of 300 container ships with a capacity of 2.5 million TEU as of June 2026. Based on TEU capacity, Hapag-Lloyd owned 60% of its fleet, with the remaining portion operated under charters. This owned-to-chartered ratio means that both operators face identical challenges regarding decarbonization and fleet renewal costs. These capital demands will dictate depreciation trends for years to come.
10.
Oversupply risk pressures: Morgan Stanley Research warns that a severe oversupply in 2027 could pressure freight rates below operating breakeven levels. The broker highlights that scrapping remains minimal despite an aging global fleet, which keeps obsolete vessels active and limits the offset from new deliveries. This structural oversupply means freight rates could face prolonged pressure as the market normalizes. Under these conditions, the probability of impairment for high-value shipping assets rises significantly.
11.
Climate policy asset impacts: The implementation of strict new environmental regulations could shorten the economic viability of older shipping vessels. Hapag-Lloyd explicitly reviews vessel useful lives and residual values annually to assess the economic impact of fleet decarbonization. This means that climate regulations could accelerate obsolescence, contradicting the assumption of standard 25-year lifetimes. Consequently, environmental mandates could force sudden depreciation accelerations or asset write-downs.
12.
Contrary evidence: Despite structural overcapacity fears, Jefferies Research suggests that a meaningful global scrapping cycle may not emerge until 2028. The broker notes that many older vessels were rechartered for multiple years during the Red Sea crisis and cannot be economically withdrawn yet. This delayed scrapping means oversupply could persist for longer than expected, increasing downcycle asset risks. This counterpoint suggests that depreciation relief alone cannot fully shield Maersk from prolonged asset-value declines.
13.
Deficient scrapping incentives: The commercial incentive to scrap older container vessels has been severely weakened by low demolition prices. Because shipowners can still generate marginal profits from operating older tonnage in congested lanes, they choose to delay retirement. This retention of older vessels means that fleet average ages remain high across the container shipping industry. This structural dynamic delays the market-clearing scrapping cycle necessary to restore freight rates.
14.
Other side of the story: Maersk's transition to a 25-year vessel useful life policy might actually prove conservative if vessel-sharing alliances optimize fleet utilization. 145 - Earnings Calls The Gemini Cooperation between Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd, which sustains schedule reliability above 90%, is expected to maximize asset efficiency and extend vessel lifespans. This superior operational reliability implies that vessel economic lives could exceed the new 25-year threshold. Under this scenario, Maersk's impairment risks would be mitigated, and long-term depreciation expenses could remain lower than sellside models forecast.

Consequently, net profit and reported EPS will decline much faster than sell-side models currently project.
