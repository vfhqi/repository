[Materiality of] Investment Thesis (BB 1) Rating = D

Thesis. Diploma is a first-rank 'Compounder' whose shares have already been paid for the year that will not repeat. At 7,105p and 25.5x earnings 24 months forward, the next stage of the case, mid-single-digit earnings growth in FY2027 as one unit's supernormal margin is given back by choice and then 10 to 11 percent a year from the acquisition engine, returns about 10 to 11 percent a year on our modal path against the framework's 20 percent bar. The 20 percent case reappears at about 5,500p, or on evidence by January 2027 that FY2027 is not the deceleration year the company itself describes.

  • The change that made the case was real, broad and is now mature.
    • Since 2019 the company has doubled its size through about £1.6bn of acquisitions at an average of 8x EBIT, moved about half of its revenue into structurally growing niches (aerospace, defence, data centres, nuclear and water), and lifted its adjusted operating margin from the high teens to a guided 26.5 percent for FY2026.
    • In the last eighteen months the change travelled the whole 'Six Layer Inputs-outputs' chain, which is why it is no longer a change we can buy ahead of.
    • Resources: a chief executive in post since February 2019 and a permanent chief financial officer confirmed on 17 December 2025.
    • Choices: a resourced pivot into structural niches and a 4,000-target acquisition pipeline
      • the long-term model deliberately held at 5 percent organic growth and 10 percent total compounding.
    • Actions: 15 acquisitions for about £310m in the twelve months to July 2026, a record, including CDM ($170m, completed 25 June 2026), the first US land-based defence platform.
    • Operating metrics: organic growth of 14 percent in Q1, 15 percent in H1 and 15 percent at Q3 of FY2026, with the business outside Peerless growing 10 percent.
    • Financial statements: FY2026 operating profit guided up a little over 40 percent
      • earnings per share on the brokers' basis up about 45 percent, from £1.75 to £2.54.
    • Sell-side estimates: consensus FY2026 earnings per share up 37 percent in twelve months
      • eleven of eleven brokers raised over six months
      • guidance raised three times in one fiscal year (18 March, 19 May and 16 July 2026).
  • The share price travelled with it, and has now stopped.
    • The shares rose from 5,030p on 17 March 2026 to 6,835p on 19 May and 7,105p on 15 September 2026, a 41.3 percent rise over six months that tracked the three upgrades.
    • Over the last three months the return is minus 0.4 percent while the July upgrade added another 7 percent to FY2026 consensus, so the price has stopped responding to upgrades at the margin.
    • That is the price telling us the upgrades are in.
  • The outputs from here improve, but at a rate that does not reach the framework's band, because the margin gives back what one unit lent it.
    • FY2026 is, in management's own words, an abnormal year: the 26.5 percent margin is "the top end of sustainable", Peerless's spot pricing is being managed down to convert spot buyers into contract customers, and the company does not expect 15 percent organic growth to repeat.
    • Our modal path (brokers' basis): earnings per share of £2.54 in FY2026 (consensus), £2.68 in FY2027 (+5.5 percent), £2.94 in FY2028 (+9.7 percent), £3.26 in FY2029 (+10.9 percent), £3.61 in FY2030 and £4.03 in FY2031
      • 8.7 percent a year over the three years to FY2029.
    • Consensus is lower: £2.65 for FY2027 (+4.1 percent) and £2.74 for FY2028 (+3.7 percent), because it models announced deals only
      • we add £300m a year of acquisitions at 8x EBIT, worth about 7 percent of EBIT a year, and take the margin down as far as the median broker does (25.0 percent in FY2027 against consensus 24.9 percent).
    • The composition is the less-coveted kind: revenue growth of about 12 percent a year (6.5 percent of it acquired) offset by a margin contraction worth minus 2.6 percent a year and a rising interest line worth minus 0.7 percent, with no help from the share count.
  • The price already assumes the step-change and a gentle normalisation, and it pays a serial-acquirer premium for some of the deals the median consensus omits.
    • At 25.5x consensus earnings 24 months forward (£2.7847) the shares sit at the 66th percentile of their own five-year range (18.83x to 34.09x; median 24.27x), the 65th percentile of ten years, and the third-highest multiple among the 41 dashboard peers with a value (Addtech 26.9x and Lagercrantz 26.1x above; the sector median about 15.8x)
    • the cohort peer Halma is at 24.6x.
    • What is not priced is the acquisition engine at its demonstrated pace, a Peerless margin that holds, or a Peerless margin that falls to 35 percent within the year.
  • On the framework's valuation convention the return is about 11 percent a year over eighteen months and 10 percent over three years, a D.
    • At each horizon we value the shares at a multiple of the earnings two years beyond it: £3.43 at eighteen months (the average of our FY2029 and FY2030) at 24.0x, and £4.03 at three years (our FY2031) at 23.0x.
    • Value plus dividends of £83.4 at eighteen months (+17.4 percent, 11.3 percent a year) and £94.8 at three years (+33.4 percent, 10.1 percent a year).
    • The re-rating is the swing factor: a held 25.5x gives 15.9 and 13.9 percent a year, a C and not a B
    • the plausibly worse drop-through from EBITDA to earnings per share gives 4.7 and 6.8 percent a year, an F.
  • The chain holds at every link but the last.
    • The forces are strong, the outputs have arrived, the transmission from guidance to earnings per share is clear, and the pattern fits.
    • It breaks at the return, because the price has captured the upgrade cycle and the year ahead is one of 4 to 6 percent consensus earnings growth against a 66th-percentile multiple.

  • The case is therefore a first-rank compounder to want at a lower price, not to chase at this one.

    • On the modal path the case clears the 20 percent bar at about 5,500p on the three-year test (23 percent below the current price, about where the shares stood in April 2026 between the March and May upgrades) and at about 6,350p on the eighteen-month test.

  • Rating = D, because the expected change from here is not material enough to deliver the 20 percent a year the 'Investment Thesis' asks for: the input forces were strong and have already travelled every layer of the 'Six Layer Inputs-outputs' chain into the accounts and the sell-side's estimates, the financial outputs from here improve at 9 to 11 percent a year with the margin giving back, and the modal return is 10 to 11 percent a year.

    • The parent diverges from its first Element ('Change Forces' at B) because materiality is measured on what is expected, not on what has happened.
    • Not C, because the return, which is the Building Block's own test, is a D on the modal path and a C only if the multiple holds through a deceleration year, a branch we put at about 42 percent.
    • Not F, because the business is delivering above its own model and the acquisition engine gives a credible route to the band's floor.

  • Most plausible competing hypothesis, and our response.

  • The competing case is the sell-side majority's, "better for longer": Peerless holds a 40 to 45 percent margin because aerospace supply tightness persists for years, the business outside Peerless compounds at 8 to 10 percent organic, £600m of capacity is deployed at 10 to 14 percent accretion, the group margin holds at 26 percent, FY2027 earnings per share grow 12 to 15 percent rather than 4, and the multiple holds at 25 to 26x because the safe-haven status and the Halma comparison justify it
    • that gives a return of 20 to 25 percent a year and a B.
  • We concede the quality, the record and the capacity, and that this branch is a genuine 30 percent, not a tail.
  • We hold our view because the company is the witness against it: management says FY2026 is abnormal, that the margin is at the top end of sustainable, that Peerless is being priced down by choice and will settle at a high-single-digit growth rate, and it refuses to raise a 5 percent organic model
    • the next platform deal, CDM, is dilutive at 17 to 18 percent margins
    • and consensus already carries the margin normalisation.
  • The bull branch needs two conditions to hold at once through the toughest comparatives in the company's history: a Peerless margin at or above 45 percent through FY2027 (possible, about 40 percent) and organic growth outside Peerless at or above 8 percent through FY2027 (plausible, about 55 percent).
  • The two are correlated through the same aerospace and industrial cycle, so the joint odds sit above their product of 22 percent
    • we put the branch at about 30 percent, because both conditions are being told to us by the company as things that will moderate and the base rate for a distributor's organic growth is the 5.7 percent fifteen-year average.
  • That is not the modal case, and it is why the 'Investment Thesis' is a D and not a C.
Change Forces: are the input change forces sufficiently strong? (E 1, BB 1) Rating = B
  • Rating = B, because the internal forces are strong on four of the twelve screen criteria (guidance raised three times in FY2026; an acquisition step-up to a record 15 deals; a completed chief financial officer succession with four divisional leadership changes; a resourced strategic pivot into structural niches) and the external forces in aerospace, defence and data centres are structural tailwinds with multi-year visibility.
  • Propagation: the strongest of these forces have already travelled fully to the outputs and are now moderating: Peerless spot pricing is being managed down, and management describes FY2026 as an abnormal year.
  • Headwinds: two external forces run the other way, European and UK industrial demand and sterling translation.
  • Not A: the forces are at their strongest behind us, not ahead, and two of the seven external forces are headwinds now.
External forces: are there sufficiently strong external forces acting on the company, new or intensifying, and in which direction? (RA 1, E 1, BB 1) Rating = B
  • Rating = B, because two sector-level demand forces are strong and structural in the company's favour (civil aerospace, with order books of 10 to 12 years, and defence budgets rising; hyperscaler data-centre construction), a third is cyclical and favourable (US infrastructure, nuclear and water), and competition and disruption are easing or contained for a specialist model.
  • Direction by force: demand is a tailwind in aerospace, defence, data centres and US infrastructure and a headwind in UK and European industrial and Canadian public healthcare
    • competition is a tailwind (broadline peers weak, specialist share gains)
    • disruption is neutral to a slight tailwind (contained)
    • political and regulatory is a tailwind on tariffs (local-for-local sourcing) and a headwind on PFAS
    • the supply side is mixed (normalisation eases inventory strain but ends the Peerless spot premium)
    • the company's own cycle is turning down from a peak year
    • macro is a headwind (sterling translation, European stagnation).
  • Not A: the aerospace supply tightness that drove Peerless's margins above 50 percent is the part of the force that is now easing, a headwind for the margin even as volume holds, and the European and UK industrial cycle and sterling are headwinds now.
Demand: is end-market demand changing, structurally or cyclically (a swing with two-plus years of visibility)? (KQ 1, RA 1, E 1, BB 1) Rating = B
  • Rating = B, because end-market demand is changing structurally in the company's favour in the markets that now make up about half of group revenue, with visibility beyond two years, while the other half is flat to down, so the group-level picture is polarised rather than uniformly inflecting.
  • Aerospace: civil aviation order books span 10 to 12 years across OEMs and maintenance networks
    • short-term organic growth is capped by supply constraints, not demand.
    • A commercial airframe needs up to 400,000 fasteners, a wide-body one million.
  • Defence: exposure rises from 3 percent to about 7 percent of revenue pro forma for CDM (completed 25 June 2026, $170m) and Spring Solutions
    • management calls it a non-cyclical volume driver and is building a Czech facility for Eastern European defence customers.
  • Data centres: Windy City Wire's low-voltage cabling grew from a standing start to $40m of annual revenue in three years, and data centres went from zero to 10 percent of its revenue
    • hyperscaler capital commitments are secured for the next few years
    • data centres are a single-digit percentage of group revenue.
    • Rexel's North American data-centre sales rose over 100 percent and Wesco's about 45 percent in Q2 2026.
  • Structural share: aerospace, data centres and clean energy are about half of group revenue and add a 1 to 2 percent structural tailwind to organic growth.
  • The soft half: International Seals declined 1 percent organically in H1 FY2026 on UK and European manufacturing weakness, with only "early signs of stabilisation" at Q3
    • North American OEM Seals face customer destocking
    • Life Sciences grew 4 percent through a product refresh, with Canadian public healthcare "highly challenging".
  • Not A: the 'Demand Inflection' test (sector-level, structural, evidenced by order books and customer capital spending) is met for aerospace, defence and data centres but not for the group as a whole.
Revenue and demand cycle: where is the company's own cycle, and is it turning? (KQ 6, RA 1, E 1, BB 1) Rating = C
  • Rating = C, because the company's own cycle is at a peak year and is turning down in growth rate, not in level: FY2026 carries 14 percent organic growth guided against a 5 percent long-term model and a margin of about 26.5 percent against a 20 percent-plus model, and management says FY2026 is abnormal, does not expect 15 percent organic growth to repeat in FY2027, and expects Peerless to settle back to a high-single-digit growth rate.
  • The year's path: organic growth 14 percent in Q1, 15 percent in H1, 15 percent at Q3 of FY2026
    • guidance 14 percent for the year.
  • Outside Peerless: organic growth of 10 percent for the year
    • Jefferies models 6.4 percent organic growth for FY2027.
  • The base it turns down to: the long-term organic average is 5.7 percent over fifteen years and 7 percent over seven
    • the group has exceeded its 5 percent target in 15 of the past 20 years
    • organic revenue turned negative only twice in fifteen years, minus 12 percent in FY2009 and minus 7 percent in FY2020.
  • Not D: the base the cycle turns down to sits above the company's own model, and the business outside Peerless is growing 10 percent.
  • For the case: this is the single most important external fact for the year ahead, because the comparatives it creates are what turn a 45 percent earnings year into a 4 to 6 percent one.
Supply side: input costs, availability, concentration? (KQ 5, RA 1, E 1, BB 1) Rating = C
  • Rating = C, because the supply-side force cuts both ways: normalising component supply chains ease inventory pressure and lead times, a modest tailwind for working capital, but the aerospace fastener supply tightness that made Peerless's spot pricing possible is the very thing now easing, and management is managing spot prices down.
  • Peerless margins: went above 50 percent from 35 percent at acquisition on aerospace supply tightness
    • management expects a structurally higher floor of 35 to 40 percent
    • spot is about 30 percent of Peerless volume.
  • Lead times: critical industrial components improved 5 to 10 percent sequentially
    • supply-chain normalisation has reduced inventory pressures across the sector.
  • Supplier attrition: the loss of a key Nordic supplier in FY2025 showed that regional operations are vulnerable to it.
  • Not D: input-cost inflation passes through easily, because components are a negligible share of the customer's cost, and the supplier base is fragmented (more than 6,000 direct suppliers, none above 6 percent of revenue).
Competition: is competitive intensity easing or tightening? (KQ 2, RA 1, E 1, BB 1) Rating = B
  • Rating = B, because competitive intensity is easing for the specialist model relative to broadline distributors, which are losing volume and margin in the industrial downcycle while Diploma gains share.
  • Broadline peers: RS Group ran organic growth of about 0 percent and a 9.2 percent operating margin in the year to 31 March 2026 and launched a £100m buyback rather than acquiring
    • Rexel and Fastenal had six consecutive quarters of organic declines through the downcycle.
  • Relative growth: Diploma's organic growth was 15 percent in H1 and at Q3 FY2026 against a distribution peer median of about 5 to 6 percent.
  • Industry conduct: value-add distributors as a class refuse to compete on price and exit low-margin agreements
    • the industry's family owners are content with local footprints and consolidation has slowed, which leaves the buyer-of-choice acquirer with less competition for targets.
  • Not A: the easing is relative (weak peers), not an absolute reduction in rivals, and a rapid private-equity consolidation of middle-market distribution could produce scaled rivals with superior digital tools.
Political and regulatory: is a change moving demand, pricing or cost? (KQ 4, RA 1, E 1, BB 1) Rating = B

Macroeconomic: is a macro force acting on this company more than on the universe? (KQ 7, RA 1, E 1, BB 1) Rating = C
  • Rating = C, because two macro forces act on the company more than on the universe, both as headwinds: sterling translation, with over 80 percent of revenue earned outside the UK, and European industrial stagnation (International Seals, DICSA, R&G).
  • Translation: FY2025 translation cut revenue by £33.7m and adjusted operating profit by £9.0m
    • H1 FY2026 by £17.9m and £7.6m.

  • Not D: about 85 percent of purchases are matched in the country of sale, so the exposure is translational (about 95 percent of the calculated sensitivity) rather than economic, and an easing rate environment lowers the refinancing cost of the acquisition engine.
Disruption: is a disruption force strengthening or being disproved? (KQ 3, RA 1, E 1, BB 1) Rating = B
  • Rating = B, because the disruption forces named (e-commerce marketplaces, direct-to-customer bypass by manufacturers, additive manufacturing, AI-enabled sourcing) are being disproved in practice for low-volume, high-complexity, certified components, and the company's own risk register treats them as long-term.
  • Digital ceiling: broadline MRO distributors face a digital penetration ceiling of about 60 percent
    • specialists run a much lower digital mix because the model is high-touch custom intervention.
  • OEM behaviour: OEMs refuse to handle low-volume custom kitting
    • a former Hercules OEM president says the value lies in taking an unknown commodity and custom-packaging it.
  • Not A: the register itself names AI-democratised technical knowledge and affordable 3D printing as live long-term risks, and the group's technology posture is deliberately incremental.
