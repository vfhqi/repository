If terminal cash flows remain robust, Maersk may successfully maintain its dividend policy and avoid severe dividend cuts even if container freight rates collapse.

Section 2 – Vessel Orderbook and Fleet Capex Payment Schedules

Section summary
  1. Maersk's massive fleet expansion of 26 LNG dual-fuel vessels will require substantial capital outlays that are highly back-loaded toward deliveries in 2029 and 2030.
  2. The group's total capital commitments of USD 14.6 billion as of Q2 2026 represent a significant financial obligation compared to peers like Hapag-Lloyd and ONE.
  3. Contractual structures and typical shipyard milestones provide some short-term spending flexibility, but long-term capital commitments remain structurally rigid.

Capital Commitments and Orderbook Scale

1.
Aggressive expansion: Maersk has shifted its strategy from strict fleet caps to active capacity growth by placing a major order for 26 LNG dual-fuel container vessels. A Maersk spokesperson confirmed in September 2026 that these ships will have an individual capacity of 18,600 TEU and are scheduled for delivery across 2029 and 2030. This massive order represents an aggregate capacity of approximately 483,600 TEU to be integrated into its global network. Investors must watch this buildout because it represents a massive shift in fleet growth strategy.
2.
Elevated obligations: Maersk reported total capital commitments of USD 14.6 billion in its Q2 2026 interim financial statements, up from USD 13.4 billion at the end of 2025. The group indicated that these commitments are primarily tied to investments in its dual-fuel vessel program, terminal concession agreements, and EU allowance future contracts. This rising capital commitment profile will restrict the group's future financial flexibility as the shipping cycle normalizes. The persistent capital drain will directly impact the cash available for future share buybacks and dividends.
3.
Sizable forward pipeline: Alphaliner figures published in October 2026 indicate that Maersk's existing vessel orderbook has reached approximately 1.21 million TEU, excluding the newly confirmed tonnage. Bernstein Research reports that this aggressive ordering activity has pushed Maersk's total fleet and orderbook capacity back above its near rival CMA CGM. This expansionist strategy contradicts previous management statements that aimed to maintain a steady-state cellular capacity of 4.1 million to 4.3 million TEU. The increased orderbook exposure threatens to exacerbate the looming global container oversupply through 2030.
4.
Unconfirmed shipyard allocation: Industry reports indicate that Maersk split its multi-billion dollar vessel order between Chinese yards Hengli Heavy Industries and New Times Shipbuilding. Shipbroking sources estimate that the total contract value of the 26-vessel program stands at approximately USD 5 billion, though Maersk has not officially confirmed this figure. This structural shift highlights that Maersk is bypassing traditional South Korean builders to secure more cost-competitive Chinese slots. If Chinese shipyards face operational delays, Maersk's planned fleet renewal schedule could be severely disrupted.

Milestone Payments and Delivery Cash Flows

5.
Structured cash outflows: Shipbuilding contracts typically require milestone payments that stagger capital outlays across the multi-year construction process. DNB Carnegie notes that a standard Chinese yard payment schedule entails 20% on signing, 10% at steel cutting, 10% at keel laying, 10% at launching, and 50% on delivery. This highly back-loaded milestone structure means that the bulk of Maersk's cash outflows for the 2029-2030 deliveries will not occur until those specific years. Consequently, the group's near-term capex commitments remain heavily concentrated around actual vessel delivery windows.
6.
Staggered construction timelines: Construction timelines for large container vessels generally range from 12 to 48 months depending on shipyard backlog. Morgan Stanley research on shipbuilding processes shows a typical wait period of 12 to 18 months before steel cutting and keel laying begin. This long lead time means that Maersk's initial outlays for the 26-vessel order are limited to early downpayments. This structural delay provides Maersk with a temporary cash preservation window before massive physical construction payments fall due.
7.
Contractual delivery risks: Maersk's official prospectus issued in April 2025 warned investors that vessel deliveries could be delayed, left incomplete, or cancelled due to shipyard financial difficulties. Management highlighted that labor disturbances, shipyard bankruptcies, and material shortages present ongoing risks to its capital deployment. If a shipyard defaults, Maersk could incur significant financial losses and fail to realize its projected fleet efficiencies. These operational risks directly threaten the group's long-term earnings per share projections.

Comparative Peer Orderbooks and Capex

8.
Substantial peer commitments: Hapag-Lloyd has also committed to a massive fleet expansion, finalizing a 24- ship order valued at USD 4 billion in late 2024. Jefferies reports that Hapag-Lloyd secured financing commitments for USD 3 billion of this total, with an initial USD 400 million deposit paid in late 2024. The remaining payments are back-dated closer to actual deliveries between 2027 and 2029, illustrating a similar backloaded structure. This comparable framework highlights that major container carriers are universally utilizing debt-financed, back-loaded capex structures to preserve current liquidity.
9.
Rigid terminal obligations: Hapag-Lloyd reported purchase obligations of EUR 3.7 billion for its 32 newbuilds at the end of 2025, with EUR 98.5 million due in 2026. The carrier also ordered eight dual-fuel methanol vessels in 2025 for 2028-2029 delivery, with the first downpayment scheduled for 2026. This parallel investment program demonstrates that Maersk's peers are also locked into rigid long-term capital spending commitments. These overlapping orders increase the risk of industry-wide overcapacity and subsequent rate collapse.
10.
Unstoppable industry leader: Mediterranean Shipping Company continues to expand its lead as the world's largest carrier, confirming options for eight additional dual-fuel LNG-powered vessels in early 2026. Alphaliner data shows MSC's orderbook approaching 30% of its existing fleet capacity with more than 2.1 million TEU on order. This aggressive expansion by the market leader dwarfs Maersk's fleet renewal efforts. MSC's massive scale allows it to drive down unit costs, placing Maersk at a competitive disadvantage if freight rates collapse.
11.
Strategic capital allocation: Ocean Network Express is executing its "ONE 2030" medium-term plan, which has approved USD 12 billion in core container shipping investments. ONE operates a fleet of over 280 vessels and holds an active orderbook of 51 vessels as of September 2026. While global peers deploy surplus funds into massive fleet growth, Morgan Stanley notes that ONE has struggled to articulate a clear long-term growth strategy. This conservative approach may protect ONE's balance sheet during a downturn, but risks losing long-term market share.

Fleet Flexibility and Mitigation Options

12.
Propulsion modification options: Shipowners facing deteriorating market conditions may seek to modify vessel specifications to preserve capital. Jefferies notes that ordering a dual-fuel capable ship adds USD 10 million to USD 15 million to construction costs, whereas a dual-fuel ready ship adds only USD 0.5 million. Shipowners are likely to shift planned 2027 and onward deliveries to dual-fuel ready status to lower immediate capex. This technical optionality provides Maersk with a viable capex reduction lever if container shipping rates collapse.
13.
Charter market reliance: Maersk's fleet structure at the end of Q2 2026 consisted of 336 owned and 403 chartered vessels. This heavy reliance on chartered capacity provides the group with substantial operational flexibility. As charter agreements expire, Maersk can easily redeliver vessels to owners, thereby reducing operating capacity without incurring capital losses. This structural cushion allows Maersk to absorb newly delivered owned vessels while actively phasing out chartered tonnage.
14.
Contrary evidence: Near-term freight rate strength and Red Sea disruptions have temporarily boosted Maersk's operating cash flow, delaying any immediate pressure to defer capital spending. DNB Carnegie reports that these temporary bottlenecks have prompted consecutive guidance upgrades, with Maersk's share price rising approximately 70% year-to-date by October 2026. This short-term cash windfall provides Maersk with a larger liquidity buffer than bearish analysts anticipated. Consequently, the group has sufficient balance sheet flexibility to maintain its aggressive capex plan.
15.
Other side of the story: While Maersk's 26-vessel order appears to lock the company into a rigid long-term spending program, the group's highly flexible charter portfolio and typical shipyard payment structures provide substantial near-term cash flow protection. With initial downpayments representing a small fraction of the total contract value, Maersk will not face massive capital calls until delivery in 2029-2030. Furthermore, the ongoing redelivery of chartered vessels allows Maersk to absorb newly built tonnage without expanding net capacity. Therefore, Maersk remains well-positioned to sustain its fleet renewal without risking its balance sheet strength.

Section 3 – Terminals Capacity Programme and Concession Commitments

Section summary
  1. A.P. Møller - Mærsk A/S's terminals division represents a high-performing business segment with substantial capital expansion requirements that tension against consolidated financial constraints.
  2. The capital spending programme for key terminals relies heavily on joint-venture partnerships that keep a significant portion of capital commitments off-balance sheet.
  3. APM Terminals continues to deliver superior returns compared to global peers like DP World and Hutchison Port Holdings Trust despite operating below its optimal capacity utilization threshold.

Port Capacity Expansion Projects

1.
Rapidly expanding: APM Terminals completed phase one of the Rijeka Gateway container terminal in September 2025 through a joint venture. The EUR 380 million project in Croatia is owned 51% by Maersk's port division and 49% by local logistics firm Enna Logic. Croatia's Rijeka Gateway initiated commercial operations with an initial capacity of 650,000 TEU, which is scheduled to expand beyond one million TEU in subsequent development phases. This project represents the largest private logistics investment in Croatia and will strengthen regional rail-based trade connections.
2.
Substantial modernization: APM Terminals is entering advanced negotiations with Eurogate to invest EUR 1 billion in modernizing the North Sea Terminal Bremerhaven. The investment aims to expand the terminal's annual capacity from 3 million TEU to 4 million TEU and completely electrify its equipment. This major capital deployment will secure high-value jobs and position Bremerhaven as a key net-zero carbon hub. This large commitment will lock in long-term capital but will improve vessel turnaround times.
3.
Electrified expansion: APM Terminals officially delivered its new Suape container terminal in Pernambuco in June 2026. This terminal represents an investment of over USD 350 million and will be the first fully electrified container terminal in Latin America. The facility is designed to increase the regional container handling capacity by 55% by initially processing up to 400,000 TEU per year. This project represents one of the largest infrastructure expansions in northeast Brazil and will drive economic growth.
4.
Sustainable financing: Port of Tanjung Pelepas issued its inaugural RM 500 million Green Sukuk in October 2025 to fund its capacity expansion. This Malaysian port represents a key joint venture between local giant MMC Group and APM Terminals. The port has also become the first in Malaysia to commission electric prime movers for horizontal container movements. The sustainable expansion will strengthen its competitive positioning against regional hubs.

Balance Sheet and Accounting Structure

5.
Strategic accounting: Maersk manages several major terminal assets through joint ventures to optimize capital efficiency. The group holds a 50% stake in North Sea Terminal Bremerhaven, which is accounted for using the equity method under IFRS. This equity-accounting treatment allows Maersk to keep associated operational liabilities off-balance sheet while receiving a proportionate share of profit. This structure mitigates direct leverage impacts but limits complete financial control over the asset.
6.
Contrary evidence: Maersk holds a 51% majority stake in Rijeka Gateway, meaning its assets and liabilities must be fully consolidated on the balance sheet rather than kept off-balance sheet under the equity method. APM Terminals also holds substantial long-term operating lease commitments of DKK 23.7 billion, demonstrating that its terminal concessions carry heavy on-balance sheet obligations. These consolidated debts will directly increase Maersk's net leverage ratios during shipping market downturns.
7.
Expanding commitments: Maersk reported that its total capital commitments increased to USD 14.6 billion in Q2 2026. The group confirmed that these commitments are heavily tied to terminal concession agreements, dual-fuel vessel programs, and EU allowance contracts. These long-term terminal concessions represent a rigid spending profile that cannot be easily deferred when freight rates fall. This rigidity means terminal investments will continue to consume cash even if ocean profits decline.

Operational Efficiency and Peer Comparison

8.
Robust profitability: APM Terminals delivered a return on invested capital of 14.8% in Q2 2026. This return is slightly lower than the 15.4% reported in the same period of 2025 due to the ramp-up of new investments. In comparison, global peer DP World reported a return on capital employed of 9.9% for FY2025, which represents an improvement from 8.9% in FY2024. This comparison shows that Maersk's port division generates superior capital returns relative to its major global peer.
9.
Sub-optimal utilization: APM Terminals reported a capacity utilization level of 81% in Q2 2026, down from 86% in the prior year period. This utilization level sits below the optimal 90% threshold typically required to maximize terminal operating efficiency. Meanwhile, global competitor DP World operated at an average gross capacity utilization of 85.3% in FY2025 across its portfolio. The lower utilization at APM Terminals suggests there is substantial capacity available to absorb additional volume.
10.
Moderating growth: Hutchison Ports reported total revenue of HKD 48.9 billion in FY2025, which represents an 8% increase compared to FY2024. APM Terminals reported a similar revenue growth rate of 11% in Q2 2026, reaching USD 1.4 billion. Hutchison Ports operates 53 ports with 295 berths globally, maintaining a larger footprint than APM Terminals' 55 terminals. This shows that while APM Terminals is smaller, its topline growth remains highly competitive.
11.
Mixed cashflows: Hutchison Port Holdings Trust reported revenue of HKD 11.86 billion in FY2025, an increase of 3% from FY2024. The trust reported a consolidated net debt of HKD 15.55 billion at the end of FY2025, down from HKD 17.06 billion in FY2024. This performance highlights the steady cash generation of mature port assets compared to cyclical shipping lines. These stable cash flows support terminal investments but are insufficient to offset wider shipping volatility.

Capital Constraints and Strategic Balance

12.
Constrained leverage: Maersk prioritizes keeping its net leverage below 1.5x net debt/EBITDA to protect its investment grade rating of BBB+ with S&P and Baa1 with Moody's. DNB Carnegie highlights that increasing leverage above 1.5x would immediately put these critical credit ratings in danger. This leverage constraint forces a conservative balance between terminal expansion capital and fleet decarbonization spending. Management must therefore selectively allocate capital to high-ROIC projects like APM Terminals to avoid rating downgrades.
13.
Conglomerate discount: Morgan Stanley argues that APM Terminals' true value is not fully recognized when embedded within the cyclical Maersk conglomerate. Listed port peers trade at an average of 12x EV/EBITDA, whereas APM Terminals is valued at less than 6x EV/EBITDA within Maersk's multiple. Valuing the terminals division at pure-play peer multiples would imply a substantial USD 14 billion increase in Maersk's enterprise value. This structural discount limits Maersk's ability to unlock terminal value unless it considers a spin-off.
14.
Other side of the story: APM Terminals' massive expansion projects could strain Maersk's liquidity during a severe ocean market downturn despite their long-term strategic value. With capital commitments rising to USD 14.6 billion, Maersk faces significant cash outflows that could force a suspension of share buybacks. If port utilization remains low, these heavy investments will fail to generate sufficient cash flows to offset ocean division losses. Therefore, the terminal capacity program represents a substantial financial risk if global trade growth slows down.

Precedents of the 2016 Downturn

1.
Comprehensive review: This analytical section evaluated twelve relevant sources, covering four major global container shipping companies, to analyze historical capital adjustment precedents across two major cycle turns. The analysis compared Maersk's defensive capital moves against key sector peers like Hapag-Lloyd and COSCO Shipping, while excluding smaller regional feeder operators like Regional Container Lines due to their lack of comparable long-term capital data. This systematic evaluation provides a reliable foundation to judge the flexibility of Maersk's future capital returns.

The company proposed a fifty percent dividend cut to DKK 150 per share for the financial year 2016 compared to DKK 300 in 2015 to defend its investment-grade credit rating. Jefferies reported on February 8, 2017, that this sharp reduction was necessary because Maersk Line suffered a massive underlying loss of USD 384 million due to historic low freight rates. This adjustment protected the group's cash reserves during a period of intense structural overcapacity.

A quarterly report by East 72 Holdings on December 31, 2016, noted that Maersk redirected its focus toward structural solutions, including separating its energy businesses to unlock capital. This defensive action reflects a clear pattern of freezing discretionary cash returns when operating cash flows collapse.
4.
Tight discipline: Maersk significantly reduced its capital expenditure during the 2016 downturn to maintain its balance sheet integrity. The group reported actual capital spending of USD 5 billion for 2016, which was below its earlier guidance of six billion dollars due to delayed drilling rig deliveries and lower terminal spending. Jefferies highlighted on February 8, 2017, that improving capital discipline would eventually lead to a capex reduction of more than 50% over the cycle. This proactive spending cut directly supported the group's free cash flow generation during the cyclical trough.

Hapag-Lloyd management committed to no new vessel investments for the next couple of years to maximize free cash flow and deleverage its balance sheet. A Hapag-Lloyd report on March 24, 2017, showed that the carrier focused heavily on consolidating with United Arab Shipping Company to achieve scale instead of spending on organic fleet growth. This comparison proves that the entire shipping sector was forced to halt capital deployment during the historic 2016 collapse.

The Downturn of February 2024

6.
Contrary evidence: DNB Carnegie reported on August 8, 2024, that Maersk did not immediately suffer from rate collapses during every phase of cyclical turns because short-term capacity bottlenecks often delay rate normalizations. Maersk's actual performance during early downturn phases has sometimes been insulated by long-term contract coverage and delayed spot rate impacts, allowing temporary cash flow upgrades. This indicates that Maersk's capital return adjustments are not always immediate, as the group sometimes maintains substantial cash buffers before enforcing cuts.
7.
Prudent preservation: Maersk implemented another major round of cash preservation measures at the turn of the cycle in early 2024. The board of directors proposed a dividend of DKK 515 per share for 2023, representing a massive decline from the record DKK 4,300 per share paid out in 2022. Morgan Stanley noted on February 8, 2024, that this steep reduction was a direct response to a highly challenging market outlook and a priority to reinforce the balance sheet. This dramatic payout cut shows that Maersk's dividend policy remains highly variable and linked to cash preservation needs.
8.
Defensive freeze: Maersk responded to deteriorating ocean freight conditions in early 2024 by immediately freezing its multi-billion dollar capital return program. The board of directors decided in February 2024 to immediately suspend the share buyback program due to the heightened market uncertainty in the Ocean segment. Morgan Stanley reported on November 1, 2024, that management characterized this move as a suspension rather than a permanent stoppage, indicating that returns would resume once conditions settled. This strategic pause allowed the company to protect its Baa1/BBB+ credit rating during the initial phase of the rate correction.
9.
Strategic restraint: Maersk managed its investment pipeline with high caution during the 2024 cyclical turn to preserve cash. The group reported a gross capital expenditure of USD 3.6 billion for 2023, reflecting intensified cost measures and cash preservation priorities in light of the weak market outlook. An annual report from A.P. Møller - Mærsk on February 8, 2024, confirmed that the group would prioritize capital allocation to business investments and debt repayment over discretionary shareholder returns. This cautious approach ensured that the group entered the downturn with a net cash position of USD 4.7 billion.
10.
Moderate correction: Hapag-Lloyd adjusted its capital return profile with slightly less aggression than Maersk during the 2024 cyclical shift. Hapag-Lloyd's executive board proposed a dividend of EUR 8.20 per share for the 2024 financial year, which was only a slight decrease from the payout of EUR 9.25 per share in 2023. Deutsche Bank Research noted on March 20, 2025, that Hapag-Lloyd's free cash flow fell by USD 1 billion to USD 2.6 billion, yet the company maintained attractive shareholder distributions. This shows that Maersk tends to react more aggressively than some of its peers to preserve cash at cycle turns.

Structural Capital Friction

11.
Persistent obligations: Maersk faced significant strategic tension between green fleet renewal obligations and capital returns during the 2024 turn. Morgan Stanley research on December 4, 2024, argued that Maersk's cash conversion was impaired by capex resetting well above pre-pandemic levels to deliver its net zero ambition by 2040. While the group suspended buybacks in February 2024, it was simultaneously forced to announce plans to acquire 800,000 TEU of new capacity to stay competitive. This structural spending commitment limits the ultimate flexibility of the group's capital adjustments.
12.
Rapid reinstatement: Maersk demonstrated high operational agility by quickly reversing its defensive capital allocation measures when market conditions temporarily improved. The board of directors announced a new USD 2 billion share buyback program in February 2025 after strong container freight rates from Red Sea disruptions boosted 2024 earnings. A press release on February 6, 2025, confirmed that the group would also pay a dividend of DKK 1,120 per share, which represented a 40% payout. This rapid pivot shows that Maersk's capital return flexibility operates dynamically in both directions.
13.
Restrictive deployment: Maersk adapted its physical capacity actively alongside financial cuts during the 2016 downturn to mitigate losses. A presentation from September 2015 showed that Maersk Line accelerated plans to reduce network capacity by not exercising vessel options and adapt existing ship orders to falling rate forecasts. Maersk's management confirmed during the fourth quarter 2015 earnings call that they idled vessels, cancelled sailings, and handed back chartered capacity to align with weak market demand.
