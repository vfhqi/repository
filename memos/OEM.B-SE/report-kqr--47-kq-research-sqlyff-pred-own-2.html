Section 2 – Currency Effects, Exchange Clauses, and Inventory Lag

Section summary

Contractual Currency Clause Mechanics

1.
Symmetrical protection: OEM International mitigates import transaction risks by using symmetrical currency adjustment clauses that protect operating profitability from exchange rate volatility. The corporate policy covers approximately 68% of sales contracts through these contractual clauses and direct purchasing currency matches, up from 71% in the prior fiscal year. These clauses adjust between 80% and 100% of exchange fluctuations from the initial sales order to the final invoicing date, which secures gross margins during currency depreciations. This contractual structure helps protect earnings per share from sudden krona movements unless exchange fluctuations remain below the established 2.25% activation threshold.
2.
Import exposure: OEM International faces significant transactional exposure because a major portion of its supply purchases are denominated in foreign currencies. The company reported that 59% of its purchases in 2025 were executed in Euros and 21% were in United States dollars, compared to 57% and 22% respectively in 2024. This heavy reliance on foreign-currency purchases means that a weaker Swedish krona increases import costs, although integrated contractual clauses symmetrically adjust customer prices. These pricing adjustments help stabilize gross profits and safeguard projected earnings per share from margin compression.
3.
Time mismatch: OEM International experiences operational timing mismatches because purchase orders to suppliers are placed long before customer deliveries occur. Purchase orders typically run 7 to 60 days prior to product delivery, while the standard trade credit period is about 30 days. This operational lag means that inventory valuation changes do not align immediately with currency adjustments, creating temporary gross margin fluctuations. These timing differences can temporarily suppress or inflate near-term quarterly earnings per share during periods of extreme foreign exchange volatility.

Inventory Lag Dynamics

4.
Inventory benefits: OEM International benefited from selling older inventory purchased at more favorable historical exchange rates before the Swedish krona depreciated further in 2026. This classic lag effect occurs because the cost of goods sold reflects the historical purchase price while current selling prices are adjusted upward immediately via currency clauses. This lag temporarily inflated the reported gross margin to approximately 37.2% in January to September 2026, which is higher than the 36% recorded in the same period of 2024. This temporary benefit is expected to fade as higher-cost replacement inventory is sold through, potentially restricting 2027 earnings per share.
5.
Regional headwinds: OEM International faced severe transactional headwinds in its peripheral regions during the early quarters of 2026 despite the presence of hedging clauses. The Rest of Europe segment suffered a negative 3% currency impact on net sales during the first quarter of 2026, although organic growth remained positive at 21%. These negative currency effects weighed on initial quarterly sales growth, demonstrating that contract clauses only offset a portion of immediate currency shocks. This initial pressure confirms that currency changes can suppress first-quarter earnings per share before pricing clauses take full effect.
6.
Peer comparison: Dustin Group also demonstrated how inventory sell-through timing can positively impact gross margins during periods of supply cost volatility. The peer noted that some inventory sold in 2026 was purchased at lower historical prices, providing a positive lag effect. This pricing lag was expected to support gross margins into the first quarter of the following fiscal year, showing that inventory dynamics are common across Nordic distributors. Such industry-wide lag benefits suggest that distributor earnings per share can remain temporarily elevated before cost inflation catches up.
